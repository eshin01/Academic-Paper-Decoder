## 1. The Gist

**A team in Spain took the standard scoring system that intensive care units worldwide use to judge how sick a patient is, and asked a simple question nobody had properly asked: the numbers in that score were chosen by a committee in 1996 and never checked against what actually happens to patients — so what happens if you let real outcome data pick them instead, while keeping the score something a doctor can still add up on paper?**

**The answer is that it works, that the improvement is large and verified about as carefully as this reading list has seen, and that an existing method nobody mentions got the same result.**

Start with what is being fixed. The **SOFA score** (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment) is the standard way of summarising how badly a critically ill patient's organs are failing. It looks at six systems — lungs, brain, blood pressure, clotting, liver, kidneys — gives each **0 to 4 points**, and adds them up for a total out of 24. It is everywhere: it defines sepsis in the official international definition, it decides who enters clinical trials, it is used to compare hospitals.

**And here is the thing the authors are right to find strange.** Those six 0-to-4 scales, and the exact cut-off values inside them, **were set by expert consensus in 1996 and have never been re-estimated from outcome data.** Nobody went back and checked whether a platelet count of 50 really deserves the same points as a bilirubin of 6. The numbers are thirty years old and were never fitted to anything.

**The obvious modern answer is to throw it away and train a machine learning model.** Those models do score better. But they produce a number nobody can check — you cannot look at a prediction and see *why*. And in an ICU, a score you cannot audit at the bedside is a score clinicians will not trust.

**So the authors tried the thing in between, and this is the good idea in the paper.** Keep the structure — same organ systems, same "add up the points" arithmetic, whole numbers only, and points that must always go *up* as the patient gets *sicker*. But let the data choose where the cut-offs fall and how many points each band is worth. They built this as a constrained optimisation problem: find the integer weights that predict death best, **subject to** staying monotonic, staying whole numbers, and staying on a sensible scale.

**What they did.** The **eICU Collaborative Research Database** — real records from **208 US hospitals, 2014–2015**. **14,848 adult first ICU admissions** with complete day-one measurements. **ICU mortality 17.03% (2,529 of 14,848).** Performance measured by **10-fold nested cross-validation grouped by hospital**, which matters enormously and I will come back to.

**The results.**

- **Recalibrated score: AUROC 0.811** (95% CI 0.800–0.819)
- **The SOFA-derived reference score: 0.751** (0.740–0.761)
- **Paired improvement: 0.059** (0.053–0.067), and it won in **all 10 folds**
- **AUPRC 0.495 vs 0.393**, improvement 0.102
- **Calibration slope 1.011** (0.970–1.055)
- And in **1,000 repetitions** of the entire pipeline from scratch, the improvement was positive **1,000 times out of 1,000**

**Now the five things I found, two of which are the paper's own best work and three of which complicate its headline.**

**First — and this is the most elegant observation in the paper, made by the authors themselves — the improvement is far more stable than the performance.** Across the 10 hospital groups, the recalibrated score's absolute AUROC swung from 0.766 to 0.857, **a spread of 0.091.** But the *difference* between the two scores swung only from 0.036 to 0.067 — **a spread of 0.031, about three times narrower.** Why? Because when you compare both scores **on the same patients in the same fold**, whatever makes one hospital group harder than another affects both scores equally and cancels out. **This is the whole logic of paired comparison, demonstrated rather than asserted**, and it is why "0.059 better" is a much more trustworthy number than "0.811."

**Second, the methodology is the most careful in this reading list, and specifically it got right the thing yesterday's and Wednesday's papers got wrong.** All encounters from a given hospital stay in the same fold, so **no hospital ever appears in both training and testing.** Every model choice — how many bins, every hyperparameter — was made **inside** the loop on a separate validation split of *whole hospitals*. Every patient contributes **exactly one** out-of-fold prediction. All comparators ran on **identical folds**. Convergence criteria were **prespecified**, a single **random seed was fixed** — the exact opposite of Wednesday's "random seeds were not fixed." And the final published weights were refit on the whole cohort and **explicitly contributed no performance estimate.** That last sentence is the one most papers get wrong and this one states outright.

**Third — the 90% exclusion, which was the question I wrote down before reading, and the answer is worse than I expected and the paper reports it anyway.** Of **148,532** ICU stays with outcome data, **133,684 (90%) were excluded** because at least one required measurement was missing. That leaves 10%. **And the 10% who stayed are dramatically sicker than the 90% who were dropped:** mechanical ventilation **64.2% versus 20.2%**, norepinephrine **28.6% versus 8.3%**, and **ICU mortality 17.03% versus 4.54% — a 3.75-fold difference.**

**The mechanism is simple once you see it.** The two worst offenders were the oxygen ratio (missing in 76.5%) and bilirubin (64%). **An oxygen ratio requires an arterial blood gas; bilirubin requires a liver panel. Neither gets drawn on a patient who is doing fine.** So requiring them does not sample ICU patients — it samples ICU patients sick enough that someone stuck a needle in an artery. **To the authors' real credit, they quantify exactly this with standardized differences, say plainly that "the primary performance estimates should not be extrapolated directly to unselected ICU admissions," and note that the database cannot tell them why any individual measurement was skipped.**

**Fourth, they ran the obvious follow-up and it cut their own margin by more than a third.** Expanding to **61,469 encounters** by filling in a single missing value with a normal-range number dropped the improvement from **0.059 to 0.037 — a 37% reduction.** It is still positive in all 10 folds. But the headline number is the best-case one, and **they published the worse one.** They also **refused to impute 5,796 ventilated patients with missing oxygen data**, on the grounds that assuming normal oxygenation in someone on a ventilator "was not considered clinically defensible." That is a researcher declining free sample size for a clinical reason.

**Fifth, and this is my main criticism: the two most quotable claims are both softer than they look.**

**The comparator is not SOFA.** It is a "SOFA-derived reference score," because vasoactive drug doses could not be harmonised across hospitals (so they became yes/no flags) and **urine output was missing in 52% of the cohort and was dropped from the kidney score entirely.** So the thing being beaten is a **handicapped** SOFA, missing information real SOFA uses. The paper says this in its limitations and says comparisons "should be interpreted against this SOFA-derived reference score rather than the canonical original SOFA." **It is the right disclaimer, and it means the 0.059 is probably an overestimate of the gain over the real thing.**

**And an existing method already matched it.** Their Table 3 reports **AutoScore at AUROC 0.810 against their 0.811** — a difference of **0.001**, with essentially identical confidence intervals. **The paper states this plainly** and correctly reframes its own contribution as *structural*: AutoScore builds a brand-new score, whereas this framework keeps SOFA's organ-domain skeleton. **That is an honest and defensible claim. It is also not a performance claim, and the abstract's framing invites you to read it as one.** Meanwhile XGBoost — the black box — still won at **0.818.** So the conclusion's flourish that "the dichotomy between predictive performance and interpretability is false" is **contradicted by the paper's own table**: the gap is small (0.007), not absent.

**What this paper is, then.** The clearest demonstration in this reading list that **a score you can add up with a pen gets you about 90% of the way from a thirty-year-old consensus instrument to gradient boosting** — validated with the best methodology here, honest about its own calibration and its own shrinking margin, and limited by one database, a severely selected cohort, and a comparator that was handicapped before the race started.

## 2. Study Snapshot

**Study type:** retrospective multicenter cohort study developing and **internally** validating a recalibrated additive integer risk score. **No external validation**, stated as the priority for future work. **No prospective evaluation.** **Not a clinical trial and not an implementation study.**

**Data source.** The **eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD)**, deidentified EHR data from adult ICUs across the United States: **200,859 ICU stays, 139,367 unique patients, admitted 2014–2015, 335 units at 208 hospitals** participating in the Philips eICU telehealth programme. Freely available to qualified investigators via PhysioNet. The final analysis drew on **153 hospitals**.

**Population.** Adults aged 18 or older, **first ICU admission only**, with all SOFA-related measurements available in the first 24 hours. **Final analytic cohort 14,848**, with **2,529 deaths (17.03%)** and 12,319 survivors (82.97%).

**The exclusion cascade, fully reported and fully traceable.** Of **148,532** encounters with outcome information, **133,684 (90%) were excluded** for at least one unavailable determination, leaving **14,848** — which I verified reconciles exactly. **Missingness by variable, as a share of 148,532:** oxygen ratio (PaO₂/FiO₂) **76.5% (113,595)**, bilirubin **64% (95,114)**, creatinine **19.6% (29,164)**, platelet count **8.8% (13,017)**, Glasgow Coma Scale **1.54% (2,290)**, mean arterial pressure **0.3% (481)**. Of the excluded, **52,417 (39.2%) lacked exactly one** determination and **81,267 (60.8%) lacked two or more** — which sum correctly to 133,684. The oxygen ratio was the *sole* missing item in **27.4%** of exclusions and bilirubin in **11.6%**.

**Two handling decisions that matter.** **Urine output was dropped from the renal score**: it was available in only 51.5% of encounters overall and **missing in 52% (7,727) of the primary cohort**, so requiring it would have cut the cohort from 14,848 to **7,121** — a figure the paper gives and I verified. The renal domain therefore used **creatinine alone**. And **missing GCS was handled by a prespecified operational rule rather than exclusion**: ventilated encounters were assigned a GCS of 7, non-ventilated 15. **This applied to only 437 encounters (2.9%)**, of which 353 were ventilated — and the paper reports that **mortality in that imputed group was 25.4% (111/437) against 16.8% (2,418/14,411)** among those with recorded scores.

**How included and excluded patients differed — reported with standardized mean differences.** Mechanical ventilation **64.2% (9,531/14,848) versus 20.2% (26,978/133,684), SMD 0.995**; norepinephrine **28.6% versus 8.3%, SMD 0.540**; **ICU mortality 17% versus 4.5%, SMD 0.411**. **Age was comparable (absolute SMDs below 0.03) and sex distributions similar.** I recomputed all three SMDs independently and they reproduce exactly.

**The comparator.** Because vasoactive dose units could not be reliably harmonised across hospitals and urine output was incompletely available, **"the canonical original SOFA score could not be reconstructed exactly."** A **prespecified SOFA-derived reference score** was used instead. Respiratory, neurological, coagulation, hepatic and creatinine-based renal components followed **original SOFA thresholds**. The cardiovascular component used MAP plus **binary** vasoactive indicators: MAP above 70 scored 0; at or below 70, no vasoactives scored 1, dopamine/dobutamine/epinephrine without norepinephrine scored 2, norepinephrine alone 3, and norepinephrine plus another 4.

**The method, in two stages.** **Stage 1, supervised discretization:** for each variable a univariate decision tree (Gini criterion, ICU mortality as target) found candidate split points; bin-level mortality was estimated with Laplace smoothing on training data and scored by **log loss on a validation set**, with a **linear complexity penalty on the number of intervals**, and the number of intervals minimising the penalized validation loss was selected. **Stage 2, constrained integer weight optimisation:** an additive score summing interval weights, optimised under **monotonicity** (points never decrease as the patient worsens), **integrality** (whole numbers only, bounded for clinical plausibility) and **scale-anchoring** (a penalty keeping the score's spread near a target so weights do not collapse), plus a **sparse-bin penalty** shrinking weights of thinly-populated intervals. Ordering was imposed by reparametrising as non-negative increments; optimisation used **L-BFGS-B** after projecting initial weights onto the feasible monotonic set by **isotonic regression**; then **scale-matched rescaling and rounding**; then a **discrete coordinate-wise local search** in the integer domain to recover performance lost to naive rounding. **Mean arterial pressure was deliberately left unconstrained** because the source field stores the most deviant value in *either* direction, so its encoding is bidirectional by construction. **Class imbalance was handled by inverse prevalence weighting during weight optimisation**, while the **logistic calibration layer was fitted on unweighted data so predicted probabilities match the cohort's natural prevalence.**

**The validation design, which is the paper's strongest feature.** **10-fold nested cross-validation grouped by hospital.** Hospitals were assigned to 10 outer folds with approximate stratification by mortality and length of stay, **"ensuring that all encounters from a given hospital remained within the same fold and that no center contributed to both development and evaluation."** Within each outer development set, hospitals were further split into internal training and validation subsets, **with about 25% of development encounters assigned to validation by whole hospitals**. Discretization schemes and hyperparameters were fitted on internal training data, selected by validation log loss, refitted on the complete outer development set, and **evaluated once** on the held-out hospitals. **Each patient contributed exactly one out-of-fold prediction.** **All comparator models used the identical outer folds and equivalent hospital-grouped tuning.** Selection and convergence criteria were **prespecified**: configurations chosen by validation log loss, with runs below 0.70 validation AUROC or with more than three predictors receiving identical weights across all intervals treated as non-convergent. **A single random seed was prespecified and held fixed throughout.** And: **"This full-cohort model was used only to derive the final thresholds and integer weights; it was not used to estimate predictive performance."**

**Statistics.** AUROC and AUPRC with **95% bias-corrected and accelerated bootstrap CIs from 5,000 stratified resamples** on pooled out-of-fold predictions. Paired comparisons on identical patients within each fold, summarised as median and IQR of the paired difference across folds. Calibration by **calibration curves, calibration slope, calibration-in-the-large, observed-to-expected ratio and Brier score.** Baseline characteristics by Mann-Whitney and chi-square. Python 3.10.

**Results — discrimination.** Recalibrated **AUROC 0.811 (0.800–0.819)** versus reference **0.751 (0.740–0.761)**, paired improvement **0.059 (0.053–0.067)**. **AUPRC 0.495 (0.477–0.515)** versus **0.393 (0.377–0.411)**, paired **0.102 (0.087–0.116)**, against an event rate of 17.03%. **Across folds**, the recalibrated score ranged **0.766 to 0.857** (median 0.807, IQR 0.791–0.829) and the reference **0.713 to 0.791** (median 0.748, IQR 0.739–0.772); test folds held **494 to 3,315 encounters from 9 to 22 hospitals**. **The recalibrated score won in all 10 outer folds**, median paired improvement **0.057 AUROC** (IQR 0.054–0.062, range 0.036–0.067). And the paper's own key observation: **"Absolute AUROC varied by 0.091 across folds, whereas the paired difference varied by only 0.031."**

**By length of stay.** Stays of 2 days or less: **0.924 (0.914–0.933) versus 0.866 (0.852–0.877)**, AUPRC 0.798 versus 0.692. Seven days or less: **0.855 versus 0.793**. Thirty days or less: **0.812 versus 0.752**. Paired improvements **+0.059, +0.062, +0.059** — nearly identical across strata. Context for reading these: **44.40% of deaths occurred within 2 days and 81.53% within 7 days.**

**Results — calibration, reported with unusual candour.** Recalibrated **slope 1.011 (0.970–1.055)**, calibration-in-the-large **0.008 (−0.042 to 0.053)**, **O:E 1.005 (0.973–1.035)**; reference **1.040 (0.990–1.094)**, **−0.006 (−0.053 to 0.036)**, **0.996 (0.963–1.026)**. Mean predicted mortality 16.9% versus observed 17.03%. **Brier score lower: 0.112 (0.109–0.115) versus 0.123 (0.119–0.126).** **But:** "the recalibrated score had a lower Brier score but **higher integrated calibration index, E50, E90, and E**max** values than the SOFA-derived reference score. Taken together, these findings **do not indicate consistently superior calibration** relative to the reference score." And in short-stay patients **both models underestimated mortality** (O:E **1.400** recalibrated, **1.507** reference).

**Results — comparison with alternatives, all on identical hospital-grouped folds.** Reference **0.751 / 0.393**. **Penalized logistic regression 0.775 (0.764–0.784) / 0.438**, Δ 0.029. **AutoScore 0.810 (0.799–0.818) / 0.490**, Δ 0.056. **Recalibrated score 0.811 (0.800–0.819) / 0.495**, Δ 0.057. **XGBoost 0.818 (0.807–0.826) / 0.510**, Δ 0.065. The paper's own reading: **"AutoScore showed nearly identical discrimination to the proposed approach (AUROC 0.810), indicating that the principal distinction between the 2 interpretable methods lies in model structure rather than predictive performance."**

**Results — the final score, and the authors' repeated refusals to over-read it.** Intervals per variable ranged from **2 to 5**. **The score runs 0 to 71, not 0 to 24**, and the paper is emphatic that "the learned weights are not on the SOFA scale" and "represent relative contributions within the fitted prognostic score rather than revised 0 to 4 organ-domain points." Largest contributions went to cardiovascular and respiratory predictors, **partly because MAP, mechanical ventilation and the four vasoactive agents enter as separate predictors rather than combined into composite SOFA domains.** The neurological component collapsed to **a single cutpoint assigning 9 points at GCS at or below 4.5** and nothing above. Among vasoactives, **norepinephrine and epinephrine each contributed 6 points, dopamine 2, and dobutamine 0**; mechanical ventilation contributed 3. The largest single interval contribution went to an oxygen ratio at or below 49.9 mm Hg. Creatinine thresholds fell at 1.085, 1.425 and 1.665 mg/dL. **The paper states that "the zero dobutamine contribution should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical neutrality," that the cutpoints "should not be interpreted as revised physiological definitions," and that the result "should therefore be interpreted as a mortality risk model rather than as a replacement for the descriptive organ dysfunction grading provided by conventional SOFA."**

**Results — stability, the most thorough in this reading list.** The **entire pipeline** — discretization, weight optimisation and integer projection — was repeated **1,000 times** with different random seeds for patient-level partitioning and weight initialisation. **"All 1000 attempted replications satisfied the prespecified convergence criteria; none was rejected or repeated."** Recalibrated median **AUROC 0.814** (IQR 0.808–0.821; range 0.784–0.839); reference **0.754** (0.747–0.761; range 0.717–0.780). Median paired improvement **0.060 AUROC** (IQR 0.055–0.066; **range 0.027–0.088**) and **0.116 AUPRC**, **both positive in 1,000 of 1,000**. The paper explicitly notes these replications "characterize the stability of the optimization procedure under patient-level resampling and are **distinct from** the hospital-grouped cross-validation used for the primary performance assessment." **Structural stability:** norepinephrine median 6 points (IQR 6–6), epinephrine 5 (5–6), dopamine 2 (2–2), dobutamine 0 (0–1), ventilation 2 (2–2). **Cutpoint stability varied** — the first GCS boundary (4.5, IQR 4.5–4.5), first MAP boundary (40.5, IQR 40.5–40.5) and first and fourth creatinine boundaries were highly consistent, while oxygen-ratio, bilirubin and platelet boundaries "showed greater variability or were selected in only a subset of replications." **Mean interquartile width of assigned points:** platelets 0.27, MAP 0.55, oxygen ratio 0.65, GCS 0.71, creatinine 0.95, **bilirubin 2.29**. **At patient level**, median within-patient score IQR **2 points on a 0–62 range**, **63.1% (9,367/14,848)** of patients varying by 2 points or less, and **median pairwise Spearman correlation between replications 0.970 (IQR 0.964–0.975)**.

**Results — the prespecified missing-data sensitivity analysis.** The whole hospital-grouped procedure was repeated on an expanded cohort of **61,469 encounters from 186 hospitals, including 46,621 with one imputed determination**, with folds redefined. A single missing bilirubin, creatinine, platelet or MAP value was **randomly drawn from a range entirely inside the corresponding zero-point SOFA category** (bilirubin 0.2–1.1 mg/dL; creatinine 0.4–1.1; platelets 150–300 ×10⁹/L; MAP 70–95 mm Hg), sampled uniformly with a fixed seed; non-ventilated encounters missing an oxygen ratio were assigned 400–500. **Ventilated encounters missing an oxygen ratio (n=5,796) were not imputed** "because assuming normal oxygenation in the presence of mechanical ventilation was not considered clinically defensible." Encounters missing two or more inputs were excluded. **The imputation used no outcome information.** Result: the recalibrated score won in **all 10 outer folds**, median AUROC **0.853 (IQR 0.847–0.857)** versus **0.818 (0.811–0.821)**, **median paired difference 0.037 (IQR 0.034–0.043; range 0.028–0.046)**; median AUPRC **0.395 versus 0.300**, paired **0.096**. The paper notes the expanded cohort differed in case mix and **event prevalence (8% versus 17%)** and interprets it as "a robustness assessment of the paired comparison rather than as a direct comparison of absolute performance."

**Also reported.** **Decision curve analysis** on the out-of-fold predictions, stratified by length of stay and overall (in an unreachable figure). An **open-source browser-based calculator** performing all computation locally.

**Limitations the authors state, six numbered plus an extended discussion.** Retrospective and **a single database**, with external validation in independent international cohorts required. **Static day-1 assessment only**, capturing no temporal trajectory. **Mortality the sole outcome**, with length of stay, functional outcomes and treatment decisions unexplored. **SOFA 2.0 could not be evaluated** as its required variables were not consistently captured. **Learned thresholds may reflect dataset-specific practice patterns.** **The comparator was a SOFA-derived operationalisation rather than an exact reconstruction of SOFA**, with the cardiovascular component using binary vasoactive indicators and the renal component creatinine alone. **And no prospective assessment of clinical utility.** Separately, they discuss at length that **complete-case selection "may therefore have enriched the primary analytic cohort for more severely ill and intensively monitored patients"** and that **"the primary performance estimates should not be extrapolated directly to unselected ICU admissions."** They also explain why they chose **not** to add missingness indicators as predictors: doing so "would introduce a distinct measurement-process signal that may reflect local testing and documentation practices in addition to patient condition, potentially limiting transportability across hospitals."

**Not reported or not retrievable:** **funding, conflicts of interest, data availability and code availability statements**; **the calculator URL and repository link** (stripped in extraction); **any reporting guideline**; **any external validation**; all figures and Supplementary Tables S1–S8 — which costs the per-threshold classification metrics (S4), the subgroup metrics (S5), the additional calibration-error values (S6), and the full cutpoint and weight distributions across the 1,000 replications (S7–S8).

## 3. How Strong Is This Evidence? (Grade 4 out of 5)

**This is the best-validated primary study in this reading list. Its methodology is what the other 82 entries should have done, its honesty about its own weaknesses is near-exemplary, and it earns a 4 rather than a 5 because everything rests on one database, a cohort that excluded 90% of eligible patients and kept the sickest tenth, and a comparator that was handicapped before the comparison began.**

**What is done better here than anywhere else in this list.**

**The cross-validation is correct in every respect that matters, and I want to enumerate them because the combination is rare.** Folds are **grouped by hospital**, so no centre appears in both development and evaluation — this is exactly technique 319, the check Wednesday's bitewing paper did not run and which that entry flagged as its decisive missing experiment. The grouping extends **into the inner loop**: the internal validation split is also by **whole hospitals**. Model selection happens **inside** the loop, so the reported numbers are not selection scores — the defect that capped entries 80 and 81. Each patient yields **exactly one** out-of-fold prediction, so no patient is counted twice. **All comparators ran on the identical folds with equivalent tuning**, which is what makes Table 3 a fair fight. Convergence criteria were **prespecified**. **A single random seed was fixed** — the direct opposite of Wednesday's disclosure. And the final published weights were refit on the full cohort with the explicit statement that this **"was not used to estimate predictive performance."** **I have not seen that last sentence in any other entry here.**

**The calibration slope is the tightest and best-centred in this entire reading list: 1.011, with a 95% interval of 0.970 to 1.055 — a width of 0.085.** Set that against entry 80's 0.856 (0.469–1.639), width 1.170 — **about 14 times wider**, and spanning severe overfitting to severe underfitting. The difference is sample size: 14,848 out-of-fold predictions against 57 test patients. **This is what an actually estimated calibration slope looks like**, and it is the answer to the "unestimated, not acceptable" problem technique 307 was written for.

**They report that their own model's calibration is not better.** In their own results, in plain words: lower Brier score but **higher** integrated calibration index, E50, E90 and E-max, and therefore **"these findings do not indicate consistently superior calibration relative to the reference score."** Most papers would have printed the favourable Brier and stopped. They also report that **both** models underestimate mortality in short-stay patients (O:E 1.400 and 1.507).

**The stability analysis is the most thorough in this list.** A thousand repetitions of the **entire** pipeline — not just the model fit but the discretization and integer projection too — reporting stability at three levels: **which cutpoints were chosen**, **how much the assigned points moved**, and **how much individual patients' total scores moved**. And the honest findings within it: oxygen-ratio, bilirubin and platelet boundaries "showed greater variability or were selected in only a subset of replications," and bilirubin's point assignment varied by **2.29 points** against platelets' 0.27. **Plus the sentence that forecloses selective reporting: "All 1000 attempted replications satisfied the prespecified convergence criteria; none was rejected or repeated."** And they correctly note that patient-level resampling measures **optimisation stability, not generalisation** — a distinction most papers blur.

**The missing-data sensitivity analysis was prespecified, run, and cut their own headline by 37%** — from 0.059 to 0.037 — and published. **And they declined to impute 5,796 ventilated patients** because assuming normal oxygenation on a ventilator "was not considered clinically defensible." That is turning down free sample size on clinical grounds.

**They characterised who the 90% exclusion removed, with standardized mean differences**, and stated the consequence correctly: estimates **"should not be extrapolated directly to unselected ICU admissions."** I recomputed all three SMDs and they reproduce exactly.

**The calibration layer was fitted on unweighted data so that predicted probabilities match the natural prevalence** — while inverse prevalence weighting was used only in the discrimination objective. **This is precisely the fix entry 77 lacked**, where SMOTE guaranteed miscalibration and no calibration was reported at all.

**And the interpretive discipline around the learned weights is the best in this list.** They state repeatedly that the cutpoints are **not** revised physiological definitions, that the 0–71 scale is **not** comparable to SOFA's 0–24, that the score is a **mortality risk model and not a replacement for organ-dysfunction grading**, and — the detail that most impressed me — that **"the zero dobutamine contribution should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical neutrality."** A lesser paper would have written a paragraph about dobutamine being safe.

**Also: a competitor that beats them is in the table** (XGBoost 0.818), **a competitor that ties them is in the table** (AutoScore 0.810), decision curve analysis was run, the method is reported to full replicability, and ethics are properly stated and justified.

**So why not a 5.**

**One database, no external validation.** 208 hospitals is genuinely multicenter and hospital-grouped folds test transfer *across* those hospitals — but all of them sit inside one US telehealth network with one data pipeline and one documentation culture. The authors name **MIMIC-IV, AmsterdamUMCdb and HiRID** as future work, which is the right list. **Until then this is internal validation with a good geographic spread, not external validation.**

**The 90% exclusion severely bounds who this applies to.** Mortality **17.03% in the included versus 4.54% in the excluded** is not a nuance; it is a different population. The score was developed and validated on **the sickest tenth of an ICU population**, selected by the presence of invasive tests. The paper says so, which is why this costs a point rather than two.

**The comparator was handicapped, and this is my main substantive criticism.** Real SOFA scores kidneys on creatinine **or urine output, whichever is worse**, and grades cardiovascular support by **drug dose bands**. This reference score has **no urine output at all** and only **yes/no** drug flags. Both of those discard real prognostic information. So the reference is weaker than canonical SOFA, and **part of the 0.059 margin is plausibly the optimiser recovering information that real SOFA already had.** The paper's disclaimer is exactly right and does not make the margin bigger.

**An existing interpretable method matched it to within 0.001.** AutoScore 0.810 against 0.811, with overlapping intervals. **The paper is honest about this and reframes its contribution as structural — keeping SOFA's organ-domain skeleton rather than building a new score — which is a real and defensible contribution.** But it means that on the paper's own evidence, **the performance gain is attributable to data-driven recalibration in general, not to this framework in particular.**

**And the conclusion overreaches by exactly one sentence.** "The dichotomy between predictive performance and interpretability is false" is contradicted by Table 3: XGBoost wins by **0.007**. The supportable claim is that **the gap is small enough not to matter clinically**, which is a strong and interesting claim. "False" is not what the table says. The abstract's "should transfer to other legacy instruments" is likewise untested speculation — one instrument, one database.

**Four smaller things.** **Day-1 only**, so nothing about trajectory, which the authors flag. **Mortality only.** **SOFA 2.0 could not be evaluated**, so a newer consensus instrument built on over a million admissions sits uncompared. And a point the paper does not make: **its own premise is that 1996 thresholds are stale, and it re-estimates them from 2014–2015 data, now 11 years old** — so the shipped calculator carries weights from a decade-old snapshot. The *method* is the durable contribution; the *weights* are cohort-specific, which the paper says, but the calculator ships them anyway.

**Why not 3.** Entries 80 and 81 were held at 3 because their headline numbers were selection scores with no held-out estimate, and entry 82 because its instrument's reliability was unreported. **None of those defects is present here.** Selection happened inside the loop, the reported numbers are out-of-fold, the measurement is death (no rater reliability to worry about), and the uncertainty is quantified at the metric, fold, replication, cutpoint and patient levels.

**What would make this a 5, concretely.** **External validation on MIMIC-IV**, which the authors already name and which is freely available. **A comparison against canonical SOFA on a dataset where urine output and vasoactive doses exist**, which would tell us how much of the 0.059 survives an unhandicapped comparator. **A head-to-head against SOFA 2.0.** And **dropping the two overclaiming sentences**, which costs nothing.

## 4. The Editor's Concerns

**1. The paired difference is more stable than the absolute performance, and the paper found this itself.** Across 10 hospital groups the recalibrated score's AUROC ran **0.766 to 0.857 — a spread of 0.091** — while the paired difference ran **0.036 to 0.067, a spread of 0.031**, about **three times narrower**. I verified both. The reason is that comparing two scores **on the same patients in the same fold** cancels whatever makes one hospital group harder than another. **This is why "0.059 better" is the trustworthy number and "0.811" is the fragile one** — and it is a general lesson most papers never state.

**2. The 90% exclusion kept the sickest tenth, and the mechanism is mundane and important.** **133,684 of 148,532 (90%) excluded**; the survivors had **64.2% versus 20.2%** mechanical ventilation (SMD 0.995), **28.6% versus 8.3%** norepinephrine, and **17.03% versus 4.54%** mortality — a **3.75-fold** difference. Why: the two biggest offenders were the oxygen ratio (missing 76.5%) and bilirubin (64%), and **an arterial blood gas and a liver panel are not drawn on a patient who is doing well.** So the inclusion criterion is effectively "someone thought this patient sick enough to need invasive tests." **Full credit to the authors**: they quantify it with SMDs, discuss it at length, and state that estimates "should not be extrapolated directly to unselected ICU admissions." **The consequence stands regardless: a score for triaging ICU patients was validated on a cohort from which 9 in 10 ICU patients were removed, and the ones removed were four times less likely to die.**

**3. The sensitivity analysis cut the margin by more than a third, and that is the number to carry forward.** Expanding to **61,469** encounters dropped the paired AUROC gain from **0.059 to 0.037 — a 37% relative reduction** — while remaining positive in all 10 folds. **The direction is robust; the magnitude is not.** The honest reading is that **0.037 is the better estimate for a less-selected population and 0.059 is the best case.** The paper publishes both, flags the prevalence shift (8% versus 17%), and correctly limits the comparison to the paired difference.

**4. The comparator is not SOFA, and a handicapped comparator inflates the apparent gain.** Real SOFA grades kidneys on **creatinine or urine output, whichever is worse**, and cardiovascular support by **dose bands**. Here urine output is **absent entirely** (missing in 52% of the cohort; requiring it would have cut 14,848 to 7,121) and vasoactives are **binary flags** because doses could not be harmonised. **Both discard prognostic information, so the reference score is weaker than the real instrument** — and part of the 0.059 is plausibly the optimiser **recovering information canonical SOFA already uses.** The paper states the limitation and says comparisons "should be interpreted against this SOFA-derived reference score rather than the canonical original SOFA." **That disclaimer is correct and it is also the reason the headline cannot be read as "beats SOFA by 6 points."**

**5. An existing interpretable method tied it, and the real contribution is structural rather than predictive.** Table 3: **AutoScore 0.810 (0.799–0.818) against the proposed score's 0.811 (0.800–0.819)** — a gap of **0.001** with near-identical intervals. **The paper says this plainly** and reframes correctly: AutoScore builds a new score, this framework **keeps SOFA's organ-domain skeleton** and updates it, preserving clinicians' decades of familiarity. **That is a genuine contribution and I credit it.** But the abstract's framing — improving discrimination "over a prespecified SOFA-derived reference score" — invites a performance reading, and on the paper's own table **the performance belongs to data-driven recalibration in general, not to this method in particular.**

**6. The conclusion overclaims by one sentence, contradicted by the paper's own table.** "The dichotomy between predictive performance and interpretability is false." **XGBoost scored 0.818 against 0.811.** The gap is **0.007** with overlapping intervals — small, arguably clinically irrelevant, **not absent.** The supportable version is far more useful: **an integer score you can add up by hand captures about 90% of the distance from the reference score to gradient boosting** (I compute 89.6% of the 0.067 headroom, against penalized logistic regression's 35.8%). **That is a better finding than the one the conclusion claims**, and it is defensible. Similarly, the abstract's "should transfer to other legacy instruments" is **untested** — one instrument, one database.

**7. The scale changed from 0–24 to 0–71, which preserves additivity but not SOFA's domain structure — and the paper admits the specific reason.** Because **MAP, mechanical ventilation and the four vasoactive agents enter as separate predictors rather than being combined into composite SOFA domains**, there is no longer a "cardiovascular 0–4" sub-score. So a clinician can still **add up** the points, but can no longer read off *which organ system* contributed what in SOFA's terms. **The paper says this explicitly**, and is scrupulous that the weights "are not on the SOFA scale." **The abstract's "without abandoning its additive, bedside-computable form" is the more flattering phrasing of a real but partial preservation.**

**8. Short-stay AUROC of 0.924 is an easier task, not better performance.** **44.40% of deaths occurred within 2 days.** In the 2-day stratum the model is predicting death that happens tomorrow from physiology measured today — nearly a measurement of current state rather than a forecast. **The honest headline figure is the 30-day stratum's 0.812**, which is essentially the whole cohort. **To the paper's credit the paired improvement is almost identical across strata (+0.059, +0.062, +0.059)**, so the *gain* is not a stratification artefact even though the *absolute* numbers are.

**9. The GCS rule imputes neurology from ventilation — a real concern, which the paper's own numbers bound to 2.9%.** Missing GCS was filled by rule: **ventilated patients got 7, non-ventilated 15.** That is not imputation from the patient's neurological state; it is imputation **from the respiratory variable**, which already sits in the score — so for those patients the neurological points partly restate ventilation. **I raised this before checking the magnitude, and the paper defuses it: the rule applied to only 437 encounters (2.9%), of which 353 were ventilated.** The authors also report that **mortality in the imputed group was 25.4% against 16.8%** elsewhere, so they looked. **At 2.9% this cannot move the headline, and I am recording it as a concern that the paper's own transparency answers.**

**10. Some cutpoints are reported with more precision than they possess.** Creatinine thresholds at **1.085, 1.425 and 1.665 mg/dL** carry four significant figures; **GCS at "4.5"** on a scale whose values are integers from 3 to 15 simply means "4 or less"; the oxygen ratio at **49.9**. **The 1,000-replication analysis shows exactly why this matters** — bilirubin and platelet boundaries "showed greater variability or were selected in only a subset of replications," and bilirubin's point assignment varied by a mean interquartile width of **2.29 points**. **A threshold estimated from data has a sampling distribution, and printing it to three decimals implies a stability the paper's own stability analysis contradicts for several variables.** The authors do report the variability, which is the saving grace.

**11. No external validation, and one database however many hospitals.** 208 hospitals with hospital-grouped folds is a real test of transfer across centres, **but all of them share one telehealth network, one data pipeline and one documentation culture.** Learned thresholds "may reflect dataset-specific practice patterns," as limitation five says. The authors name **MIMIC-IV, AmsterdamUMCdb and HiRID** — the right targets, and MIMIC-IV is free.

**12. A premise that applies to the paper itself.** The argument is that SOFA's thresholds were fixed by consensus **in 1996 and never re-estimated**, and that populations evolve so scores should be periodically refitted. **The data here are from 2014–2015 and the paper appeared in 2026 — an 11-year-old snapshot**, against the 19 years of staleness it criticises. The *framework* is the durable contribution and the paper says the weights are cohort-specific. **But the open-source calculator ships those 2014–2015 weights, and a tool whose selling point is being up to date deserves a visible note about when its numbers were estimated.**

**13. No reporting guideline named — and this is the most interesting instance of this list's running observation.** **TRIPOD+AI** exists for exactly this study type and is not mentioned. **And yet this paper does nearly everything TRIPOD+AI asks**, and more thoroughly than entry 69, which *did* name TRIPOD+AI and deposit a completed checklist: participants, predictors and outcome fully defined; missing data described, quantified **and** sensitivity-tested; discrimination **and** calibration reported with slope, calibration-in-the-large and observed-to-expected ratio; the full model specification published so anyone can compute it; comparison against a simple baseline; and uncertainty at five separate levels. **So the standing rule reaches its sharpest form: naming a checklist does not predict quality, and the best-reported primary study in this reading list names none.** The caveat that cuts the other way is small but real — **funding, conflicts, data availability and code availability are all absent from the retrieved body**, and those are items a checklist would have pinned down.

## 5. Statistics Spotlight

Three concepts, numbered from 331 in the running list in `queue.md`. The first is what makes this paper's numbers believable; the second is what bounds who they apply to; the third is a pair of distinctions the paper handles unusually well.

**Grouped nested cross-validation: splitting by the thing you want to generalise across, and keeping every choice inside the loop.**

To know whether a model works, you must test it on data it has never seen. **Cross-validation** does this by splitting the data into parts — here 10 — training on nine and testing on the tenth, then rotating. **But two details decide whether the resulting number means anything, and this paper gets both right.**

**Detail one: what you split by.** The naive approach splits individual patients at random. **That silently answers the wrong question.** If patients from the same hospital land on both sides of the split, the model can learn that hospital's habits — how it documents, which drugs it stocks, when it draws labs — and score well on patients from a hospital it already knows. **That tells you nothing about a new hospital.** This paper instead **grouped by hospital**: "all encounters from a given hospital remained within the same fold and no center contributed to both development and evaluation." So each fold's score answers the question you actually care about: **how does this do at a hospital it has never seen?** This is technique 319, and it is exactly the check Wednesday's bitewing paper could not run across its three mannequins.

**Detail two: where you make your choices.** Every model involves decisions — how many bins per variable, how strong the penalties. **If you make those choices by looking at your test data, your reported number is not a performance estimate, it is the best of many tries** — the defect that capped entries 80 and 81. **"Nested" means the choices happen in an inner loop.** Here, within each outer fold's development data, hospitals were split **again** into internal training and validation, configurations were selected by validation log loss, refitted on the full development set, and **evaluated once** on the held-out hospitals. **The test hospitals never influenced a single decision.**

**And then the sentence that almost no paper writes.** After all that, the published weights were refit on the full cohort — necessary, since you want the final tool to use all the data — and the paper states: **"This full-cohort model was used only to derive the final thresholds and integer weights; it was not used to estimate predictive performance."** **That one sentence closes the most common loophole in prediction research.**

**Here is the payoff, and it is the paper's own cleverest observation.** Across the 10 hospital groups the recalibrated score ranged from **0.766 to 0.857 — a spread of 0.091.** But the *difference* between the two scores ranged only **0.036 to 0.067 — a spread of 0.031**, roughly **three times narrower**, and it was positive in **all 10 folds**. **Because both scores were measured on the same patients in the same fold, whatever made one hospital group harder affected both equally and cancelled.** That is the logic of **paired comparison**: compare within, not across.

**The everyday version.** You want to know whether a new running shoe is faster. **Unpaired:** some runners wear the new shoe, others the old, and you compare times — but runners differ far more than shoes do, so the shoe effect drowns in it. **Paired:** every runner runs both, and you look at each person's *difference*. Fitness, weather and course cancel out. **You will see the shoe effect clearly even though the raw times are all over the place** — which is exactly the 0.091 versus 0.031 pattern here.

**Watch out for:** cross-validation reported without saying **what the unit of splitting was.** Random patient-level splits in multi-site data, repeated-measures data, or data with few distinct subjects **overstate performance.** Ask three questions. **Were folds grouped by site, patient or device — whatever you want to generalise across?** **Was every tuning decision made inside the loop?** **And did any patient contribute more than one prediction?** Then one more, for papers with a comparator: **were both arms evaluated on identical folds, and is the headline the absolute score or the paired difference?** **Prefer the paired difference. It is usually the more stable and more honest number.**

**Complete-case analysis: a 90% exclusion is a change of population, and standardized mean differences are how you see it.**

This study required every day-one measurement to be present. That excluded **133,684 of 148,532 encounters — 90%**, leaving **14,848**.

**The instinct is that losing 90% of your data costs you precision. The real cost is different and worse: it changes who you are studying.**

**Why, concretely.** Missingness was concentrated in two variables: the **oxygen ratio** (missing in 76.5%) and **bilirubin** (64%). An oxygen ratio needs an **arterial blood gas** — a needle into an artery. Bilirubin needs a **liver panel**. **Neither is ordered for a patient who is stable and improving.** So "has all measurements present" is not a neutral filter. It is a proxy for **"was sick enough that clinicians ordered invasive tests."**

**The paper measures exactly this, and the numbers are stark.** Among included versus excluded encounters: mechanical ventilation **64.2% versus 20.2%**, norepinephrine **28.6% versus 8.3%**, and **ICU mortality 17.03% versus 4.54% — included patients were 3.75 times more likely to die.** Age and sex, by contrast, were nearly identical.

**The tool for seeing this is the standardized mean difference (SMD).** It expresses a gap between two groups in units of their own spread, so you can compare across variables measured on different scales. For two proportions it is the difference divided by the square root of the average of each group's variance. **A rule of thumb: above 0.1 is an imbalance worth noting; above 0.8 is large.** Here: ventilation **0.995**, norepinephrine **0.540**, mortality **0.411**, age **below 0.03**. **I recomputed all three of the big ones and they reproduce exactly.** A small correction worth recording: my own first attempt used a pooled-variance formula and got 0.535 for mortality instead of 0.411 — **the paper was right and my formula was wrong**, which is a reminder that "SMD" names a family of closely-related statistics and you should use the standard one.

**Why SMD rather than a p-value.** With 148,532 encounters, **every** difference will be "statistically significant," including ones too small to matter. **The SMD tells you how big the difference is, which is the question.** That is technique 186 applied to a selection problem.

**What the authors do with it, and it is right.** They state that complete-case selection "may therefore have enriched the primary analytic cohort for more severely ill and intensively monitored patients" and that **"the primary performance estimates should not be extrapolated directly to unselected ICU admissions."** They also note the database **cannot tell them why** any individual measurement was skipped. **And they ran the follow-up**: expanding to 61,469 encounters by filling single missing values with normal-range numbers, which **cut the improvement from 0.059 to 0.037, a 37% reduction**, published rather than buried.

**The everyday version.** You want to know how long people wait in a hospital emergency department, so you use records where a full set of vital signs was recorded. **But full vitals get recorded on the patients staff were worried about.** Your "average wait" is the average wait **of worrying patients** — and it will look nothing like the sprained-ankle crowd who were triaged, seated and forgotten. **You have not got a noisy estimate of the general wait; you have a clean estimate of a different question.**

**Watch out for:** "complete-case analysis," "patients with available data," "after excluding records with missing values" — especially when the excluded fraction is large. **Three questions. What fraction was excluded? How do included and excluded patients differ, in SMDs rather than p-values? And is the missingness plausibly caused by how sick the patient was?** That last one is decisive: **missing-because-nobody-ordered-the-test is missingness that depends on the outcome**, and it cannot be fixed by any imputation method, only bounded by sensitivity analysis — which is what this paper did.

**Discrimination and calibration are different claims — and a precision-recall curve moves with prevalence while a ROC curve does not.**

This paper makes two distinct claims, and only one of them succeeds. **Keeping them apart is one of the most useful habits a reader can have.**

**Discrimination** asks: can the score put the patients in the right *order*? **AUROC** is the chance that a randomly chosen patient who died scored higher than one who survived. 0.5 is a coin flip. **Here: 0.811 against 0.751, and the confidence intervals — 0.800–0.819 against 0.740–0.761 — do not overlap at all**, with a gap of 0.039. Non-overlapping intervals are a **stricter** test than the usual p-below-0.05 threshold, so this improvement is unambiguous (technique 283).

**Calibration** asks a different question: when the score says "30% chance of death," do about 30% actually die? **A score can rank perfectly and still have badly wrong numbers** — multiply every probability by three and the ordering is untouched while the numbers become nonsense.

**The headline calibration measure is the calibration slope**, which compares predicted risk against observed. **1.0 is perfect.** Here it is **1.011 with a 95% interval of 0.970 to 1.055 — a width of 0.085**, which is the tightest in this reading list. Compare entry 80's **0.856 (0.469 to 1.639)**, a width of **1.170 — about 14 times wider** and spanning severe overfitting to severe underfitting. **The difference is sample size: 14,848 out-of-fold predictions versus 57 test patients.** Technique 307's point was that a wide interval means **unmeasured**, not acceptable; **this paper shows what measured actually looks like.**

**And here the paper does something rare: it reports that its own calibration is not better.** The **Brier score** — which bundles discrimination and calibration into one number — favoured the new score (**0.112 versus 0.123**). But **the integrated calibration index, E50, E90 and E-max were all higher**, and the paper concludes: **"these findings do not indicate consistently superior calibration relative to the reference score."** **The right reading: the new score ranks patients better and does not describe their risks better.** For triage and trial enrolment, ranking is what you need. For telling a family a number, calibration is.

**Now the part the paper reports but does not explain — and it is the most useful thing in this section.** In the expanded sensitivity cohort, **AUROC went UP (0.811 to 0.853) while AUPRC went DOWN (0.495 to 0.395).** Same method, same approach — opposite directions. **How?**

**Because AUPRC depends on how common the outcome is and AUROC does not.** Prevalence fell from **17% to 8%**. The **area under the precision-recall curve** measures how well you can flag the positives without too many false alarms, and **a useless model scores roughly the prevalence** — so the floor fell from 0.17 to 0.08. Measured against that floor: **0.495 / 0.17 = 2.91 times the floor** in the primary cohort, and **0.395 / 0.08 = 4.94 times the floor** in the expanded one. **Relative to what was achievable, the AUPRC got better, not worse.** The apparent drop is prevalence halving.

**The everyday version.** You have a metal detector. On a beach where 1 in 5 buried objects is gold, most of your beeps will be gold. Move to a beach where 1 in 12 is gold and **the same detector** will produce far more false alarms per find. **The detector did not get worse; the haystack got bigger.** A ROC curve measures the detector. A precision-recall curve measures the detector **on that beach.**

**Watch out for:** comparing AUPRC across cohorts, time periods or sites with **different event rates** — a drop may be entirely prevalence. **Always read an AUPRC next to the prevalence, and compute the ratio.** Conversely, a paper reporting **only** AUROC on a rare outcome is hiding how unusable the alerts would be; AUROC can look excellent while almost every alarm is false (technique 275). **The pair of habits: read AUPRC against its prevalence floor, and never accept a calibration claim that rests on a Brier score alone**, since Brier mixes the two things you were trying to tell apart — which is exactly why this paper's own candour about its calibration measures is worth copying.

## 6. Jargon Translator

**SOFA (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment)** — the standard ICU score for how badly organs are failing. Six systems (lungs, brain, cardiovascular, clotting, liver, kidneys), **0 to 4 points each, total out of 24.** Used in the official sepsis definition, in trial enrolment and in hospital comparisons. **Its thresholds were set by expert consensus in 1996 and never re-estimated from outcome data** — the problem this paper addresses.

**SOFA-derived reference score** — the comparator actually used here, **not canonical SOFA**. Follows original SOFA thresholds for lungs, brain, clotting and liver, but **omits urine output** from the kidney score and uses **yes/no drug flags** instead of dose bands for cardiovascular support, because doses could not be harmonised across hospitals.

**SOFA 2.0** — a recent consensus update built with empirical analysis of over a million ICU admissions. **The authors could not evaluate it** because its required variables were not consistently captured here.

**eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU-CRD)** — a free, deidentified database of US ICU records: **200,859 stays, 139,367 patients, 2014–2015, 208 hospitals.** Access via PhysioNet after ethics training and a data use agreement.

**Additive integer score** — a score where each finding contributes a whole number of points and you add them up. **The point is auditability**: you can see exactly which findings drove a prediction, and compute it with a pen.

**Monotonicity constraint** — a rule forcing points to never decrease as the patient gets sicker. **Imposed here rather than learned**, so the score cannot produce the nonsense of a worse lab value earning fewer points.

**Supervised discretization** — cutting a continuous measurement into bands using the outcome to decide where the cuts go. Here done with a small decision tree per variable, with the number of bands chosen by **penalized validation log loss**.

**Constrained optimisation** — finding the best numbers subject to rules that cannot be broken (whole numbers, monotonic, bounded). **The rules cost some accuracy and buy interpretability.**

**L-BFGS-B** — a standard numerical routine for minimising a function within bounds. The ordering rules were imposed by rewriting the weights as non-negative increments so a bounds-only method could handle them.

**Isotonic regression** — a method that finds the closest set of numbers obeying a required ordering. Used here to make the starting weights monotonic before optimisation.

**Scale-anchoring penalty** — a term keeping the score's spread near a target so the weights do not all shrink toward zero, which would make the integer rounding meaningless.

**Cross-validation** — splitting data into parts, training on some and testing on the rest, rotating through. **10-fold** here.

**Grouped (by hospital)** — all records from one hospital stay in the same fold, so **no hospital appears in both training and testing.** The feature that makes these numbers mean "performance at a new hospital" — technique 331.

**Nested** — model choices made in an inner loop on separate validation data, never on the test fold. **Prevents the reported number from being a selection score.**

**Out-of-fold prediction** — a prediction for a patient made by a model that never saw that patient. **Each patient here contributed exactly one.**

**AUROC** — the chance the score ranks a patient who died above one who survived. 0.5 is a coin flip. **Measures ordering only, and does not change with how common the outcome is.**

**AUPRC** — area under the precision-recall curve. **Depends heavily on prevalence**; a useless model scores about the event rate, so **always read it against that floor** — technique 333.

**Calibration slope** — compares predicted risks against observed. **1.0 is perfect.** Here **1.011 (0.970–1.055)**, the tightest in this reading list.

**Calibration-in-the-large** — whether predictions are on average too high or too low overall. **0.008 here**, essentially nil.

**Observed-to-expected (O:E) ratio** — observed deaths divided by predicted. **1.005 overall; 1.400 in short-stay patients**, meaning both models under-predicted death there.

**Brier score** — average squared error of predicted probabilities. **Lower is better, but it mixes discrimination and calibration**, which is why a lower Brier does not establish better calibration.

**Integrated calibration index / E50 / E90 / E-max** — measures of how far the calibration curve strays from perfect, on average, at the median, at the 90th percentile and at its worst. **All were higher (worse) for the new score**, which is why the paper declines to claim better calibration.

**Standardized mean difference (SMD)** — a difference between two groups expressed in units of their own spread. **Above 0.1 is a notable imbalance.** Used here to show that included and excluded patients were very different — technique 332.

**Complete-case analysis** — using only records with no missing values. **Excluded 90% here**, and the survivors were far sicker.

**Inverse prevalence weighting** — up-weighting the rarer outcome during fitting so the model does not ignore it. Used here **only in the discrimination objective**; the calibration layer was fitted **unweighted** so probabilities match real prevalence.

**AutoScore** — an existing published method that builds interpretable point-based scores from scratch. **Scored 0.810 against this paper's 0.811.**

**XGBoost** — a strong, non-interpretable "black box" gradient-boosting model. **Scored 0.818**, the best in the table.

**Decision curve analysis** — a way of asking whether using a model beats treating everyone or no one, across different thresholds of concern. Run here, in an unreachable figure.

**TRIPOD+AI** — the reporting guideline for prediction-model studies using AI. **Not named here, in the best-reported primary study in this reading list.**

## 7. What You Can (and Can't) Say

**You can say:**

"In 14,848 adult first ICU admissions from 208 US hospitals in the eICU database (2014–2015), with ICU mortality of 17.03%, a recalibrated SOFA-derived integer score achieved an AUROC of 0.811 (95% CI 0.800–0.819) against 0.751 (0.740–0.761) for a prespecified SOFA-derived reference score, a paired improvement of 0.059 (0.053–0.067)."

"The improvement held in all 10 hospital-grouped cross-validation folds, in all three length-of-stay strata, and in all 1,000 replications of the full modelling pipeline."

"Performance was estimated from pooled out-of-fold predictions of 10-fold nested cross-validation grouped by hospital, with no hospital contributing to both development and evaluation, and the final published weights were refit on the full cohort and contributed no performance estimate."

"The calibration slope was 1.011 (95% CI 0.970–1.055) and the observed-to-expected ratio 1.005, with the Brier score lower than the reference score's (0.112 versus 0.123) — but the authors report that other calibration-error measures were higher and state that the findings do not indicate consistently superior calibration."

"On the same folds, penalized logistic regression scored 0.775, AutoScore 0.810, the recalibrated score 0.811 and XGBoost 0.818."

"Of 148,532 encounters with outcome data, 133,684 (90%) were excluded for at least one missing measurement, and included patients were substantially sicker than excluded ones — 64.2% versus 20.2% mechanically ventilated, and 17.03% versus 4.54% ICU mortality."

"In a prespecified sensitivity analysis expanding the cohort to 61,469 encounters, the median paired AUROC improvement was 0.037."

"The authors state that external and prospective validation is required before clinical use, and that the primary performance estimates should not be extrapolated directly to unselected ICU admissions."

**You cannot say:**

"The new score beats SOFA." **The comparator was a SOFA-derived score lacking urine output and using binary rather than dose-graded vasoactive indicators**, because canonical SOFA could not be reconstructed from this database. The paper says comparisons should be read against that reference, not against canonical SOFA.

"It improves ICU mortality prediction by 6 percentage points." **In the less-selected sensitivity cohort the improvement was 0.037**, a 37% smaller margin. Quote both numbers, or quote the smaller one.

"These results apply to ICU patients." **They apply to the sickest tenth** — a cohort selected by having had an arterial blood gas and a liver panel drawn, with nearly four times the mortality of those excluded. The authors say so explicitly.

"It is better calibrated than SOFA." **The paper explicitly declines this claim.** The Brier score was lower but the integrated calibration index, E50, E90 and E-max were all higher.

"This method outperforms other interpretable scoring methods." **AutoScore scored 0.810 against 0.811**, with overlapping intervals. The paper's own contribution claim is **structural** — preserving SOFA's organ-domain skeleton — not predictive.

"It matches black-box performance" or "the interpretability–performance trade-off is a myth." **XGBoost scored 0.818.** The gap is small (0.007) and arguably clinically unimportant, but it is not zero, and the conclusion's stronger phrasing is contradicted by the paper's own table.

"The new weights show what organ failure really means." **The paper refuses this four separate times.** The score runs 0–71 rather than 0–24, the cutpoints are explicitly "not revised physiological definitions," and the zero weight for dobutamine "should not be interpreted as evidence of clinical neutrality."

"It can replace SOFA." **No.** The authors state it is "not intended to replace the canonical SOFA for organ dysfunction assessment" — SOFA also serves description, sequential assessment and the Sepsis-3 definition, none of which this addresses.

"Validated across 208 hospitals, so it generalises." **That is internal validation with a wide geographic spread**, all within one US telehealth network, one data pipeline and one documentation culture. **No external validation was performed**; the authors name MIMIC-IV, AmsterdamUMCdb and HiRID as future work.

"It predicts death with 92% accuracy in short stays." **The 0.924 AUROC in the 2-day stratum reflects an easier task** — 44.40% of deaths occurred within 2 days, so that is largely predicting imminent death from current physiology.

"The score is ready to use — there's a calculator." **A calculator exists and the authors say prospective evaluation is needed first.** They also note that workflow integration "has not been evaluated prospectively."

**The honest one-sentence version:** *the best-validated primary study in this reading list — hospital-grouped nested cross-validation with every choice inside the loop, the tightest calibration slope here, a thousand-replication stability analysis, and a sensitivity analysis that cut its own headline by 37% and was published anyway — showing that an integer score you can add up by hand captures about 90% of the distance from a 1996 consensus instrument to gradient boosting, and limited by one database, a cohort that excluded 9 in 10 eligible patients and kept the sickest, a comparator handicapped by missing urine output and drug doses, and an existing method that matched it to within 0.001.*

## 8. Bottom Line for Your Life

**If you or someone you love is in intensive care:** nothing here changes any treatment, and this is not a tool anyone can use on you yet — the authors say external and prospective validation must come first. **What is worth knowing is more general and more useful.** ICU teams use scores like SOFA to summarise how many organ systems are struggling, and those scores inform conversations about prognosis. **This paper is a reminder that such a score is a summary, not a verdict** — it was built to describe groups of patients, its numbers were set by a committee in 1996, and even the improved version here is explicitly described by its own authors as "a mortality risk model" rather than a statement about an individual. **If a number is quoted to you, the fair questions are what it is based on and what it would change.**

**If you work in an ICU or build clinical scores, this paper is the strongest argument yet for a third option.** The usual choice is presented as "keep the familiar score" or "deploy a black box nobody can audit." **Here the integer score — computable with a pen, every point traceable to a named clinical range — landed 0.007 below gradient boosting and 0.036 above penalized logistic regression, on identical folds.** That is about **90% of the available improvement, for none of the opacity.** **And the honest caveat the authors supply themselves: an existing method, AutoScore, got the same number.** So the lesson is not "use this framework"; it is **"re-estimating an old score's thresholds from outcome data is worth doing, and you do not have to give up auditability to do it."**

**If you build or evaluate prediction models, copy four specific things from this paper.** **Group your cross-validation folds by whatever you need to generalise across** — site, patient, device — and group the *inner* validation split the same way. **Put every tuning decision inside the loop**, then say in one sentence that your final full-data refit contributed no performance estimate; that sentence closes the commonest loophole in the field. **Report your paired difference, not just your absolute score** — here the absolute AUROC wandered by 0.091 across hospital groups while the paired difference moved only 0.031, so the comparison was three times more stable than the measurement. **And run the sensitivity analysis that could embarrass you, then publish it**: expanding the cohort cut this paper's headline from 0.059 to 0.037, and they printed it.

**The habit most worth taking from this entry, though, is about missing data.** **A 90% complete-case exclusion is not a precision problem, it is a population change** — and the way to see it is standardized mean differences between included and excluded, not p-values, which are meaningless at this sample size. **Here the mechanism is worth remembering because it generalises far beyond the ICU: an arterial blood gas and a liver panel are not drawn on patients who are doing well, so "has complete data" quietly means "was sick enough to be investigated."** Any study built on routinely collected health records faces some version of this. **Ask what fraction was excluded, how the two groups differ, and whether the missingness was plausibly caused by how sick the patient was.** If the answer to the last is yes, no imputation method fixes it — only a sensitivity analysis bounds it.

**And one lesson for reading numbers anywhere.** This paper's expanded-cohort analysis shows the **same models** producing a **higher** AUROC and a **lower** AUPRC at the same time, purely because the death rate fell from 17% to 8%. **Measured against the floor a useless model would achieve, the precision-recall performance actually improved — 2.91 times the floor became 4.94 times the floor.** So whenever you see a precision-recall number, or any "how many of my alerts are real" statistic, **find the underlying event rate before deciding whether the number is good.** The same detector looks worse on an emptier beach.

**If you are reading the coverage:** expect "AI modernises 30-year-old ICU score" or "machine learning improves sepsis scoring by 6 points." **Four things will likely be missing.** That the comparator was a SOFA-derived score missing urine output and drug doses, not SOFA itself. That 90% of eligible patients were excluded and the remainder were four times more likely to die. That the improvement fell to 0.037 in a less-selected cohort, in the paper's own sensitivity analysis. And that **an existing published method scored 0.810 against this one's 0.811** — which the authors state plainly and which makes their real contribution a structural one about preserving a familiar score, not a performance record.

**This is education, not medical advice.** Nothing here concerns any diagnosis or treatment decision, and no score discussed here is validated for clinical use. Questions about the care of a critically ill person belong with the treating team.
