## 1. The Gist

**Two groups of people with depression had their brains scanned before starting treatment and again one or two weeks later. Some got a real antidepressant; some got a dummy pill. A computer could tell, from the brain scans alone, which scan came first — with about 94% accuracy. But it could not tell who had taken the real drug. That second fact is the paper's real finding, and the authors say so plainly.**

**Start with the problem.** Antidepressants work, but **barely better than a dummy pill** in many trials, and nobody can predict who will benefit. Treatment is, in the paper's words, *"trial-and-error clinical practice."* So the question: **in the first week or two, what actually changes in the brain — and how much of it is the drug rather than the act of being treated?**

**What they did.** They took **two existing studies**. The first, **EMBARC**, randomly assigned people with depression to **sertraline** (a common antidepressant) or to **placebo**, and scanned them before treatment and again **one week** in: **123 on sertraline, 125 on placebo.** The second, **CAN-BIND-1**, gave everyone **escitalopram** (another antidepressant) and scanned at baseline and **two weeks** in: **138 people.** Total **386**, which I verified adds up, as does the sex split (257 female, 129 male).

**The first result, and it is striking.** They set the computer a deliberately odd task: given two scans from the same person **with the order hidden**, say which came first. **It got this right 94.4% of the time in the placebo group and 91.1% in the sertraline group** — near-perfect. The brain changes were **the same in both arms** (similarity 0.813), and a model trained on one arm worked on the other. **So something real and highly consistent happens in the brain in the first week of treatment — and it happens just as much on a dummy pill.**

**What changes:** connections strengthened among the **visual cortex, the precuneus** (a hub at the back of the brain involved in self-referential thought) **and the thalamus** (the brain's relay station). **All 60 connections in this system went up.** The authors call this a **"universal placebo effect."**

**But here is the honest caveat they themselves raise first.** Their own words: these changes *"may reflect a universal placebo effect **and/or changes pertaining to repeated scan sessions**."* Being scanned twice might itself produce the pattern — you are less nervous the second time, you know what the scanner sounds like. **To rule this out they tested healthy volunteers who got no treatment but the same two scans, and the model failed on them: 48.3% and 41.4%, which is chance.** Good test. **The problem is that there were only 29 healthy volunteers**, and I worked out that with 29 people the range consistent with pure chance runs from about **32% to 68%**. **So this control cannot exclude a moderate scanning effect — it can only say there isn't a large one.** The paper nonetheless concludes there is a *"high likelihood"* the signal is not scan-related. That is the one place where its language outruns its evidence.

**The second result is the one that matters most, and it is a negative.** They tried to predict, from the first week's brain changes, **who got the drug and who got the placebo.** It did not work. **Early brain changes cannot distinguish an antidepressant from a sugar pill.** They also tried to predict who would get better on sertraline: that did not work either. Then they tried to predict the "real drug" part of improvement by subtracting the expected placebo part: **three separate attempts, every one worse than useless (R² below zero).** **All four of these failures are reported in the paper.** That is unusual and it deserves saying plainly.

**The third result is where the paper gets ambitious, and where I have concerns.** Since a straightforward drug-versus-placebo classifier failed, they tried a different approach: look for a pattern of brain change that **varies more** in the drug group than the placebo group — the logic being that a drug adds its own effect on top of the placebo effect, so it should widen the spread. **This worked** (variance ratio F = 1.58, P = 0.005), and the pattern centred on the **amygdala, midcingulate cortex, orbitofrontal cortex and cerebellum**. **And they ran the test backwards as a check — placebo as the "drug" group — which correctly found nothing.** They declared that falsification test in advance. Credit.

**Then they split the sertraline group in two**: **47 people (38%)** with the drug-specific pattern, **76** without. The claim is that the second group *"responded as if treated with placebo"* — and that the modest overall edge of sertraline over placebo comes mainly from the first group.

**Three numbers carry that claim, and all three sit just inside the line.** The placebo-response model predicted improvement in the no-drug-pattern group at **P = 0.032** (explaining 5.7% of the variance). The brain–symptom correlation in that group was **P = 0.048**. And the difference in improvement between the two subgroups was **P = 0.037 — one-tailed, which is P = 0.074 if you test both directions.** **And the subgroups themselves were created by a cutoff anchored to the single most extreme score in the placebo group** — so if that one person's number had been different, the groups, and every result resting on them, would shift.

**Fourth, a clean statistical error, and it is worth learning from.** The paper reports that the visual-precuneus-thalamus change correlated with improvement **in the placebo arm (r = 0.193, P = 0.043)** but **not in the sertraline arm (r = 0.154, P = 0.124)**, and infers *"a potential specificity of the VPT system to pure placebo effect."* **But 0.193 and 0.154 are almost the same number.** I tested the difference directly: **P = 0.75.** **The two correlations are statistically indistinguishable.** One landed just under 0.05 and the other just over, and that gap is noise, not specificity.

**Fifth, what "two independent cohorts, N = 386" actually means.** Only **EMBARC has a placebo group.** **CAN-BIND-1 gave everyone the drug** — so every placebo comparison in this paper rests on **248 people, not 386.** The second cohort also used a different drug, a different scan interval (**two weeks, not one**), different symptom scales, different entry criteria and different scanner resolution. **The paper says all of this clearly in its Methods and Discussion**, and even recommends that future studies include a placebo arm. **The abstract does not.**

**And something genuinely new, which I want to put on record.** This paper does something no previous entry in this list has done: **it builds the reliability of its own measurement into the model's objective.** Brain connectivity measured by MRI is notoriously noisy. Because each person was scanned **twice at each time point**, the authors required that any brain pattern the model uses must **give the same answer across the two runs** — reliability as a mathematical constraint rather than a hoped-for property. **And they show it improved the result.** They close by recommending the field adopt it. **That is the correct response to a problem this reading list has raised against paper after paper.**

**Finally, the check this list has never been able to run.** The paper names a trial registration, so **I retrieved the registry record itself.** It shows the parent trial's **Aim 2, written in 2011**: *"To identify early phase (week 1) changes in neuroimaging, neurophysiological, and behavioral tasks as mediators of differential treatment outcomes... to SERT and PBO."* **That is this paper's subject, prespecified fifteen years before publication.** After five consecutive entries that named no guideline and could not be checked, here is one whose research question is verifiably on the record in advance.

## 2. Study Snapshot

**Study type:** secondary mechanistic neuroimaging analysis of two existing treatment cohorts, one randomised placebo-controlled and one single-arm, using newly developed predictive and contrastive machine-learning frameworks. **Not a report of a trial's primary outcome.**

**Discovery cohort — EMBARC.** Recruited **296 patients with major depressive disorder and 52 healthy controls**, aged 18–65, **randomly assigned to sertraline or placebo**, with randomisation *"stratified to ensure balanced distributions of study site, depression severity, and chronicity across treatment arms."* Entry required MDD as primary diagnosis by structured interview, a **Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology score ≥14**, **onset before age 30**, and **no antidepressant failure in the current episode**. Thirteen exclusion criteria are listed. Dosing began at **50 mg rising to a maximum of 200 mg**. Response measured as **change in the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale** from pre- to post-treatment. **fMRI at baseline and 1 week.**

**EMBARC flow, and a sentence worth untangling.** The paper states that *"with 9 patients having dropped out before receiving the first dose and 22 discontinued during the treatment, 137 patients in the sertraline arm and 142 in the placebo arm completed 1 week of treatment, among which 129 sertraline-treated and 136 placebo-treated patients completed the 8-week full course."* **Read naively, 296 − 9 − 22 should give the 1-week figure; it does not** (265 against 279). **Worked through properly, every number is correct and mutually consistent:** 296 − 9 = 287 received a first dose; 287 − 22 = **265**, which is exactly 129 + 136, the 8-week completers; and the 22 splits as 8 lost during week 1 (287 − 279) plus 14 after it (279 − 265). **So the 9 and 22 describe attrition down to eight weeks, not down to one.** I verified all of it.

**Imaging exclusions.** *"Only the participants with two high-quality resting-state fMRI runs at both time points were retained,"* giving **123 sertraline (88 female, 35 male) and 125 placebo (82 female, 43 male)**, plus **29 healthy controls**. **That is 14 and 17 removed from the 1-week completers, 31 in all — of which only 11 are itemised** (six placebo and five sertraline with a single run at some time point), **leaving 20 unexplained**. **The healthy controls fell from 52 to 29, a 44% loss that is never accounted for.** Patients without an endpoint Hamilton score were also excluded.

**Replication cohort — CAN-BIND-1.** Initially **196 patients and 110 healthy controls**; outpatients aged 18–60 with an episode of **at least 3 months** and a **Montgomery–Åsberg score ≥24**; thirteen exclusion criteria including resistance to four or more antidepressants. After a washout of at least five half-lives, **all patients received escitalopram 10–20 mg daily for 8 weeks** — **there is no placebo arm.** **fMRI at baseline and 2 weeks.** Analysed: **138 patients (87 female, 51 male) and 87 healthy controls.**

**Totals verified:** 123 + 125 + 138 = **386**; 88 + 82 + 87 = **257 female**; 35 + 43 + 51 = **129 male**; 257 + 129 = **386**. Every figure in the abstract reproduces exactly.

**Imaging.** EMBARC: single-shot gradient echo-planar, four sites on **three different scanner vendors** (GE, Siemens, Philips) with standardised parameters — 2,000 ms repetition time, 28 ms echo time, **3.2 × 3.2 × 3.1 mm voxels**, 39 slices, 180 volumes. CAN-BIND-1: 10-minute eyes-open scan, six sites across three vendors, 2,000 ms repetition time, 30 ms echo time, **4 × 4 × 4 mm voxels**, with *"minor protocol variations."* Both preprocessed with **fMRIPrep**, including fieldmap distortion correction, boundary-based registration, 6 mm smoothing and independent-component motion removal, with **quality control excluding data above 0.5 mm framewise displacement**.

**Connectivity.** Time series averaged over **135 regions** — the 100-parcel Schaefer cortical atlas plus 35 subcortical parcels covering striatum, cerebellum, amygdala, hippocampus and thalamus. Connectivity computed as Pearson correlation between every region pair, then Fisher transformed. **The paper never states how many connections that is. It is 135 × 134 ÷ 2 = 9,045 per scan.**

**The analytic framework, and its central innovation.** A single objective combining a **signal-to-noise constraint**, a **prediction loss** and **L0 sparsity regularisation**. The signal-to-noise term exploits the fact that **each person had two fMRI runs at each time point**: it defines noise as the disagreement between the two runs and signal as the difference between time points, and minimises their ratio. In the authors' framing, *"dimension scores from different fMRI runs collected at the same time point for the same participant should agree."* **Crucially the dimension-finding and the prediction are optimised jointly, not in stages:** *"the identification of reliability-aware FC dimensions and the prediction task form an integrated process, instead of being separated into different stages."* A careful normalisation note is included: features were standardised **with both time points pooled**, because otherwise *"the difference in mean across time points, which is essentially the treatment-induced change we seek, would be eliminated."*

**Result 1 — the chronology task.** Each data point is a pair of scans from one person with the order hidden and randomly reversed for half the sample. **Placebo arm: accuracy 94.4%, AUC 0.991, permutation P < 0.001. Sertraline arm: accuracy 91.1%, AUC 0.984, P < 0.001.** Cross-arm: **93.5% and 94.4%**. Pattern similarity between arms: **cosine 0.813**. **Healthy controls: 48.3% and 41.4%** (binomial P = 1 and 0.458). Generalisation to the **11 excluded single-run patients: 100%, P < 0.0005** — which I verified as 0.5¹¹ = 0.000488. The pattern comprised **28 regions and 60 connections, all increasing**.

**Result 1b — what the VPT change relates to.** Correlated with 8-week placebo-arm response at **r = 0.193, P = 0.043**; **not** in the sertraline arm at **r = 0.154, P = 0.124**. Correlated with pretreatment flanker accuracy (**r = 0.205, P_FDR_ = 8.56 × 10⁻³**). **No** association with other cognitive tasks, baseline severity, childhood trauma, personality or mood/anxiety profiles.

**Result 2 — predicting placebo response.** From 1-week connectivity change: **R² = 0.113, r = 0.375, permutation P < 0.001.** **No significant predictability for sertraline response** (Supplementary Table 1, unreachable). The predictive changes were mostly **decreases**, involving striatum–ventral attention, cerebellum–dorsal attention, striatum–frontoparietal, within-dorsal-attention and striatum–default-mode connections. The prediction score correlated with VPT change (**r = 0.286, P = 2.33 × 10⁻³**) and with choice reaction time (**r = −0.256, P_FDR_ = 0.046**).

**Result 3 — the drug-specific dimension.** Motivated explicitly by a null: *"Given the lack of significant results from the treatment arm prediction model based on linear FC dimensions."* The contrastive variance quotient seeks a dimension with greater variance in the drug arm, with a **test–retest penalty in its denominator**, inside **five-round 20-fold cross-validation** (95% training, 5% held out). Result: **F = 1.58, P = 0.005; permutation P = 0.010**. Regions: **left default-mode-linked striatum, right amygdala, right midcingulate, left orbitofrontal cortex, left cerebellum**. *"Incorporating test–retest reliability terms significantly improved the generalizability."* **The prespecified falsification test — placebo as foreground — found nothing**, as required. **A feature pre-screen is applied: "selecting FC features that show potentially significant specificity to the treatment arm (uncorrected one-tailed P value <0.01 from the F-test)."**

**Result 4 — the subtypes.** Threshold **τ = max(|placebo dimension score|) × √F_crit**, with F_crit ≈ 1.5. **47 sertraline-specific (38.2%) and 76 non-specific = 123**, verified. The placebo model predicted improvement in the non-specific group (**R² = 0.057, r = 0.275, P = 0.032**) while failing on the full arm (**R² < 0, r = 0.155, P = 0.121**). VPT correlated with response in the non-specific group (**r = 0.254, P = 0.048**). Specific patients improved more than non-specific (**one-tailed t-test P = 0.037**). Pretreatment connectivity differed between subgroups (**P_FDR_ < 0.05**); **no structural connectivity or clinical measure distinguished them**. **Real drug response — actual minus model-expected placebo response — was not predictable in three separate attempts, all R² < 0.**

**Result 5 — generalization.** Because CAN-BIND-1 lacks a placebo arm and differs in timing and drug, the paper *"focus[es] on the generalization of early FC changes that are not tailored to specific prediction targets,"* using healthy controls from both cohorts as background. Cross-validated: **placebo F = 16.7, sertraline F = 14.8, escitalopram F = 1.57** (all P ≤ 0.01). Dimension-score correlations: placebo-derived against escitalopram-derived **r = 0.641 / 0.579 / 0.611** across the three arms; sertraline-derived against escitalopram-derived **r = 0.500 / 0.614 / 0.689**; **sertraline-specific against escitalopram-derived r = 0.300 and 0.405, and r = 0.055 (P = 0.542) in the placebo arm** — the negative control behaving as it should.

**Limitations the authors state.** No early signature reliably predicts individual sertraline response, *"which may reflect the dominance of placebo effect at early stages."* **The absence of a placebo arm in CAN-BIND-1 means they "were unable to determine whether sertraline and escitalopram exhibit distinct early drug-specific FC changes,"** with an explicit recommendation that future studies *"include a placebo control or a placebo lead-in period."* Direct replication of the prediction tasks *"was not feasible due to differences in medication type and fMRI acquisition timing."* They also call for more time points, dose-escalation studies, work in more severe and hospitalised patients, and *"clinical trials... to test the utility of drug-specific subgrouping."*

**Not reported or not retrievable:** **funding, conflicts of interest, data availability, code availability**; **the number of connectivity features**; **CAN-BIND-1's own registration number**; **any reporting guideline**; an accounting for 20 of the 31 imaging exclusions and for 23 of the 52 healthy controls; Supplementary Tables 1 and 2 (which hold the null results); all 17 supplementary figures; and Appendix A.

## 3. How Strong Is This Evidence? (Grade 4 out of 5)

**The strongest methodological profile this reading list has assessed: a research question verifiably prespecified in a 2011 trial registration, measurement reliability built into the model's objective function rather than hoped for, four substantial null results reported including ones that undercut the paper's own clinical ambition, and a falsification test declared in advance that came out the right way. It falls short of 5 because the clinically load-bearing subtyping claim rests on three threshold-grazing results in subgroups the paper defined itself, because one arm-specificity inference is a clean statistical error, and because the control that excludes the headline's main rival explanation has only 29 people in it.**

**What is done well, and the first three items are better than anything previously in this list.**

**The research question was prespecified, and I verified it against the registry rather than taking the paper's word.** The ClinicalTrials.gov record for NCT01407094 lists, as **Aim 2 of the parent trial in 2011**: *"To identify early phase (week 1) changes in neuroimaging, neurophysiological, and behavioral tasks as mediators of differential treatment outcomes (symptom change, tolerability) to SERT and PBO."* **That is precisely this paper's subject.** The registered primary outcome is **HAM-D17 at week 8**, and the paper uses exactly that scale and interval as its response measure. **There is no outcome switching here** — the week-8 primary belongs to the parent trial's own report, and this is the registered mediator analysis. **After five consecutive entries that named no guideline and could not be checked, this is the first whose central question is demonstrably on the public record in advance.**

**Test–retest reliability is an optimisation constraint, not an afterthought — and this is the single most important thing in the paper.** Resting-state connectivity is acknowledged in the paper's own words to have *"low SNR"* and *"high test–retest variability,"* and connectivity **changes** are worse still, because differencing two noisy numbers amplifies noise. Because each person was scanned **twice at each time point**, the authors could require that any dimension the model learns must agree across the two runs, and they wrote that requirement into the loss function. **They then demonstrate it worked** — *"incorporating test–retest reliability terms significantly improved the generalizability of the identified FC change dimension"* — and recommend the field follow suit: *"We recommend that future resting-state fMRI studies adopt SNR-aware objectives."* **This list has raised measurement reliability against paper after paper (techniques 294, 317, 329, 360). This is the first paper to solve it by construction.**

**Four distinct null results are reported, including ones that damage the paper's own story.** Early connectivity change **cannot** distinguish drug from placebo. It **cannot** predict sertraline response. Estimated "real drug response" was **not predictable in three separate attempts, all R² below zero** — and the paper states each attempt and each failure. **No** structural or clinical measure distinguished the subgroups. **A paper that reports the failure of the exact analysis a reader would most want to succeed is behaving correctly**, and the contrast with entry 84, whose headline was contradicted by its own table without comment, could not be sharper.

**A falsification test was declared in advance and passed.** Before running the variance-contrast analysis, the paper specified: *"A negative result of this analysis would support the significance of the identified sertraline-specific dimension, whereas a positive result would suggest the drug-specific FC change findings may be a false positive."* Running placebo as the foreground found nothing. **Specifying in advance what result would refute you, then reporting it, is the practice this whole reading list exists to look for.** A second negative control in the generalization analysis behaved correctly too (**r = 0.055, P = 0.542** in the placebo arm).

**The paper limits its own headline interpretation, at length and unprompted.** An entire Methods subsection explains what the chronology analysis **cannot** establish: *"the non-overlapping components do not necessarily represent placebo- or sertraline-specific FC changes... The chronology prediction models are designed to identify the maximally shared FC changes... and are not capable of isolating genuine 'placebo-specific' and 'sertraline-specific' effects."* **Authors rarely write a paragraph whose purpose is to stop readers over-reading their best number.**

**And it does not commit the error I was watching for.** The pre-registered worry was that a within-group before-and-after change would be presented as a treatment effect. **The paper's central intellectual move is exactly the opposite:** it finds a large within-group change, observes that it occurs equally on placebo, and therefore **names it a placebo effect rather than claiming it for the drug.** Separating the two is the paper's whole point.

**Also to its credit:** randomisation **stratified by site, severity and chronicity**; permutation testing throughout; **FDR correction applied to the correlational analyses**; a subtle normalisation trap identified and avoided; **cross-arm held-out testing** in both directions; dual-cohort ethics and compensation disclosed for both; and a limitations paragraph that names the missing placebo arm and recommends placebo lead-ins for future work.

**Why this is not a 5.**

**The clinical claim rests on three results that sit just inside the threshold, in subgroups the paper created.** *"The higher efficacy of sertraline is largely attributable to sertraline-specific patients"* depends on: the placebo model predicting improvement in the non-specific group at **P = 0.032** and **R² = 0.057** (5.7% of variance); the brain–symptom correlation in that group at **P = 0.048**; and the between-subgroup improvement difference at **P = 0.037 one-tailed — which is P = 0.074 two-tailed.** **Three threshold-grazing results supporting one narrative is a fragile structure**, and the paper presents the subgrouping as established enough to propose *"a brain signature that may serve... to identify sertraline-specific patients at baseline."*

**And the subgroups hinge on a single observation.** The cutoff is **τ = max(|placebo dimension score|) × √F_crit** — anchored to **the largest absolute score in the placebo arm**. A sample maximum is among the least stable statistics there is: it has high variance and drifts upward with sample size. **Every number in the paragraph above inherits that instability**, because all of them are computed within groups the threshold defines.

**One inference is a clean statistical error.** From **r = 0.193 (P = 0.043)** in placebo and **r = 0.154 (P = 0.124)** in sertraline, the paper infers *"a potential specificity of the VPT system to pure placebo effect."* **Testing the difference between those two correlations directly gives P = 0.75** — they are indistinguishable. One crossed 0.05 and one did not, and nothing follows from that. **The paper does hedge with "potential," and offers a mechanistic reason why the sertraline arm might obscure the effect, which is fair. But the stated evidence for specificity is not evidence.**

**The control that excludes the headline's main rival explanation has 29 people in it.** The authors correctly raise repeated scanning as an alternative to "universal placebo effect," and correctly test it in untreated controls, who came out at **48.3% and 41.4%**. **But with 29 controls the range consistent with pure chance spans roughly 32% to 68%.** A null on 29 people rules out a large scanning effect, not a moderate one — and the paper concludes a *"high likelihood"* that the signal is not scan-related. **With 94% accuracy as the headline, this is the caveat that most deserved a power statement and did not get one.**

**"Two independent cohorts (N = 386)" overstates what was replicated.** **Only EMBARC has a placebo arm**, so every placebo-versus-drug contrast in the paper rests on **248 people**. CAN-BIND-1 differs in drug, **scan interval (2 weeks against 1)**, symptom scale, entry criteria and **voxel size (4 mm against 3.2 mm)**. **The Methods and Discussion say all of this explicitly and the paper earns credit for that** — but the abstract does not, and a reader who stops there will believe the dissociation was replicated in 386 people.

**The generalization correlations are not out-of-sample clinical tests.** Correlations of **r = 0.5 to 0.69** between dimension scores look like strong replication. But **both scores in each comparison are different weighted sums of the same connectivity changes in the same scans of the same people.** "Independent cohort" here means *the arm not used to derive that dimension* — a real and meaningful check on where the weights came from, but not a test of a clinical claim on new data, and two weight vectors over shared input will share variance for arithmetic reasons alone.

**Also against it:** the **feature space is never stated** (I computed 9,045 connections from the stated 135 regions), and the variance-contrast analysis applies an **uncorrected, one-tailed screen at P < 0.01**, which on 9,045 edges would admit about **90 features by chance alone**, with the text leaving it **ambiguous whether that screen ran inside or outside the cross-validation folds**; the **escitalopram dimension is roughly 10.6× weaker in F than the placebo one (1.57 against 16.7)** and this goes unremarked; **20 of 31 imaging exclusions and 23 of 52 healthy controls are unaccounted for**; **effect sizes are small throughout** (3.7%, 5.7%, 6.5% and 11.3% of variance); **funding, conflicts, data and code are all not retrievable**, which is especially unfortunate given the subject matter; **CAN-BIND-1's registration is never given**; and **no reporting guideline is named**.

**Why this is not a 3.** Entries 85, 86 and 87 each earned a 3 with a central claim that was asserted but untested, or tested only against a comparator that could not settle it. **Here the central negative claims are directly tested and reported as failures, the key alternative explanation is tested rather than dismissed, the falsification condition was declared in advance, the measurement problem is solved by construction, and the research question is on the public record from 2011.** The defects are in an exploratory extension that the paper itself labels empirical, and in one hedged inference. **What is claimed is, for the most part, less than what is shown.**

**What would make this a 5, concretely.** **Test the difference between the two correlations** instead of comparing their significance. **Replace the maximum-anchored threshold** with a percentile or a model-based cutoff, and show the subgroup results are stable across reasonable alternatives. **Report a power statement for the healthy-control test**, or pool the 87 CAN-BIND-1 controls into it — which would take the control group from 29 to 116 and could settle the scanning question outright. **State the number of connectivity features and whether the univariate screen sat inside the folds.** **And report the subgroup difference two-tailed.** **Every one of these uses data already in hand**, and the fourth is a sentence.

## 4. The Editor's Concerns

**1. The registry check — and it comes out in the paper's favour, which is worth stating first.** This list has spent five entries noting that a named checklist tells you nothing about what was done. **This is the first entry where a registered protocol existed and could be retrieved.** The record for **NCT01407094** shows the parent trial as **EMBARC, UT Southwestern, Phase 2, enrollment 296, running 2011-07-29 to 2016-04**, with a **single registered primary outcome: HAM-D17 at week 8** — the exact measure and interval this paper uses for treatment response. **And registered Aim 2 is, verbatim, this paper's subject**: early week-1 neuroimaging changes as mediators of differential sertraline-versus-placebo outcome. **The question was prespecified at design, fifteen years before this publication, and there is no sign of outcome switching.** **Two gaps the registry also reveals:** registered interventions are **Sertraline, Placebo and BupropionXL** — **no escitalopram**, confirming that the 138-patient escitalopram cohort comes from a different study whose **registration the paper never provides**; and **BupropionXL, which is registered, does not appear here** (it is EMBARC's second stage, so its absence is expected, but a reader comparing paper to registry should know). **The single NCT number in the abstract covers 248 of the 386 participants.**

**2. Only one of the two cohorts has a placebo arm, so the headline N overstates the evidence base.** **CAN-BIND-1 gave escitalopram to everyone.** Every placebo contrast — the universal placebo effect, the response-predictive placebo effect, the drug-specific dimension, the subtyping — **rests on EMBARC's 248 patients.** The two cohorts also differ in drug, **scan interval (1 week against 2)**, symptom scale (**Hamilton against Montgomery–Åsberg**), entry threshold, age range, treatment history (EMBARC excluded prior treatment in the current episode; CAN-BIND-1 did not) and **voxel size**. **The paper states all of this plainly in Methods and Discussion and recommends a placebo arm for future work — genuine credit.** The abstract's *"two independent cohorts of patients with MDD who are receiving medication (N = 386)"* does not.

**3. A difference in significance is treated as evidence of a difference.** VPT change against response: **placebo r = 0.193 (P = 0.043), sertraline r = 0.154 (P = 0.124)**, read as *"a potential specificity of the VPT system to pure placebo effect."* **Testing the difference gives P = 0.75.** In variance terms the two explain **3.7% and 2.4%** — a gap of about one percentage point. **The paper hedges with "potential" and supplies a plausible mechanism for why the sertraline arm might mask a placebo effect, which is reasonable reasoning. The numbers offered as evidence for it are not.**

**4. The headline's main rival explanation is excluded on 29 people.** The paper raises repeated scanning itself — *"and/or changes pertaining to repeated scan sessions"* — and tests it properly in untreated controls, finding **48.3% and 41.4%**. **But 29 controls give a chance interval of roughly 32% to 68%.** The design of the test is right; its power is not stated. **And 87 further untreated controls exist in CAN-BIND-1 and are used elsewhere in the paper as background data — pooling them would take the control group to 116 and could settle this.** Against a headline of 94% accuracy and AUC 0.991, this is the gap most worth closing.

**5. Three threshold-grazing results carry the clinical narrative, in self-defined subgroups.** **P = 0.032 (R² = 0.057), P = 0.048, and P = 0.037 one-tailed (0.074 two-tailed).** Each is individually defensible; **together, as the sole support for "the higher efficacy of sertraline is largely attributable to sertraline-specific patients," they are thin** — particularly since the paper goes on to propose a baseline brain signature for identifying such patients prospectively.

**6. The subtype threshold is anchored to a sample maximum.** **τ = max(|DimScore_PLA|) × √F_crit.** A maximum has high variance and grows with sample size, so **the 47/76 split — and everything computed within those groups — depends on one placebo patient's extreme score.** A percentile cutoff, or a sensitivity analysis across cutoffs, would cost nothing and is not reported.

**7. The feature space is never stated, and one screen is uncorrected.** **135 regions means 9,045 connections per scan**, a number the paper never gives. Most of the multiplicity is handled legitimately — by **cross-validated prediction with held-out testing and L0 sparsity** rather than mass univariate testing, which is a defensible alternative to correction. **But the variance-contrast analysis pre-screens features at an "uncorrected one-tailed P value <0.01,"** which on 9,045 edges would pass about **90 by chance alone**, and the text does not make clear whether that screen ran **inside each cross-validation fold or once on the whole sample.** Given that this analysis produces the paper's drug-specific finding, the distinction matters, and it is exactly entry 86's nested-selection question in a new setting.

**8. The generalization correlations share their input data.** Correlations of **0.5 to 0.69** between dimension scores are presented as cross-cohort replicability. **But each correlation compares two different weighted sums of the same connectivity changes in the same scans.** The arm "not used to derive the dimension" is a real safeguard about where weights came from; it is **not** an out-of-sample test of the drug-versus-placebo dissociation, which **cannot be tested in CAN-BIND-1 at all.** **The paper's own Methods say exactly this**, and the negative control (**r = 0.055** in the placebo arm) is reassuring — but the Discussion's *"these early FC changes were further validated using an independent cohort"* invites a stronger reading than the design supports.

**9. The escitalopram dimension is an order of magnitude weaker, and nobody says so.** Cross-validated **F = 16.7 (placebo), 14.8 (sertraline), 1.57 (escitalopram)** — the replication cohort's own treatment signature is about **10.6× weaker in F** than the discovery cohort's. All three are reported as *"significant cross-validation performance"* without comment on the gap. It may reflect the 2-week interval, the different scanner resolution or the different background controls; **the paper offers no account.**

**10. A fifth of the imaging exclusions and nearly half the controls are unexplained.** From the 1-week completers to the analysed sample, **31 patients were removed and only 11 itemised.** **Healthy controls fell from 52 to 29 — 44% — with no accounting.** Since the controls carry the entire repeated-scanning argument, how those 29 were selected from 52 is not a trivial detail.

**11. The effect sizes are small, and the mechanistic language is large.** The abstract says the work *"implicate[s] striatal and attention networks in mediating placebo-related symptom improvement."* **"Mediating" is a strong causal word for R² = 0.113 and r = 0.193.** The paper's Discussion is more careful, building a plausible case from opioid and metabolic imaging literature — but **nothing here tests mediation**, and a 3.7%-of-variance correlation is a long way from a mediating circuit.

**12. No guideline, no disclosures — and a sixth consecutive distinct pattern.** 83 named none and met TRIPOD's substance; 84 claimed STROBE and TRIPOD and missed TRIPOD's core items; 85 named none and landed between; 86 named none and met the modelling items while ignoring measurement validity; 87 named none and met the data-handling items while ignoring the comparator. **Entry 88 names none, discloses nothing retrievable — and is nonetheless the only entry whose research question could be verified as prespecified, because its parent trial was registered in 2011.** **Six consecutive entries, six combinations. And a new lesson on top of the old one: the checklist tells you nothing, but the registry can tell you a great deal — when one exists, look it up.**

## 5. Statistics Spotlight

Three concepts, numbered from 367 in `queue.md`. The first is a clean error worth learning from; the second is the best practice this list has seen.

**A difference in significance is not a significant difference — test the difference itself.**

**This is the most common statistical error in the published literature, and here is a clean, checkable example.**

The paper measured how strongly the brain change related to symptom improvement, separately in each arm:

- **Placebo arm: r = 0.193, P = 0.043** — under 0.05, so "significant."
- **Sertraline arm: r = 0.154, P = 0.124** — over 0.05, so "not significant."

From this it concludes *"a potential specificity of the VPT system to pure placebo effect"* — the brain change matters for placebo but not for the drug.

**But look at the two correlations: 0.193 and 0.154.** They are nearly the same number. **What differs is not the effect; it is which side of an arbitrary line each P-value fell on.**

**Here is why that happens.** A P-value answers *"could this one result have arisen by chance if nothing were going on?"* It is not a measure of effect size. Two similar effects measured in similar samples can easily land on opposite sides of 0.05 — one at 0.043 and one at 0.124 — purely from noise. **With 125 people, the correlation needed to reach P = 0.05 is about 0.176. The placebo arm's 0.193 clears that bar by 0.017. The sertraline arm's 0.154 misses it by 0.022.** The gap between "significant" and "not significant" here is about **four-hundredths of a correlation**.

**The right test asks directly whether the two correlations differ.** The standard tool converts each correlation to a quantity whose behaviour is well understood (Fisher's transformation), then compares them:

- placebo: 0.193 → **0.1955**
- sertraline: 0.154 → **0.1552**
- difference: **0.0403**, against a standard error of **0.1286**
- so the test statistic is 0.0403 ÷ 0.1286 = **0.31**, giving **P = 0.75**

**P = 0.75. The two correlations are statistically indistinguishable.** There is no evidence of arm specificity in these numbers at all.

**In fairness, the paper hedges** with *"potential,"* and offers a real mechanistic reason the sertraline arm might look weaker — expectancy and drug effects mixed together could obscure the placebo signal. **That reasoning is legitimate. What is not legitimate is offering these two P-values as the evidence for it.**

**The everyday version.** Two shops run the same promotion. In shop A sales rise 19%, and the statistician says the rise is "real." In shop B sales rise 15%, and the statistician says that rise "could be chance." **It would be wrong to conclude the promotion works in shop A and not shop B.** A 19% rise and a 15% rise are nearly the same thing; all you learned is that shop A had slightly tidier numbers.

**Watch out for:** any sentence of the form *"X was significant in group 1 but not in group 2, suggesting specificity to group 1."* **This pattern is everywhere** — in subgroup analyses, sex differences, responder-versus-non-responder comparisons, and treatment-arm contrasts. **Three steps. Write down the two effect sizes, not the two P-values — if they are close, stop and be suspicious.** **Then ask whether the paper tested the difference** (an interaction term, a Fisher z test, a difference-in-differences): if the answer is no, the specificity claim is unsupported, however many asterisks appear. **And remember that a smaller group almost always has the larger P-value for the same effect**, so "significant only in the large subgroup" is usually a statement about subgroup size.

**Build test–retest reliability into the objective — the best practice in this reading list.**

**This paper's methodological innovation is the correct answer to a problem this list has raised repeatedly, and it deserves to be taught rather than merely praised.**

**The problem.** Functional connectivity from resting-state MRI is **noisy**. Scan the same person twice an hour apart and the numbers move, for reasons having nothing to do with their brain changing: head motion, breathing, caffeine, drowsiness, scanner drift. The paper states this plainly — connectivity has *"low SNR"* and *"high test–retest variability,"* which *"limit[s] the replicability of its findings."*

**And the problem gets worse, not better, when you study change.** This paper's whole subject is the **difference** between two scans. **Subtracting two noisy numbers gives a result noisier than either.** If each scan carries a certain amount of measurement error, their difference carries about **1.4 times** that error — while the real treatment signal may be small. **So a change score is the worst possible thing to compute from an unreliable measurement, and it is exactly what this paper needs.**

**The usual response is to hope, or to cite a reliability study from someone else's data. This paper's response is to make reliability a requirement.**

**How it works, and it is elegant.** Every participant was scanned **twice at each time point** — two runs before treatment, two runs a week later. That gives something precious: **two independent measurements of the same underlying thing.** So the authors defined:

- **noise** = how much the two runs *at the same time point* disagree
- **signal** = how much the measurement *changed between time points*

and then told the model to **find patterns that maximise signal divided by noise.** In their words, the model seeks dimensions where *"dimension scores from different fMRI runs collected at the same time point for the same participant should agree."* **A pattern that flickers between two scans taken minutes apart is rejected before it can ever be reported as a treatment effect.**

**And they show it mattered:** *"incorporating test–retest reliability terms significantly improved the generalizability of the identified FC change dimension, underscoring the importance of controlling for run-to-run fluctuations."* They end by recommending the field adopt it.

**The everyday version.** You want to know whether a child grew over the summer. You measure them in June and again in September — but your tape measure is sloppy, so each reading could be off by a centimetre or two. **The fix is to measure twice on each occasion.** If June's two readings disagree by three centimetres, you know not to trust a two-centimetre "growth." **And if you are choosing between several ways of measuring — against the wall, standing on tiptoe, with shoes on — pick the one whose two readings agree best, before you start comparing June with September.** That is exactly what this model does, automatically, across thousands of candidate measurements.

**Watch out for:** any study reporting a **change** in a noisy measurement — brain connectivity, heart rate variability, most biomarkers, questionnaire scores. **Three questions. Was the measurement repeated at each time point, so reliability could be estimated at all?** **If not, is a reliability figure quoted from anywhere, and is it from this population and protocol?** **And is the reported change larger than the measurement's own run-to-run variation?** **The tell that something has gone wrong: a change score with a reported precision tighter than the reliability of the thing being differenced.** Most papers cannot answer these questions. **This one made them part of the model.**

**A null result from a small control group is not evidence of absence — work out what the control could have detected.**

**The paper's most striking number needs a control group, and the control group is too small to do the job.**

**Set the scene.** A model distinguishes a person's before-treatment scan from their after-one-week scan with **94.4% accuracy**. That is remarkable. **But the obvious rival explanation is not treatment at all: it is that being scanned a second time differs from being scanned the first time.** You are calmer, you know the noise, you have practised holding still. The paper raises this itself — *"and/or changes pertaining to repeated scan sessions."*

**The test is right.** Take people who were scanned twice on the same schedule but **received no treatment**, and see whether the model can order their scans. If treatment is doing the work, it should fail. **It did fail: 48.3% and 41.4%, which is coin-flipping.**

**Now the question nobody asked: how big an effect could 29 people have detected?**

With 29 people, pure guessing produces accuracies scattered around 50% with a standard error of about **9 percentage points**. So the range consistent with chance runs roughly from **32% to 68%**.

**Which means: an accuracy of 65% — a substantial scanning effect — would have fallen comfortably inside "chance" on this test.** The study could only have detected a *large* scanning effect. **A moderate one would look exactly like what was observed.**

**So the honest reading of the control is: "repeated scanning does not produce a large effect here."** The paper's reading is stronger — *"a high likelihood it is primarily detecting changes related to starting a new treatment... and not changes pertaining to repeated scanning."*

**And the fix is sitting in the same paper.** CAN-BIND-1 contributed **87 untreated healthy controls**, used elsewhere as background data. **Pooling them would take the control group from 29 to 116, shrinking the chance range to about 41%–59%** and making a moderate scanning effect detectable. **The data are in hand.**

**Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence** — and the way to tell them apart is to ask what the study could have found if the effect were real.

**The everyday version.** You taste two cups of coffee and cannot tell which is decaf, so you declare them identical. **But you only had two sips.** Someone who tasted thirty cups might distinguish them easily. **Your failure to detect a difference is partly a fact about the coffee and mostly a fact about how little you drank.**

**Watch out for:** any claim resting on a **negative control**, a **non-significant** confounder check, or "no difference between groups." **Two steps. Find the control group's sample size** — it is often far smaller than the main analysis, because controls are cheaper to skimp on. **Then ask what effect that sample could have ruled out**, which for a proportion is roughly 2 ÷ √n in either direction. **The tell: a headline computed on hundreds of people, resting on a reassurance computed on dozens.**

## 6. Jargon Translator

**Major depressive disorder (MDD)** — clinical depression. The paper notes it carries *"substantial morbidity and mortality"* and that treatment remains *"trial-and-error."*

**Sertraline and escitalopram** — two **SSRIs** (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors), the most commonly prescribed class of antidepressant. **Sertraline was dosed 50–200 mg; escitalopram 10–20 mg.**

**Placebo** — a dummy pill with no active drug. **Only the EMBARC cohort had one.**

**Placebo effect** — real improvement following a treatment that has no active ingredient, driven by expectation, attention and the act of being cared for. **This paper's central claim is that most of the first week's brain change is of this kind.**

**Placebo lead-in** — giving everyone a dummy pill first, before active treatment. **The paper recommends it for future studies.**

**Functional connectivity (FC)** — how much two brain regions' activity rises and falls together while a person lies still doing nothing. Computed here as a correlation between their time series, then Fisher-transformed.

**Resting-state fMRI** — scanning the brain while the person simply rests, rather than performing a task.

**BOLD signal** — the blood-oxygen-level-dependent signal that fMRI actually measures: a proxy for neural activity via local blood oxygenation.

**Parcellation / Schaefer atlas** — dividing the cortex into standard regions. **Here 100 cortical parcels plus 35 subcortical ones = 135 regions, giving 9,045 region pairs** — a number the paper never states.

**Precuneus** — a hub at the back and middle of the brain, heavily involved in self-referential thought. **The strongest contributor to the universal pattern.**

**Thalamus** — the brain's central relay station. **Striatum** — deep structures central to reward and motivation. **Amygdala** — central to threat and emotion. **Cerebellum** — classically motor, increasingly implicated in cognition and mood. **Orbitofrontal cortex** and **midcingulate cortex** — frontal regions involved in valuation and conflict monitoring.

**Default-mode, frontoparietal control, dorsal attention, ventral attention and somatomotor networks** — standard groupings of regions that tend to activate together.

**Visual–precuneus–thalamus (VPT) system** — this paper's name for the pattern that changed in everyone: **28 regions, 60 connections, all increasing.**

**Framewise displacement** — a measure of head movement between scan volumes. **Data above 0.5 mm were excluded.**

**Test–retest reliability** — whether repeating a measurement on the same unchanged person gives the same answer. **Built into this paper's objective function rather than assumed** — technique 369.

**Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)** — how large the thing you want to measure is relative to the measurement's own wobble.

**Chronology prediction** — this paper's clever framing: hide the order of a person's two scans and ask the model which came first. **Forces it to find a consistent direction of change.**

**Contrastive variance quotient (CVQ)** — the paper's method for finding a pattern that **varies more** in one group than another, on the logic that a drug adds variability on top of the placebo response.

**L0 regularisation** — a penalty that forces most model weights to exactly zero, producing a small, interpretable set of features.

**Cross-validation** — repeatedly training on part of the data and testing on the held-out part. **Here five rounds of 20-fold, so each held-out set was about 5%.**

**Permutation test** — shuffling the labels many times to see how often chance alone reproduces your result.

**AUC / AUROC** — the chance the model ranks a true case above a non-case. **0.991 and 0.984 here.**

**R²** — the share of variance explained. **0.113 for placebo-response prediction; negative values mean worse than predicting the average.**

**Correlation (r)** — strength of linear association, −1 to +1. **Squaring it gives variance explained: r = 0.193 means 3.7%.**

**Fisher z transformation** — the standard way to compare two correlations properly — technique 367.

**One-tailed versus two-tailed test** — a one-tailed test looks for a difference in one prespecified direction and is easier to pass. **The key subgroup comparison here is one-tailed at P = 0.037, which is P = 0.074 two-tailed.**

**FDR (false discovery rate) correction** — adjusting P-values for having run many tests. **Applied here to the correlational analyses, reported as P_FDR_.**

**Mediator** — a variable that carries a treatment's effect through to the outcome. **The registry's Aim 2 uses this word; testing mediation requires more than a correlation.**

**ClinicalTrials.gov / NCT number** — the public registry where trials record their design and primary outcome **before** running. **NCT01407094 is EMBARC** — technique 368.

## 7. What You Can (and Can't) Say

**You can say:**

"In 248 patients with depression randomised to sertraline or placebo and scanned before treatment and one week later, a model could identify which scan came first with 94.4% accuracy in the placebo arm and 91.1% in the sertraline arm, with highly similar brain patterns in both arms (cosine similarity 0.813)."

"The pattern comprised 28 regions and 60 connections spanning visual cortex, precuneus and thalamus, all of which increased, and models trained on untreated healthy controls' scans performed at chance (48.3% and 41.4%)."

"Early connectivity change did not significantly distinguish the sertraline arm from the placebo arm, did not significantly predict sertraline response, and did not predict an estimated drug-specific component of response in three separate attempts."

"A variance-contrast analysis identified a sertraline-specific connectivity dimension involving amygdala, midcingulate, orbitofrontal cortex and cerebellum (F = 1.58, P = 0.005; permutation P = 0.010), with a prespecified reverse analysis using placebo as foreground finding no comparable dimension."

"Forty-seven of 123 sertraline-treated patients (38%) showed this pattern; the remaining 76 did not, and in that subgroup a placebo-response model predicted improvement (R² = 0.057, P = 0.032)."

"Incorporating a test–retest reliability constraint into the learning objective improved the generalizability of the identified dimension, and the authors recommend that future resting-state fMRI studies adopt signal-to-noise-aware objectives."

"The authors state they could not determine whether sertraline and escitalopram have distinct early drug-specific effects, because the escitalopram cohort had no placebo arm."

"The parent trial's registered Aim 2, recorded in 2011, was to identify early week-1 neuroimaging changes as mediators of differential sertraline-versus-placebo outcome."

**You cannot say:**

"The study shows antidepressants and placebo produce different early brain changes." **The direct test of that failed** — early connectivity change could not distinguish the arms. The drug-specific finding comes from a second, variance-based analysis run after the first came out null.

"Brain changes in the first week mediate placebo improvement." **Nothing here tests mediation.** The association is r = 0.193, about 3.7% of variance, and the prediction model reaches R² = 0.113.

"The brain change matters for placebo but not for the drug." **r = 0.193 against r = 0.154 — testing the difference gives P = 0.75.** The two are indistinguishable.

"Repeated scanning has been ruled out as the explanation." **Ruled out as a large effect, on 29 controls, where the chance range spans roughly 32% to 68%.** A moderate scanning effect would look just like this result.

"The findings were replicated in 386 patients across two independent cohorts." **Only 248 had a placebo comparison.** The escitalopram cohort has no placebo arm, a different drug, a two-week rather than one-week interval, different scales and different scanner resolution — and the paper says the prediction tasks could not be replicated in it.

"Cross-cohort correlations of 0.5 to 0.69 validate the clinical findings." **Both scores in each correlation are different weighted sums of the same scans of the same people.** This checks where the weights came from, not whether a clinical claim holds on new data.

"About 40% of people on sertraline get a real drug effect and 60% are responding to placebo." **These are study-defined subgroups from a threshold anchored to the single most extreme placebo score**, and the clinical comparison between them is P = 0.037 **one-tailed** (0.074 two-tailed).

"This could be used to decide who should take an antidepressant." **Not demonstrated, and the paper does not claim it** — it calls for *"clinical trials... to test the utility of drug-specific subgrouping."*

"Sertraline barely beats placebo because most patients are placebo responders." **The paper suggests this; its direct tests of drug-specific response prediction all failed**, and the subgroup difference supporting it is one-tailed and marginal.

"The patterns generalise across antidepressants." **The escitalopram dimension is about 10.6× weaker in F than the placebo-derived one (1.57 against 16.7)**, a gap the paper does not address.

**The honest one-sentence version:** *the strongest methodological work this reading list has assessed — a question verifiably prespecified in a 2011 registry, measurement reliability written into the model's objective, four substantial nulls reported including the failure of the analysis readers most want, and a falsification test declared in advance and passed — showing that something large and highly consistent happens in the brain during the first week of treatment and happens just as much on a dummy pill, while the clinically interesting extension, that a minority of patients show a genuine drug signature, rests on three threshold-grazing results in subgroups defined by a cutoff anchored to a single observation.*

## 8. Bottom Line for Your Life

**If you take an antidepressant, or are deciding whether to:** nothing here changes what you should do, and the authors say explicitly that clinical trials are still needed before any of this guides treatment. **But there is one finding worth carrying, and it is not the one in the headline.** In the first week, **something real and strikingly consistent happens in the brain of almost everyone who starts treatment — and it happens just as much in the people taking a dummy pill.** That is not a debunking of antidepressants. **It is evidence that the improvement people feel early on is accompanied by genuine, measurable brain change whether or not the pill is active** — which is a reason to take early improvement seriously as real, and also a reason not to read "I felt better within days" as proof that this particular drug is the right one for you. **The paper's own framing is worth quoting: the placebo effect "is both real and stable."**

**If you are waiting to see whether a new antidepressant is working:** the most useful thing in this paper may be a negative. **Early brain changes could not predict who would respond to sertraline** — not with sophisticated methods on a well-collected dataset. **The honest state of the science is that nobody can yet tell you in week one whether your medication will work**, and anyone who claims otherwise is ahead of the evidence. The paper's own limitations paragraph says this first.

**If you work with brain imaging, or any noisy measurement, copy one thing from this paper.** **It wrote the reliability of its own measurement into the model's objective.** Because every person was scanned twice at each time point, the authors could require that any pattern the model uses must give the same answer across the two runs — so a pattern that flickers between scans minutes apart was rejected before it could ever be reported as a treatment effect. **And they showed it improved their results.** **This reading list has raised measurement reliability against paper after paper. This is the first paper to solve it by construction, and its closing recommendation — that the field adopt signal-to-noise-aware objectives — is the right one.**

**The reading skill from this entry, and it will serve you constantly.** When a paper says an effect was **significant in one group but not another**, and concludes the effect is specific to the first group — **write down the two effect sizes and compare them.** Here they were **0.193 and 0.154**, and testing the difference directly gave **P = 0.75**: indistinguishable. One crossed an arbitrary line and the other didn't. **This pattern is everywhere — subgroup analyses, sex differences, responder-versus-non-responder comparisons — and it is almost always wrong.** The question to ask is never "was it significant in each group?" but **"did they test whether the groups differ?"**

**A second one, about reassurances.** Whenever a study rules something out — a confounder, an alternative explanation, a difference between groups — **check the sample size of the thing doing the ruling out.** Here a headline computed on 248 patients rests on a control group of **29 people**, in which anything up to about 68% accuracy would have looked like chance. **A moderate version of the alternative explanation would have been invisible.** And 87 more controls were sitting in the same paper, used for something else. **"We tested it and found nothing" and "we could have detected it" are different claims, and only the second one settles anything.**

**And the practice this entry adds for the first time: when a paper names a trial registration number, look it up.** It takes one search. **Here it paid off in the paper's favour** — the registry shows the research question was written down in 2011, which no checklist or author statement could have established. **It also showed what the paper did not say:** that the registration covers only one of the two cohorts, and that the second cohort's own registration is never given. **A registry entry is the only part of a study written before the results were known. It is the closest thing the literature has to a receipt.**

**If you are reading the coverage:** expect "AI reveals how antidepressants change the brain" or, with the opposite spin, "brain scans show antidepressants are no better than placebo." **Both overshoot.** What the study shows is that the first week's brain changes are mostly shared between drug and placebo; that a drug-specific pattern is detectable in a minority of patients by an indirect method, after the direct method failed; and that nothing here yet predicts who will get better. **The authors are considerably more careful than the headlines will be.**

**This is education, not medical advice.** Depression is a serious and treatable condition, and antidepressants help many people. **Do not start, stop or change a medication based on anything here** — stopping an antidepressant abruptly can cause withdrawal effects and relapse. Those decisions belong with a clinician.

**(2) "What correction was applied across the connectivity tests, and is the number of tests stated anywhere?"** — **The number of tests is NEVER stated. I computed it: 135 regions (100 Schaefer cortical parcels + 35 subcortical) gives 135 × 134 ÷ 2 = 9,045 connections per scan.** **Most of the multiplicity is handled by a legitimate alternative to correction**: cross-validated prediction with held-out testing, permutation testing, and **L0 sparsity** forcing most weights to exactly zero — so the paper is not running 9,045 univariate tests and cherry-picking. **FDR correction IS applied to the correlational analyses** (reported as P_FDR_ throughout). **But one screen is uncorrected: the CVQ analysis pre-selects features at an "uncorrected one-tailed P value <0.01 from the F-test," which on 9,045 edges would admit about 90 features by chance alone — and the text leaves it ambiguous whether that screen ran inside each cross-validation fold or once on the whole sample.** Since this analysis produces the paper's drug-specific finding, the distinction matters; **this is entry 86's nested-selection question (355) in a new setting, and here it cannot be resolved from the text.**

**(3) "With a placebo arm present, is the headline comparison drug-versus-placebo, or is a within-group pre-post change reported as if it were the treatment effect?"** — **Both comparisons are present, and the paper is unusually clear about which is which. It does NOT commit the error I was watching for; avoiding it is the paper's central intellectual move.** The chronology/VPT finding **is** a within-group before-and-after change — and the paper's response to finding it equally in both arms is to **name it a placebo effect rather than claim it for the drug**. The drug-specific claim uses a genuine **between-arm** contrast (placebo as background). **The paper even devotes a Methods subsection to what the within-group analysis cannot establish:** *"The chronology prediction models are designed to identify the maximally shared FC changes... and are not capable of isolating genuine 'placebo-specific' and 'sertraline-specific' effects."* **Credit in full.**

**The decisive error found today, verified by direct computation.** **The paper infers arm specificity from a difference in significance.** VPT change against 8-week response: **placebo r = 0.193 (P = 0.043), sertraline r = 0.154 (P = 0.124)**, read as *"a potential specificity of the VPT system to pure placebo effect."* **Testing the difference properly — Fisher z on 125 and 123 — gives z₁ = 0.1955, z₂ = 0.1552, SE = 0.1286, z = 0.31, two-sided P = 0.75.** **The two correlations are statistically indistinguishable.** In variance terms they explain **3.7% and 2.4%**. And the margin is tiny on both sides: **at n = 125 the correlation needed for P = 0.05 is about 0.176**, so the placebo arm clears it by 0.017 and the sertraline arm misses by 0.022. **The paper hedges with "potential" and offers a fair mechanistic reason the sertraline arm might mask the effect — but the numbers presented as evidence are not evidence.**

**The second finding: the control that excludes the headline's rival explanation has 29 people.** The paper itself raises repeated scanning — *"and/or changes pertaining to repeated scan sessions"* — and tests it correctly in untreated controls, obtaining **48.3% and 41.4%**. **But with 29 controls the standard error about chance is 9.3 points, so the range consistent with pure guessing spans roughly 32% to 68% — meaning an accuracy of 65%, a substantial scanning effect, would have fallen inside "chance."** The paper concludes a *"high likelihood"* the signal is not scan-related. **And the fix is in the same paper: CAN-BIND-1 contributed 87 untreated controls, used elsewhere as background data. Pooling would take the control group from 29 to 116 and narrow the chance range to about 41–59%.**
