## 1. The Gist

Chinese university students are stressed. Music therapy helps a bit. Music therapists usually talk over the music — "you are relaxed, your body is becoming heavy" — and those scripts were mostly written in English and translated. The researchers' idea was that a translated script may land badly in a culture that prizes indirect, metaphorical expression, and that a large language model could write a better one.

So they asked ChatGPT, in Chinese, to write about thirty minutes of suggestion script built around Eastern imagery and the Chinese film *Hero*. Experts reviewed and refined it. Then 113 students did a deliberately stressful 40-minute task and were randomly split three ways: the *Hero* soundtrack with the AI-written script; a standard clinical music therapy recording with a conventional script; or Joe Hisaishi piano music with no talking at all.

Here is what happened, and it is not what the title suggests. The outcome the researchers had **registered in advance as their primary measure** — state anxiety — showed no difference between groups at all (p = .511). Negative mood did differ, with the AI-script group lowest. Heart rate also differed — but in the *wrong direction* for the hypothesis: the standard therapy group had the lowest heart rate, and the AI-script group was no better than silent piano music.

And underneath all of it sits a design problem the authors are honest about. The three groups differed in the music they heard, in whether anyone talked, in what was said, and in how long it lasted. So even where a difference appeared, you cannot tell whether it came from the AI-written words, from Tan Dun's soundtrack, or from the four extra minutes.

The authors' own conclusion is the right one: this "does not establish efficacy or the independent effect of culturally adapted verbal suggestions." What the study really demonstrates is that an LLM can draft a culturally specific therapy script that people rate as resonant. Whether it works is untested.

## 2. Study Snapshot

**Design:** Three-arm parallel randomised trial, described in the paper as a "pilot randomized exploratory study." Single session, single centre, no follow-up.

**Where and when:** A university in China. Participants recruited 23–25 January 2026.

**Who:** Chinese undergraduates aged 18–25. 156 screened; 43 excluded before randomisation (4 ineligible, 39 declined or withdrew); **113 randomised and analysed**. 94 female (83%) and 19 male (17%). No one was lost after randomisation, and all four outcomes had complete data — genuinely clean follow-up.

**Exclusions as stated in the paper:** severe hearing impairment and diagnosed cardiovascular conditions. (The registry lists more; see The Editor's Concerns.)

**Randomisation:** computer-generated **simple** randomisation — not blocked — which is why the arms came out uneven at 36 / 38 / 39.

**The stressor:** a 40-minute time-pressured task building a mind map of course content. The pre-test was taken **after** this task, so "baseline" here means "at the stress peak," not "at rest." The authors note they never verified that the task actually induced stress.

**The three arms:**
- **Group 1 (n = 36), "Eastern imagery":** the *Hero* (2002) soundtrack by Tan Dun, seven sections, **26 min 19 s**, with a script generated by **ChatGPT 5.2** from two Chinese-language prompts and then refined over several rounds by the research team, including a licensed music therapist and researchers in Chinese aesthetics. Sample line: "Mountains and rivers lie in stillness. In this moment, all things are with you."
- **Group 2 (n = 38), "standard therapy":** a clinical music therapy recording from a textbook by Professor Tian Gao, **29 min 56 s**, conventional neutral script with no cultural references. Sample line: "Imagine a glowing, rotating sphere in front of you…"
- **Group 3 (n = 39), "music only":** Joe Hisaishi's soundtrack for *Kikujiro* (1999), eight instrumental tracks, **25 min 35 s**, no talking at all.

**Measures, taken immediately before and immediately after:**
- State anxiety (STAI-S, 20 items; Cronbach's α 0.85 pre, 0.84 post)
- Positive affect (PANAS, 10 items; α 0.93 pre, 0.95 post)
- Negative affect (PANAS, 10 items; α 0.87 pre, 0.90 post)
- Heart rate, by **smartphone-camera photoplethysmography** using the "JingDong Health" app, roughly one minute per reading
- Trait anxiety (STAI-T) at pre-test only, α 0.85

**Analysis:** ANCOVA on each post-intervention score with group as the factor and the pre-intervention score as covariate. Homogeneity of regression slopes tested, Levene tests run, HC3 robust standard errors as a check, outlier sensitivity analyses, and Holm correction across the three pairwise comparisons within each significant outcome. This is a conscientious analysis plan.

**Results (adjusted group effects):**
- **State anxiety: F(2, 109) = 0.68, p = .511, ηp² = .012 — no difference.** This was the registered primary outcome.
- Positive affect: F(2, 109) = 1.03, p = .360, ηp² = .019 — no difference.
- **Negative affect: F(2, 109) = 4.60, p = .012, ηp² = .078.** Eastern imagery lower than standard therapy (difference −0.42, 95% CI −0.73 to −0.11, Holm p = .026) and lower than music only (−0.40, 95% CI −0.70 to −0.10, Holm p = .026). Standard therapy and music only did not differ (p = .893).
- **Heart rate: F(2, 109) = 10.56, p < .001, ηp² = .162.** Standard therapy lower than Eastern imagery (−3.01 bpm, 95% CI −4.76 to −1.26, Holm p = .002) and lower than music only (−3.76 bpm, 95% CI −5.47 to −2.06, Holm p < .001). **Eastern imagery and music only did not differ (p = .388).**
- All three groups' heart rates fell: 82.81 → 78.61, 81.11 → 74.95, 81.92 → 79.03 bpm.
- Cultural resonance, asked of Group 1 only: rated culturally relevant 5.03 (SD 1.18) and personally resonant 5.23 (SD 1.03) on 7-point scales, both above the midpoint at p < .001.
- Trait anxiety subgroup within Group 1: 29 high vs 7 low, no significant differences. The authors say outright this should not be read as evidence of broad applicability.

**Ethics and registration:** approved by the Institutional Review Board of Universiti Sains Malaysia, Aba Teachers College. Registered at ClinicalTrials.gov as **NCT07359950** ("EAST-MUSIC"). Participants were compensated with a gift worth 20 Chinese Yuan.

**Funding and conflicts of interest:** not stated in the text retrieved.

## 3. How Strong Is This Evidence? (Grade 2/5)

Two out of five. The statistical craft is better than average; the design cannot answer the question in the title; and the registry shows the headline is not the outcome the study set out to measure.

**What is genuinely good here.** Randomisation was real, every randomised participant was analysed, and no data were missing — that is a cleaner dataset than most trials deliver. The questionnaires are standard and their reliability was reported and good (every Cronbach's α between 0.84 and 0.95). The analysis plan is careful in a way that suggests someone thought about it: ANCOVA rather than crude change scores, the homogeneity-of-slopes assumption actually tested rather than assumed, Levene tests, HC3 robust standard errors as a cross-check when residual normality failed, an outlier sensitivity analysis with a pre-stated rule, and **Holm correction correctly applied** to the pairwise comparisons. I reproduced every partial eta squared from the reported F statistics and every Holm-adjusted p-value from the reported confidence intervals; they all check out exactly. That is the signature of real analysis rather than decoration.

The authors are also candid in the right places. They state that conditions differed in musical material and duration, that "the independent effects of the LLM-generated, culturally adapted suggestions could not be isolated," that the negative-affect result "cannot be attributed specifically to cultural adaptation or LLM generation," and that the trait-anxiety null "should not be interpreted as evidence that the intervention is broadly applicable." They flag that the stress induction was never verified. They recommend the correct fix — identical music across arms, varying only the words.

**What pulls it down to 2/5.**

First and largest: **the design confounds at least three things at once.** Group 1 differs from Group 3 in music *and* in whether anyone spoke. Group 1 differs from Group 2 in music *and* in what was said *and* in four and a half minutes of duration. There is no arm anywhere in this study that isolates the AI-written script. The title names "LLM-generated culturally adapted verbal suggestions" as the thing being integrated and studied; the design never varies that one ingredient on its own. The authors acknowledge this, but acknowledgement does not create the missing arm.

Second: **the registered primary outcome was null, and the paper leads with a secondary one.** I retrieved the ClinicalTrials.gov record. It names a single primary outcome — change in state anxiety — and lists positive affect, negative affect and heart rate as secondary. State anxiety came back at p = .511. The abstract's one positive sentence is about negative affect, a secondary outcome, and nowhere does the paper tell the reader that the registered primary measure was the one that found nothing. A reader who never opens the registry would reasonably come away thinking the study found what it went looking for.

Third: **several other registry-versus-paper mismatches**, none fatal alone, cumulative in effect. The registry lists enrolment of 123; the paper analyses 113. The registry defines every outcome as a **change score**; the paper analyses baseline-adjusted post scores (a better choice statistically, but a change from the plan and not flagged as one). The registry's exclusion criteria include diagnosed psychiatric disorders, psychotropic medication and participation in other studies in the previous 30 days; the paper's stated exclusions are only hearing impairment and cardiovascular disease. The registry gives a study start of 1 October 2025; the paper says recruitment ran 23–25 January 2026. And the registry calls this "A Randomized Controlled Trial" while the paper calls it "a pilot randomized exploratory study" — a softening that arrives after a null primary outcome.

Fourth: **the physiological result contradicts the hypothesis and the paper does not treat this as a problem.** If culturally adapted AI language is the active ingredient, the Eastern imagery group should have shown the greatest calming. It showed the *least* of the three on heart rate — statistically indistinguishable from piano music with nobody talking, and significantly worse than the standard therapy recording. The discussion explains this away by crediting the standard protocol's guided breathing, which is plausible, but it is also an admission that a different ingredient entirely was doing the work.

Fifth: **the null results are uninformative rather than reassuring.** With 113 participants across three arms, the study had roughly 80% power to detect a partial eta squared of about .081 — a medium effect. The state-anxiety result (.012) and the positive-affect result (.019) sit far below that floor. These are not findings of no difference; they are "we could not have seen a difference this size." Achieved power for the state-anxiety effect was about 16%.

**Smaller problems.** No a priori power calculation is reported, only a retrospective assertion that the sample "was adequate to detect moderate effects." The sample is 83% female. Heart rate was measured with a phone camera; the differences being interpreted are 3 to 4 beats per minute. The cultural-resonance questions were asked of Group 1 only, which the authors correctly refuse to call a manipulation check — but it means the single piece of evidence that cultural adaptation worked has no comparison. Trait anxiety was measured after the stress task, so a supposedly stable trait was assessed in a stressed state. And in the state-anxiety sensitivity analysis, the homogeneity-of-slopes assumption **failed** (p = .010), which the authors flag but which means the adjusted comparison in that model is on shaky ground.

**Where 2/5 sits.** The arithmetic is sound and the hedging is appropriate. But the study cannot support any claim about AI-generated cultural adaptation, its registered primary question came back empty, and its physiological result points away from its hypothesis. As a demonstration that an LLM can draft a therapy script people find resonant, it works. As evidence that doing so helps, it does not.

## 4. The Editor's Concerns

**The arm that isn't there.** To test whether an AI-written culturally adapted script helps, you need two conditions identical in every respect except the script. Same music, same duration, same delivery; one gets the AI script, one gets the conventional script. That comparison does not exist in this study. The authors propose exactly this for future work, which tells me they know. I would ask for the title and abstract to be rewritten to reflect what was actually compared: three different multi-component packages, not one ingredient.

**Tell the reader which outcome was registered.** This is my main request. A paper with a registration should state the registered primary outcome and report it as primary. Here the registered primary — state anxiety — is reported third in the results and never identified as primary, while a secondary outcome carries the abstract. Readers rely on registration precisely so that the outcome order cannot shift after the data arrive. The fix costs one sentence.

**Reconcile 123 and 113.** The registry says 123 enrolled; the paper's CONSORT flow says 156 screened, 43 excluded, 113 randomised. The number 123 appears nowhere in the paper. This may be a stale registry field, but a reader cannot tell, and the paper should say.

**Flag the analysis change.** Registering an outcome as "change from before to after" and then analysing baseline-adjusted post scores is an *upgrade* — ANCOVA is the better method (see the Statistics Spotlight). But a deviation from a registered analysis plan, even an improvement, should be declared, with both results ideally shown.

**Match the eligibility criteria.** The paper lists two exclusions; the registry lists five, including diagnosed psychiatric disorders and current psychotropic medication. Those are not minor — they change who the sample represents. If they were applied, the paper should say so; if they were not, the registry should be corrected.

**A 4.3-minute difference in exposure is not nothing.** The arms ran 26.3, 29.9 and 25.6 minutes — the longest is 17% longer than the shortest. In an intervention whose mechanism is sustained relaxation, dose is part of the treatment. The arm with the longest exposure is the one that won on heart rate.

**Verify the stressor.** The whole design assumes a 40-minute mind-mapping task raised anxiety. Nobody measured anxiety before it. If the task did not actually stress people, the "baseline" is just a baseline, and the universal post-session decline in heart rate across all three arms could be nothing more than sitting still for 25 minutes. The authors raise this themselves and propose the right fix.

**Measurement precision versus the effects being read.** Smartphone photoplethysmography estimates pulse from colour changes at a fingertip through a phone camera. It is reasonable for resting heart rate, but the paper is interpreting differences of 3.01 and 3.76 bpm as meaningful. The paper cites prior work supporting the method's reliability but reports no agreement statistics in this sample. Given the honest statement that readings were taken "immediately after" a session where participants had their eyes closed, small differences in how quickly each group was roused could move the number by this much.

**Blinding.** Participants were not told the hypotheses or the group differences, which is good. But the arms are obviously different from the inside — one has Chinese imagery narration, one has generic narration, one has silence — so nobody was meaningfully blind. Three of the four outcomes are self-reported. The single blind-ish outcome, heart rate, is the one that did not favour the hypothesis.

**One unverifiable thing worth noting.** The paper says the LLM script was generated by "ChatGPT 5.2" with two specific Chinese-language prompts, then revised over multiple expert rounds. Because the revision was substantial and the final script sits in an appendix I could not retrieve, how much of the delivered script is the model's output and how much is the research team's rewriting is not determinable from the text available. That matters for a paper about what an LLM contributes.

## 5. Statistics Spotlight

### Concept 1: What is the comparison actually isolating?

This is the load-bearing idea, and it is not really a formula — it is a question you ask before you look at any number.

Randomisation is a tool for making groups comparable at the start. It does this beautifully. What it cannot do is make the *treatments* comparable. If the thing you give group A differs from the thing you give group B in four ways, randomisation guarantees only that the people were alike. Any difference in outcome is attributable to the whole bundle, and to no particular item in it.

Lay the bundles side by side:

- Group 1: Tan Dun's *Hero* soundtrack, AI-written Eastern-imagery narration, 26 min 19 s
- Group 2: a clinical therapy recording, conventional neutral narration, 29 min 56 s
- Group 3: Joe Hisaishi's *Kikujiro* soundtrack, no narration, 25 min 35 s

Compare Group 1 to Group 3 and you have changed the music *and* added narration. Compare Group 1 to Group 2 and you have changed the music *and* the narration style *and* added almost four minutes. Nowhere do two arms differ in the script alone.

A kitchen analogy. You want to know whether a new spice improves a soup. You make three soups: the new spice in a tomato base simmered 26 minutes; an old spice in a chicken base simmered 30 minutes; and a mushroom base with no spice simmered 25 minutes. Tasters prefer the first. Was it the spice? The tomatoes? The simmer time? You cannot say, because you changed everything at once. The randomisation of tasters to bowls was impeccable and entirely beside the point.

**The fix** is a factorial design: hold the music fixed and vary only the script — ideally crossing both factors, so you could also see whether the AI script works better with some music than others. The authors recommend exactly this.

**Watch out for this in the wild.** It is the single most common flaw in digital health and AI intervention papers. A study gives one group "the AI-powered platform" and the other "usual care," finds a difference, and reports it as evidence the AI works — when the AI platform also came with reminders, a nicer interface, a dedicated coordinator and more contact time. Ask every time: *what is the single thing that differs between these arms?* If the answer is "several things," no result can be attributed to any one of them.

There is a second lesson hidden in this paper's results. **Look at whether the different outcomes point the same way.** Negative affect favoured the AI-script arm. Heart rate favoured the standard-therapy arm and showed the AI-script arm no better than silence. When a single supposed mechanism produces opposite rankings on two outcomes, the simplest explanation is usually that different ingredients in the bundles are driving different outcomes — which is precisely what you would expect from a confounded design.

### Concept 2: ANCOVA — adjusting for where people started

Everyone's anxiety was measured before and after. The obvious analysis is to subtract: compute each person's change and compare the three groups' average changes. The paper does something better.

**What ANCOVA does.** Analysis of covariance takes the *post* score as the outcome and puts the *pre* score in as a covariate — a predictor whose influence is removed before groups are compared. The output is a set of **adjusted means**: what each group's post score would have been had every group started from the same average baseline.

In this paper the raw baseline negative-affect scores were 2.03, 2.42 and 2.19 — not identical, because randomisation makes groups similar on average, not identical in any one run. The adjusted post-intervention means (1.57, 1.99, 1.97) answer "where did each group end up, correcting for the fact that they did not all start in the same place?"

**Why it beats change scores.** Two reasons, one obvious and one not.

The obvious one: fairness. A group that happened to start worse has more room to improve. Subtracting rewards them for it; ANCOVA does not.

The subtle one: **precision**. A change score carries the measurement noise of *two* measurements — the before and the after — so it is noisier than either one alone. ANCOVA uses the baseline as a predictor rather than subtracting it, which removes the part of the post score the baseline explains without importing the baseline's full noise. In a randomised trial, ANCOVA is almost always the more powerful analysis. This is well established and it is why regulators generally prefer it.

**A worked intuition.** Imagine measuring everyone's height before and after a week, with a tape measure that is off by a centimetre at random each time. Change scores would be almost entirely noise — two errors stacked on a real change of nearly zero. Using the first measurement to predict the second instead lets you keep the signal without doubling the error.

**The assumption that must hold: homogeneity of regression slopes.** ANCOVA assumes the relationship between baseline and outcome is the same shape in every group — the lines are parallel, just at different heights. If one group's line is steeper, a single adjusted comparison is meaningless, because the group difference depends on where you are along the baseline.

The authors tested this properly, by checking the group × baseline interaction. In the main models it was non-significant (p = .113 to .709) — the assumption holds. **But in the state-anxiety sensitivity analysis, after removing two outliers, the interaction became significant (p = .010)**, and they correctly warn that the common-slope estimates there should be read cautiously. That is exactly the right thing to do, and most papers never check at all.

**Watch out.** ANCOVA is the right tool when baseline differences arose by chance, as in a randomised trial. It is a trap in observational studies where the groups differ systematically — there, "adjusting for baseline" can reverse an apparent effect or manufacture one. This is the classic puzzle known as Lord's paradox. The reason ANCOVA is safe here is the randomisation, not the method.

### Concept 3: Partial eta squared, and the Holm step that explains two identical p-values

**Partial eta squared (ηp²)** is the effect size for an ANOVA or ANCOVA: the share of variation in the outcome attributable to the group difference, after accounting for everything else in the model. It runs from 0 to 1. Cohen's rough benchmarks: .01 small, .06 medium, .14 large.

You can compute it straight from the F statistic:

ηp² = (df₁ × F) / (df₁ × F + df₂)

Check it on this paper's negative-affect result: (2 × 4.60) / (2 × 4.60 + 109) = 9.20 / 118.20 = **.078**, exactly as reported. All four of the paper's values reproduce this way, as does the sensitivity analysis. The numbers are internally consistent.

So the study's effects were: negative affect .078 (medium), heart rate .162 (large), positive affect .019 and state anxiety .012 (both below small).

**Now the thing those last two numbers hide.** With 113 participants in three arms and one covariate, this design had roughly 80% power to detect a ηp² of about **.081** — just past the medium mark. Achieved power for the observed state-anxiety effect of .012 was about **16%**. So "no difference in state anxiety" means *this study could not have detected a small or moderate difference in state anxiety even if one existed*. It is not evidence of equivalence; it is absence of evidence. A paper reporting a null is making a claim about its own sensitivity, and the honest version of that claim is always "we could rule out effects larger than X."

**Watch out with ηp².** It is not comparable across studies with different designs, because the "partial" means other factors have been removed from the denominator — add covariates and ηp² grows for the same underlying effect. Never compare a ηp² from a one-way design with one from a design containing three covariates.

**And now the Holm detail, which is quietly instructive.** Both significant pairwise comparisons for negative affect report *exactly* the same adjusted p-value, .026. That looks like a typo. It is not.

Holm's procedure works step-down. Order the raw p-values smallest first, multiply the smallest by the number of tests, the next by one fewer, and so on. From the reported confidence intervals I recovered the raw values: .0084, .0094 and .895.

- Smallest, .0084 × 3 = .0251
- Next, .0094 × 2 = .0188
- Largest, .895 × 1 = .895

But .0188 is *smaller* than .0251, and that would be nonsense — a weaker result cannot come out more significant than a stronger one after correction. So Holm enforces **monotonicity**: each adjusted p-value is raised to at least the previous one. The second becomes .0251 as well. Both round to **.026**, precisely as the paper reports.

I reproduced the heart-rate adjustments the same way (.0001, .0018, .3845 against the paper's "<.001", .002, .388). The corrections were done correctly.

**Watch out.** Holm is uniformly better than Bonferroni — same guarantee against false positives, more power — so there is no reason to use plain Bonferroni any more. But note what it was applied to *here*: the three pairwise comparisons *within* each outcome. It was **not** applied across the four outcomes. The paper tested state anxiety, positive affect, negative affect and heart rate, and corrected within each but not between them. Correcting within a family and not across families is a defensible convention, but it means the headline "p = .012 for negative affect" has not been adjusted for the fact that four outcomes were examined. Correct across all four by Holm and the negative-affect result (.012 × 3 = .036 at its step) would still clear .05 — so the conclusion survives — but the reader should know which family the correction covered.

## 6. Jargon Translator

**ANCOVA (analysis of covariance)** — Comparing groups on an outcome while mathematically accounting for a variable measured beforehand, usually the baseline value of the same outcome.

**Covariate** — The variable being adjusted for. Here, each person's pre-intervention score.

**Adjusted mean** — What a group's average outcome would have been if every group had started from the same baseline.

**Homogeneity of regression slopes** — The ANCOVA assumption that the baseline-to-outcome relationship has the same strength in every group. Tested by checking whether group and baseline interact.

**Partial eta squared (ηp²)** — The share of outcome variation attributable to group differences in an ANOVA/ANCOVA. Benchmarks: .01 small, .06 medium, .14 large.

**F statistic, and df₁ / df₂** — F is the ratio of variation between groups to variation within them; bigger means a clearer group difference. The two degrees-of-freedom numbers describe the test's size: here df₁ = 2 (three groups minus one) and df₂ = 109 (113 people minus three groups minus one covariate).

**Holm correction** — A step-down method for adjusting p-values when several comparisons are made, so that testing many things does not manufacture a false positive. Strictly better than Bonferroni.

**Monotonicity (in Holm)** — The rule that an adjusted p-value is never allowed to fall below the one before it. This is why two of this paper's comparisons report the same .026.

**HC3 robust standard errors** — A way of computing uncertainty that stays valid when the spread of the data differs across groups. Used here as a cross-check; it gave the same conclusions.

**Levene's test** — Checks whether the spread of values is similar across groups.

**Shapiro–Wilk test** — Checks whether data (here, the model's residuals) follow a normal distribution.

**Residuals** — The leftovers: how far each person's actual value sits from what the model predicted. ANCOVA's normality assumption is about these, not about the raw data — which is why the authors were right to test residuals rather than the skewed raw heart rates.

**Externally studentized residual** — A standardized measure of how far one observation sits from what the model predicts when that observation is left out of the fitting. Values beyond ±3 are conventionally flagged as potential outliers.

**Simple randomisation** — Assigning each person independently, like a coin flip. Unbiased, but leaves group sizes uneven (36/38/39 here). **Block randomisation** forces balance.

**STAI-S and STAI-T** — State and Trait subscales of the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory. State is how anxious you feel right now; Trait is how anxious you generally are. 20 items each.

**PANAS** — Positive and Negative Affect Schedule. Two independent 10-item scales; you can be high on both at once.

**Cronbach's α** — Whether the items on a scale hang together as a measure of one thing. Above 0.8 is good. Every scale here was 0.84 or better.

**Photoplethysmography (PPG)** — Estimating pulse by detecting tiny colour changes in the skin as blood pulses through it. A smartphone does this with its camera and flash on a fingertip.

**Verbal suggestion** — In music therapy, the spoken guidance layered over the music: imagery, breathing cues, body-awareness prompts.

**Manipulation check** — A measurement confirming the intervention did what you intended. The authors correctly decline to call their cultural-resonance question one, because only one group answered it.

**Power** — The probability of detecting an effect of a given size if it is really there. Conventionally 80%.

**CONSORT** — The standard reporting checklist for randomised trials, including the participant flow diagram.

**Pilot / exploratory study** — A small study meant to assess feasibility and generate hypotheses, not to establish whether a treatment works.

## 7. What You Can (and Can't) Say

**You CAN say:**
- "A large language model can draft a culturally specific music therapy script, and in one Chinese student sample the people who received it rated it as culturally relevant and personally meaningful."
- "In a three-arm randomised study of 113 Chinese undergraduates, the group receiving AI-written Eastern-imagery narration over Chinese film music reported lower negative mood afterwards than the two comparison groups."
- "Heart rate fell in all three groups, with the largest drop in the standard music therapy condition — not the AI condition."
- "No difference was found between groups in state anxiety or positive affect."
- "The study was registered, used complete data with no dropout, and applied careful statistical checks."
- "The authors state that the independent effect of the AI-generated suggestions could not be isolated."

**You CAN'T say:**
- ❌ "AI-generated culturally adapted therapy scripts reduce negative emotion." The arms differed in music, in narration and in duration. Nothing isolates the script.
- ❌ "AI beat standard music therapy." On the registered primary outcome there was no difference; on heart rate, standard therapy beat the AI condition.
- ❌ "This study found AI-adapted music therapy reduces anxiety." Anxiety — the registered primary outcome — showed p = .511.
- ❌ "The null results show the conditions are equivalent." The study could only detect roughly medium-or-larger effects; achieved power for the observed anxiety effect was about 16%.
- ❌ "Cultural adaptation was validated." The cultural-resonance rating was collected from one group only, with nothing to compare it against. The authors say explicitly that it is not a manipulation check.
- ❌ "This shows AI can replace a therapist's judgement." The script was revised by the research team over multiple rounds, including a licensed music therapist, and the authors write that human review "remains essential because cultural appropriateness, clinical safety, and therapeutic fit cannot be assumed from model output alone."
- ❌ "The effect would hold for students generally." 83% female, one Chinese university, one 25–30 minute session, no follow-up of any kind.
- ❌ "Music therapy lowers heart rate by about 4 beats per minute." All groups fell, including the one that just listened to piano music, and there was no no-music control at all — so some or all of the drop may simply be what happens when you sit quietly for 25 minutes after a stressful task.

## 8. Bottom Line for Your Life

If you are a student who finds that putting on a film soundtrack and sitting still for twenty-five minutes after a brutal study session makes you feel better: that is real, it is free, and nothing in this paper argues against it. What the paper cannot tell you is whether an AI-written narration adds anything on top, because it never tested that on its own.

If you work in mental health and are wondering whether to have an LLM draft culturally adapted materials: the honest read is that the model produced something a research team could refine into a script participants found resonant. That is a real and useful finding about *generation*. It is not a finding about *efficacy*, and the authors do not claim it is. Their own note is the one to carry: human review is essential, because cultural appropriateness, clinical safety and therapeutic fit cannot be assumed from model output.

For your paper-reading, this one teaches two habits worth more than the study itself.

**First: when a paper has a registration number, open it.** It takes a minute. Here the registry showed that the outcome the study was designed around — state anxiety — came back null, and that the sentence carrying the abstract concerns a secondary outcome. Nothing in the paper is false; the shift is simply never mentioned. The registry is the only place a reader can see the question as it was asked before anyone saw the data. This is the single highest-yield check available to a non-specialist, and it needs no statistics at all.

**Second: before reading any result, ask what single thing differs between the groups.** If the answer is "several things," no result can be pinned on any one of them, however small the p-value. Randomisation makes the *people* comparable; only design makes the *treatments* comparable. That distinction is where an enormous amount of AI-in-healthcare research quietly falls apart, usually in studies that compare an AI-powered package against ordinary care and credit the AI for whatever the whole package did.

A closing word in the authors' favour. They say the confound exists. They say efficacy is not established. They say the trait-anxiety null should not be read as broad applicability. They recommend the exact study that would answer the question. Those statements are why this is a 2/5 rather than a 1/5 — and why it is still worth reading. The gap between a paper's own careful words and the headline a casual reader takes away is, very often, the whole story.
