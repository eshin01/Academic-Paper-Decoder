## 1. The Gist

**Nineteen researchers across the DRC, the United States, the UK and Malawi took smartphone photographs of 308 children and young adults in Kinshasa — half with sickle cell disease, half without — measured 73 things about each face, and asked whether the disease leaves a measurable mark on facial shape. It does. The honest size of that finding is smaller than the headline, and the most important thing in the paper is a pattern the authors report but do not quite draw a conclusion from.**

Start with why this matters, because the stakes here are different from every other entry in this list.

**Sickle cell disease** is an inherited blood disorder. A mutation in the gene for haemoglobin makes red blood cells stiff and sickle-shaped, so they break apart and jam in small blood vessels. The paper's figures: **about eight million people worldwide**, with **nearly half a million affected births every year**, and more than three-quarters of those affected are of African or South Asian ancestry. In the **Democratic Republic of the Congo** — home to one of the largest sickle cell populations in the world — the paper notes that limited access to newborn screening and confirmatory testing contributes to substantial childhood death.

**And that gap is the point.** In countries with newborn screening, a baby found to have sickle cell disease starts penicillin and gets extra vaccinations, and that alone prevents a large share of early deaths. **Where there is no screening, children are diagnosed when they fall ill — and some die before anyone knows.** So a test needing nothing but a phone would genuinely matter.

**What they did.** From **2019 to 2023** they recruited **154 children and young adults with electrophoresis-confirmed sickle cell disease** from the main specialist blood clinic in Kinshasa, and **154 controls matched one-to-one for age and sex** from the local community. Ages ran **from 5 months to 19 years**. One standardised front-facing smartphone photograph each. Then a researcher **hand-placed 33 anatomical landmarks** on every photograph, and from those points the software computed **13 geometric measurements** (distances and angles) and **60 texture measurements** (local contrast patterns around each landmark) — **73 features in total, all defined in advance.**

**The results.** Of the 73 features, **14 differed between the groups** after correcting for having tested so many. Eight were geometric; six were texture. A machine learning model using **six geometric measurements** achieved an **AUROC of 0.843** (95% CI 0.798–0.887) and an **accuracy of 0.795** (0.750–0.840), with sensitivity 81.2% and specificity 77.9%. The six: the **distance between the inner and outer corners of the eye**, the **angle at the flare of the nostril**, the distance from the **bridge of the nose to the groove above the lip**, from the **inner eye corner to the base of the nose**, from the **base of the nose to the lower lip**, and the **width between the nostrils**.

**The biology is plausible.** Sickle cell disease drives the bone marrow into overdrive, and in the skull that marrow expansion is known to reshape the midface. So midface and nasal measurements are exactly where you would expect to find something.

**Now the first thing I found, and it is the paper's single most important methodological decision — in its favour.**

**They nested the feature selection inside the validation loop, and said so.** This is the trap I wrote down before reading, and it is the commonest fatal error in small-sample studies like this. If you pick your best features by looking at all 308 people and *then* test how well those features work, the test is rigged: every person you are about to "hold out" already helped choose the features. **The paper states plainly that "within each leave-one-out iteration, statistical feature screening and recursive feature elimination were performed using only the training participants; the held-out participant was used only to generate the corresponding out-of-fold prediction."** That is the right way, and most papers that do it never say so.

**But one dial escaped.** The paper also says the number of features was chosen by progressively increasing it "until the AUROC converged." **So *which* features were picked was protected, but *how many* — six — was decided by watching the very score that is reported.** A small leak in an otherwise clean design.

**Second, and this is the finding I am most confident of: the paper's two biggest results are the same result counted twice.**

The top two features by effect size are the **distance between the inner and outer corners of the eye** (reported smaller in sickle cell disease) and the **distance between the two inner eye corners** (reported larger — the only feature in the whole paper that goes the other way).

**Those two cannot move independently.** All horizontal distances were divided by the width between the two *outer* eye corners, to cancel out how big the face is or how close the camera was. But that total width *is* the two eye widths plus the gap between the inner corners. **So once you divide by it, the gap plus twice the eye width must add up to 1.** Always. By arithmetic.

**I checked, and it holds:** in controls, 0.408 + 2 × 0.298 = **1.004**; in sickle cell disease, 0.433 + 2 × 0.285 = **1.003**. Both within four-thousandths of one.

**So there is one finding in the eye region, not two: relatively wider spacing between the eyes and correspondingly narrower eyes.** The model behaved correctly — it took one of the pair and dropped the other. **Table 1 presents them as two separate discoveries, ranked first and second.**

**Third, the measurement was made by hand, and we are not told whether the person doing it knew which children had the disease.** Every geometric feature is a distance between points a human placed with a mouse. **The paper never states that the annotators were blinded.** This matters more than it usually would, because of what I just described: **a small systematic tendency to place the inner eye corners slightly further apart in the photographs of children known to have sickle cell disease would produce exactly the paper's headline result** — spacing up, eye width down — automatically. Blinding is the control for that, and it is unreported.

**Fourth, and this is the clinically decisive pattern.** The paper broke the results down by age, and found the differences were **absent in the youngest children and strongest in the oldest.** The eye-spacing difference **did not appear at all** in the two youngest groups. The inner-corner-to-nose measurement was pronounced **only** in the over-18s. The figure of average faces **left out the youngest group entirely** for want of numbers.

**Put that next to why screening matters.** The lives saved by finding sickle cell disease early are **babies' lives** — penicillin from a few months old, extra vaccines, parents who know what a crisis looks like. **The facial signal is weakest or absent precisely in the age group where detection would change the most, and clearest in young adults who were diagnosed years ago.** The paper reports the age pattern plainly; it does not spell out this consequence.

**In fairness, there is a second reading and it must be stated too.** The youngest group had just **18 children — about nine per side.** At that size you could miss a real difference easily. **So "no difference in infants" is also a statement about having too few infants.**

**Fifth, the control group cannot separate sickle cell disease from being chronically ill.** Cases came from **the specialist blood clinic**; controls came from **the community**. Sickle cell disease causes growth failure and delayed development, and there was **no comparison group of children with some other chronic illness.** So a genuine finding about sickle cell facial shape and a general finding about chronically unwell, undernourished children look identical here. **The paper names the single-site limitation and the fact that controls never had a confirmatory blood test — honestly noting that untested carriers among the controls would make the groups look *more* alike, not less. It does not raise the clinic-versus-community asymmetry.**

**And on how big the effects are: smaller than "14 significant features" sounds.** Of the 14, **only five reach what is conventionally a medium effect**; **nine are small**; and **five sit at a q-value of 0.049** — a hair under the 0.05 cutoff. **Two of the six features the model actually chose are among the weak ones**, with effect sizes of 0.139 and 0.114.

**What this paper is.** A careful, genuinely exploratory study in a population that digital-health research almost always ignores, with **the best-documented consent process in this reading list**, the main statistical trap avoided and documented, and **seven separate statements that this is not a diagnostic tool.** Its real finding is narrower than its table suggests: **one midface measurement pattern, in older children and adults, measured by hand without reported blinding, in cases from a clinic against controls from the street.**

## 2. Study Snapshot

**Study type:** cross-sectional case-control study of facial morphometry with an internally cross-validated classifier. The paper calls it **"an exploratory feasibility analysis."** **No external validation, no longitudinal follow-up, and explicitly not a diagnostic study** — the stated emphasis is "quantitative phenotype characterization and preliminary internal discrimination rather than clinical diagnosis."

**Setting and recruitment.** Kinshasa, DRC, **2019 to 2023**, by **convenience sampling**. **Cases:** recruited at the **Centre de Medecine Mixte et d'Anémie SS (CMMASS)**, described as "the primary specialized care clinic for patients with hematological conditions in Kinshasa," and eligible if **sickle cell disease had been confirmed by gel electrophoresis**. **Controls:** "collected from the local community" and "considered not to have SCD based on assessment by our local clinicians" — **no confirmatory electrophoresis**. **Each participant matched 1:1 to a control by sex and age; "additional matching was not performed."**

**Cohort.** **154 cases and 154 controls, 308 participants and 308 photographs — one image each**, so there is no within-person clustering. **154 males (50%), mean age 11.31 ± 6.35 years; 154 females (50%), mean age 12.51 ± 6.11 years.** All participants recruited in Kinshasa and described as of African descent. **"The hemoglobin type of the confirmed SCD cases was unknown."**

**Age groups, chosen to match developmental phases.** Group 1, 5 months–3 years, **n = 18**; group 2, 3–6 years, **n = 38**; group 3, 6–13 years, **n = 92**; group 4, 13–18 years, **n = 74**; group 5, over 18 years, **n = 86**. **I verified these sum to 308.** **An ambiguity I could not resolve: the abstract gives the age range as "5 months to 19 years," but group 5 is defined as "over 18 years," holds 86 of 308 participants (27.9%) and is described as "young adults" with "fully developed craniofacial features."** Twenty-eight per cent of a cohort inside a single year at the top of a nineteen-year range is arithmetically possible given the reported means and standard deviations, but implies a very lumpy distribution, and **the paper never states the actual maximum age or group 5's upper bound.**

**Image acquisition, described thoroughly.** Front-facing photographs using **mGene** image software on **iOS devices** by trained research personnel. Participants seated or standing, face **10–18 inches from the camera**, obstructions removed, **camera lens at eye level and centred**, with a **neutral expression, eyes open and mouth closed**. Photographs taken under **diffuse ambient lighting** where feasible, avoiding direct sunlight, strong shadows and backlighting. **Two-stage quality control:** research staff reviewed every image immediately after acquisition and repeated it for excessive head rotation or tilt, facial occlusion, motion blur, poor focus, inadequate illumination or incomplete facial boundaries; then the **US-based team ran secondary checks**, asking the in-country team to recapture, or excluding the photograph. **The number excluded is not reported.**

**Feature extraction.** **33 facial landmarks were manually annotated on each image.** These drove a rigid landmark-based registration to a reference face by least-squares point-set alignment, after which images were converted to greyscale and cropped to a standardised **150 × 150 pixels**. From the landmarks the software computed **13 geometric features — 11 distances and 2 angles**. **Normalisation, which matters for Section 4: "horizontal distances were normalized with respect to the distance between the lateral canthi, and vertical distances were normalized to the distance between the lateral canthi and the oral commissures."** It also computed **radiomic-style texture features using multi-resolution local binary patterns**, "sensitive to lines, shadows, and local contrast," averaged across the 13 symmetric landmark pairs, at **three radii (4, 8 and 12 pixels)** — **60 appearance features**, giving **73 features in total, all prespecified**. **"No feature-value imputation was performed. Images that failed landmark detection were excluded before analysis."** Area measures and symmetry indices were not computed.

**Analysis.** Feature filtering by **two-sided Mann-Whitney U test with Benjamini-Hochberg correction for false discovery rate**, excluding features with q above 0.05. The most discriminative of the survivors were selected by **recursive feature elimination** with a **linear-kernel support vector machine**, chosen as "adequate for a small to medium dataset." **"To select the optimal number of features for SCD identification and avoid overfitting, the number of selected features was progressively increased until the AUROC converged."** **And the key sentence: "Model training and validation were performed using leave-one-out cross-validation. Within each leave-one-out iteration, statistical feature screening and recursive feature elimination were performed using only the training participants; the held-out participant was used only to generate the corresponding out-of-fold prediction. Model performance was not interpreted as external or prospective validation."**

**Results — the 14 features.** **Of 73 prespecified features, 14 differed at q < 0.05: eight geometric and six texture.** Seven geometric measures were **smaller** in sickle cell disease: distance between medial and lateral canthi, nasion to philtrum, columella to lower lip, medial canthi to columella, distance between nasal alae, nose length, and the angle at the nasal alae. **Only one was larger: the distance between the medial canthi (q < 0.001).** The six texture descriptors clustered around the nasal alae, nasal root, columella, philtrum and upper lip.

**Effect sizes, from Table 1** (rank-based r = |Z|/√N): distance between medial and lateral canthi **0.402**; distance between medial canthi **0.395**; nasion to philtrum **0.355**; angle at nasal alae **0.351**; nose length **0.303**; columella to lower lip **0.215**; medial canthi to columella **0.139**; then the six texture features at **0.132, 0.131, 0.119, 0.116, 0.114 and 0.112**; and distance between nasal alae **0.114**. **So five of 14 reach r ≥ 0.30, nine fall below 0.22, and eight below 0.14.** **Five features sit at q = 0.049.**

**Results — the model.** Six features, **all geometric**, were "frequently retained," in this order of discriminatory power: distance between medial and lateral canthi, angle at nasal ala, nasion to philtrum, medial canthi to columella, columella to lower lip, distance between nasal alae. **AUROC 0.843 (95% CI 0.798–0.887); accuracy 0.795 (0.750–0.840); sensitivity 0.812 (0.750–0.871); specificity 0.779 (0.707–0.838).** At a classification threshold of 0.5: **PPV 0.788, NPV 0.811, F1 0.803, balanced accuracy 0.799.** **I verified every confidence interval is consistent with its stated denominator** — the accuracy interval is a standard binomial interval on 308, the sensitivity and specificity intervals on 154 each, and the AUROC interval matches a Hanley–McNeil calculation on 154 against 154. **No inflated precision anywhere.**

**And a meaningful negative result: "no appearance features were selected by the ML model in our discriminatory analysis."** The six texture features that differed between groups were all dropped. **The model ran on geometry alone.**

**Results — the age pattern.** The intercanthal distance **did not differ in the two youngest groups**, differed at group 3 (q = 0.034) and was "further accentuated" at groups 4 and 5 (q < 0.001). The nasal alar angle differed in groups 2–5. Nasion to philtrum differed from group 3 onward. Columella to lower lip differed at group 4 (q = 0.031) and group 5 (q < 0.001). **Medial canthi to columella was pronounced only at group 5.** **The distance between nasal alae did not differ by age at all.** The paper states: **"The cross-sectional nature of these differences indicates age-associated differences and not within-person progression."** The composite-faces figure **excluded age group 1 "due to the insufficient sample size."**

**Results — sex.** Of the six selected features, two differed by sex within disease status: medial canthi to columella was smaller in males with sickle cell disease than females with it (q = 0.024), and the nasal alae distance was larger in males than females among controls (q = 0.004). Comparing cases against controls within females, all but two features differed.

**Limitations the authors state.** **Single site**, limiting "representativeness across regions and ancestry." **Cross-sectional**, so growth influences cannot be assessed. **"Clinical factors including hemoglobin genotype, anemia severity, transfusion history and other cumulative complications were not available to researchers."** **"controls were considered non-SCD by local clinicians however did not undergo confirmatory electrophoresis. If trait status influences facial morphology, inclusion among controls, may reduce between group separation."** **"relied on internal cross-validation without external validation."** Possible **cohort and survival effects** confounding any developmental reading. And the ethnic diversity of Kinshasa as a limit on generalisation.

**Not reported or not retrievable:** **funding and conflicts of interest**; **whether the landmark annotators were blinded to case status**; **the number of images excluded** for quality or landmark failure; **the actual maximum age** and group 5's upper bound; **the haemoglobin genotype of any case**; any clinical severity data; any reporting guideline; **S1 Data** with the full feature values; and all figures as images, including the confusion matrix (S1 Fig) and the sex-stratified comparisons (S2 Fig).

## 3. How Strong Is This Evidence? (Grade 3 out of 5)

**A careful exploratory study in a population digital-health research routinely ignores, with the best-documented consent process in this reading list and the central statistical trap both avoided and documented. It earns a 3 rather than a 4 because its headline pair of features is one finding counted twice, its measurement was made by hand without reported blinding, and its control group cannot distinguish sickle cell disease from chronic childhood illness.**

**What is genuinely well done, and some of it is exemplary.**

**The feature selection was nested inside the cross-validation loop, and the paper says so explicitly.** This was the pre-registered question and it is the commonest fatal flaw in studies of this shape — selecting features on all the data and then "validating" on data that helped select them. **The paper's sentence is unambiguous: screening and recursive feature elimination used "only the training participants," with the held-out participant "used only to generate the corresponding out-of-fold prediction."** Most papers that get this right never state it, and most that state it get it wrong.

**The feature set was prespecified** — 73 descriptors defined in advance from an existing published framework — **and multiplicity was handled properly** with Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate control across all 73. With 14 discoveries at q < 0.05 the expected number of false positives is under one. **This is not a fishing expedition; the pond was drawn before the fishing started.**

**Effect sizes are reported for every feature alongside the q-values**, which is why Section 4 can say that most of the significant features are small. A paper reporting only p-values would have concealed that.

**The texture result is a meaningful negative.** Six texture features differed between groups, and **none was selected by the model.** That matters because texture on a facial photograph is where the obvious confounds live — skin tone, the yellowing of the eyes and jaundice that sickle cell disease causes, pallor from anaemia, and differences in lighting or camera. **The model ran on geometry alone, so those confounds do not drive the reported discrimination.** The paper also calls for "standardized color calibration" in future texture work.

**Every confidence interval matches its denominator.** I checked all four: the accuracy interval is a binomial interval on 308, sensitivity and specificity on 154 each, and the AUROC interval reproduces a Hanley–McNeil calculation on 154 against 154. **No precision is claimed that the sample does not support** — in pointed contrast to entry 82, where the interval was eight times wider than a naive one for good reason, and to papers that report the naive one.

**The scope limitation is stated seven separate times** — "exploratory feasibility analysis," "preliminary internal discrimination rather than clinical diagnosis," "not interpreted as external or prospective validation," "does not support standalone clinical use," "not the replacement of established testing nor as a standalone clinical tool," "relied on internal cross-validation without external validation," and the explicit note that cross-sectional age differences are "not within-person progression." **That exceeds entry 81's five-fold declaration, which was the previous best in this list.**

**And the ethics and consent process is the best documented here.** Kinshasa IRB #418; community information sessions with clinic nurses and administrators *before* recruitment; consent in French or Lingala; **verbal consent accepted where literacy prevented written documentation**; participants explicitly told their facial images would be used for research; **every participant given a copy of their consent form**; **a separate, additional authorization required for public release of identifiable images**, obtained individually from everyone in the published composite figure; and the landmark illustration drawn on an **AI-generated face** rather than a participant. **For a study photographing children's faces in a low-resource setting, this is how it should be done, and it deserves saying plainly.**

**The honesty about the control group is real too.** The paper states that controls never had confirmatory electrophoresis and works out the direction of the resulting bias correctly — untested carriers among controls would make the groups **more** alike, so the reported separation is if anything conservative on that axis.

**So why not a 4.**

**The two largest effects are one finding counted twice, and I verified it.** Horizontal distances were normalised by the width between the outer eye corners. But that width *is* the two eye widths plus the inner-corner gap — so after normalisation, **the gap plus twice the eye width must sum to 1 by construction.** In controls: 0.408 + 2 × 0.298 = **1.004**. In cases: 0.433 + 2 × 0.285 = **1.003**. **The paper's first-ranked feature (effect size 0.402) and second-ranked feature (0.395, the only one reversed in direction) are algebraically locked together.** The model handled it correctly by taking one and dropping the other; **Table 1 does not, and presents them as two discoveries.** The honest statement is one midface finding, not two.

**The measurement was hand-placed and blinding is not reported.** All 33 landmarks were **manually annotated**, and every geometric feature is a distance or angle between them. **The paper never says the annotators did not know who had sickle cell disease.** And the specific risk is not hypothetical: **a small systematic tendency to place the inner eye corners slightly wider apart on photographs of known cases would generate precisely the reported pattern** — intercanthal distance up, eye width down — because of the algebraic link above. **The paper's biggest effect and its only direction-reversed effect are the two halves of exactly that artefact.** Blinding is the control; it is unreported.

**The control group cannot isolate sickle cell disease.** Cases came from **the city's specialist haematology clinic**; controls came from **the community**. Sickle cell disease causes growth failure and delayed maturation, and **there is no chronically-ill control group.** So "the facial phenotype of sickle cell disease" and "the facial phenotype of a chronically unwell, undernourished child" are indistinguishable in this design. **The paper names the single-site limitation and the untested controls, but not the clinic-versus-community asymmetry** — which is the more consequential of the two.

**And no clinical data at all.** **"The hemoglobin type of the confirmed SCD cases was unknown,"** so HbSS, HbSC and HbS-β-thalassaemia — which differ greatly in severity — are pooled. Genotype, anaemia severity, transfusion history and complications were all unavailable. **So the paper cannot relate morphology to disease severity, which is the hypothesis its own age findings most invite.**

**One hyperparameter escaped the nesting.** The number of features was chosen by increasing it "until the AUROC converged." **Which features were protected; how many was not** — so the reported 0.843 is a selection score with respect to that one choice.

**The effects are mostly small.** Five of 14 features reach a conventionally medium effect size; **nine are below 0.22 and eight below 0.14**; and **five sit at q = 0.049**, right at the threshold where false-discovery control is least certain. **Two of the six model-selected features are among the weak ones**, at 0.139 and 0.114.

**The signal is weakest where it would matter most** — absent in the two youngest age groups, strongest in the over-18s — which Section 4 takes up.

**A one-patient inconsistency in the reported metrics.** Rebuilding the confusion matrix from the reported figures, **the seven metrics cannot all come from the same table of counts**: sensitivity and accuracy as printed correspond to 125 of 154 cases correctly flagged, while PPV, NPV, F1 and balanced accuracy all correspond to 126. **Immaterial to any conclusion — one patient in 308 — but it is what a check catches.**

**Also: no external validation** (stated repeatedly), **single site, convenience sampling**, **the number of excluded images unreported**, **an unresolved ambiguity about the actual age range**, and **funding and conflicts not retrievable**.

**Why not 2.** Entry 84 scored 2 because its headline was contradicted by its own sensitivity figure, its explanation named a variable with no association to the outcome, its performance table did not reconcile, and its tool was already public. **None of that applies here.** The intervals are honest, the multiplicity is handled, the selection is nested, the texture confound is ruled out by the paper's own negative result, and the scope is limited seven times over. **What is claimed is close to what is shown — and what is claimed is deliberately modest.**

**Why not 4.** The grade tracks what a reader may safely take away. **What a reader may take away is that in one clinic-recruited sample, older children and young adults with sickle cell disease have a measurably different midface shape than community controls, measured by hand without reported blinding, in a design that cannot separate the disease from chronic illness generally.** That is a real contribution to a neglected literature and it is one finding, not fourteen.

**What would make this a 4, concretely.** **State whether the landmark annotators were blinded** — and if not, re-annotate a blinded sample and report the agreement. **Collapse the algebraically dependent features** into the single finding they represent. **Add a chronically-ill control group**, or at minimum report height, weight and nutritional status so chronic illness can be adjusted for. **Report the haemoglobin genotype.** **And report how many images were excluded, by group.** The first, second and fifth need no new data.

## 4. The Editor's Concerns

**1. The feature selection was nested inside the loop, and this deserves the first word.** The pre-registered worry was the classic selection-bias trap: screen features on everyone, then "validate" on people who helped choose them. **The paper states that within each leave-one-out iteration, screening and recursive feature elimination used "only the training participants," with the held-out participant "used only to generate the corresponding out-of-fold prediction."** **That is correct, and stating it is rarer than doing it.** **One caveat and one tension.** The caveat: the number of features was chosen by raising it "until the AUROC converged," so **the feature count was tuned on the reported estimate** even though feature identity was protected. The tension: a separate Methods paragraph describes filtering first ("Features with q > 0.05 were excluded"), and the Results report 14 of 73 significant **across all 308**. **So Table 1's q-values are whole-cohort descriptive statistics while the model used nested screening.** Both are stated; the model paragraph is unambiguous, so I credit the nesting and record that Table 1 is not nested.

**2. The top two features are algebraically dependent — one finding, reported as two.** Horizontal distances were normalised by **the distance between the lateral canthi** (the outer eye corners). But that span *is* the left eye width plus the inner-corner gap plus the right eye width. **So in normalised units, the intercanthal distance plus twice the eye width equals 1, by construction.** Verified: **controls 0.408 + 2 × 0.298 = 1.004; cases 0.433 + 2 × 0.285 = 1.003.** Both within four-thousandths of one — and the tiny shortfall is exactly what you expect when adding **medians** of ratios rather than ratios of medians, which strengthens rather than weakens the point. **These are the paper's first- and second-ranked features by effect size (0.402 and 0.395), and the second is the only feature in the entire study reported in the opposite direction.** **The model handled the collinearity correctly, selecting one and dropping the other. Table 1 does not, listing both as separate discoveries.** The honest statement: **relatively wider eye spacing and correspondingly narrower eyes — one measurement.**

**3. Hand-placed landmarks with no reported blinding, and the risk is specific rather than generic.** **"33 facial landmarks were manually annotated on each image,"** and every geometric feature is a distance or angle between those points. **The paper does not state that annotators were blinded to case status.** Why this is not boilerplate: **the single artefact most likely to arise — placing the inner eye corners slightly wider on photographs of children known to have the disease — produces exactly the paper's headline result**, because of the algebraic lock in concern 2. Intercanthal distance rises, eye width falls, automatically, from one small bias. **The paper's largest effect and its only reversed effect are the two faces of that one possible slip.** Blinding, or a blinded re-annotation with inter-rater agreement, is the control. Neither is reported. **This is entry 317's lesson in a new form: when the measurement is a human judgement, its reliability is the study's precision — and here not even the possibility was addressed.**

**4. The signal is weakest exactly where detection would save lives.** The age-stratified results: the intercanthal difference **absent in groups 1–2**, appearing at group 3 (q = 0.034) and accentuated at 4–5; nasion to philtrum from group 3 onward; columella to lower lip at groups 4–5; **medial canthi to columella pronounced only in the over-18s**; and the nasal alae distance **not differing by age at all**. **The composite-faces figure excluded the youngest group entirely for insufficient numbers.** **Now set that against the clinical reality the paper's own introduction describes.** What newborn screening buys in sickle cell disease is **penicillin prophylaxis, pneumococcal vaccination and parental education, from infancy** — the interventions that prevent early death. **A facial measure that is silent in infancy and loudest in young adults is least informative precisely where a test would change outcomes, and most informative in people diagnosed years earlier.** The paper reports the pattern honestly and does not draw this conclusion. **And the fair counter-reading must be stated: age group 1 had 18 children, about nine per side — so "no difference in infants" is also a statement about having almost no infants** (technique 308).

**5. The control group cannot separate the disease from chronic illness.** Cases came from **CMMASS, "the primary specialized care clinic for patients with hematological conditions in Kinshasa."** Controls were **"collected from the local community."** Sickle cell disease causes growth failure and delayed maturation; **there is no chronically-ill comparison group and no anthropometric data.** So a finding about sickle cell midface morphology and a finding about chronically unwell, undernourished children are indistinguishable. **The paper names two related limitations — single site, and controls lacking confirmatory electrophoresis, with the bias direction correctly worked out — but never the clinic-versus-community asymmetry**, which is the one that threatens the interpretation rather than merely the precision.

**6. No genotype and no clinical data at all.** **"The hemoglobin type of the confirmed SCD cases was unknown."** HbSS, HbSC and HbS-β-thalassaemia carry very different severity, and all are pooled. **"Clinical factors including hemoglobin genotype, anemia severity, transfusion history and other cumulative complications were not available to researchers."** **So the hypothesis the age findings most invite — that facial change tracks cumulative disease burden — cannot be tested at all**, and the paper says as much.

**7. Most of the 14 significant features are small, and five sit at the threshold.** By rank-based effect size: **five of 14 reach r ≥ 0.30; nine fall below 0.22; eight below 0.14.** And **five features carry q = 0.049** — a hair under the cutoff, where false-discovery control is least certain. **Two of the six features the model selected are in that weak group**, at **0.139** and **0.114**. **The paper reports all of this transparently**, which is why it is checkable; the point is that "14 discriminative features" and "a handful of modest midface differences plus a tail of marginal ones" describe the same table.

**8. Texture differed but was never used — a credit worth stating.** Six texture descriptors reached significance, and **"no appearance features were selected by the ML model."** **This rules out the confounds that should worry anyone about facial photographs in this disease**: skin tone, scleral icterus and jaundice, conjunctival pallor from anaemia, and systematic lighting or camera differences between a clinic and a community setting. **The discrimination rests on geometry alone.** The paper also flags the need for colour calibration before texture is taken seriously.

**9. A one-patient inconsistency across the reported metrics.** Rebuilding the confusion matrix from the published figures, **the seven metrics cannot all derive from one set of counts.** Sensitivity (0.812) and accuracy (0.795) correspond to **125 of 154** cases correctly flagged with 120 of 154 controls cleared. But **PPV (0.788), NPV (0.811), F1 (0.803) and balanced accuracy (0.799) all correspond to 126** — and balanced accuracy in particular is fixed by sensitivity and specificity alone, so 0.799 cannot follow from 0.812 and 0.779 on any counts. **Immaterial: one patient in 308, changing nothing.** But it is what rebuilding a table catches (technique 345), and I note it because this list checks.

**10. An unresolved ambiguity about the age range.** The abstract gives **"5 months to 19 years."** But group 5 is **"> 18 years,"** holds **86 of 308 (27.9%)**, and is described as "young adults" with "fully developed craniofacial features." **Twenty-eight per cent of participants inside a single year at the top of a nineteen-year range is arithmetically possible** given the reported means of 11.31 and 12.51 years with standard deviations around 6.2 — I checked — **but it implies a very lumpy distribution, and the paper never states the actual maximum age.** Either the range extends past 19 or group 5 is unusually concentrated; the text does not say which.

**11. Two deployment facts that the paper's framing makes fair but a reader should still know.** First, **the landmarks are hand-placed**, so this is not a phone app a health worker could run — it is a research measurement pipeline with a human in the middle. Second, the study's **1:1 matched design fixes prevalence at 50%**, so its PPV of 0.788 describes no real population — Section 5 works out what it becomes at realistic prevalence. **Neither is a criticism of the paper, which claims neither screening use nor automation, and says so seven times. Both are criticisms of the headline this work will attract.**

**12. Unreported image exclusions.** The quality-control process is described well — immediate in-country review with recapture, then secondary US review — and **"Images that failed landmark detection were excluded before analysis."** **But the number excluded is never given, and nor is its breakdown by group.** If photographs from one group failed more often, that is selection, and it cannot be assessed.

**13. No reporting guideline named, and the three-day sequence now has a fourth case.** **Entry 83 named no guideline and satisfied nearly every TRIPOD item in substance. Entry 84 claimed STROBE and TRIPOD and missed TRIPOD's core items. Entry 85 named none and landed in between.** **Entry 86 names none and is a fourth pattern again:** the prediction-model items TRIPOD cares most about are **partly met** — multiplicity controlled, selection nested, intervals honest, performance fully reported — while **the measurement-validity items are not addressed at all**, since blinding, annotation reliability and exclusion counts go unreported. **Four consecutive entries, four different combinations of claim and substance. The rule holds: the checklist is a claim about intent. Read the methods.**

## 5. Statistics Spotlight

Three concepts, numbered from 355 in `queue.md`. The first is the paper's largest methodological credit; the second is the sharpest finding in this entry.

**Nesting the feature selection inside the resampling loop — what it protects, and the one dial that escaped.**

This study measured **73 things** about each of 308 faces and then asked which handful best separates the groups. **That setup contains a trap so reliable it has a name in the literature, and the paper avoided it.**

**The trap.** Suppose you test all 73 features, keep the 14 that look different, pick the best 6 of those, train a model, and then check how well it does by holding out one person at a time. **It will look good — and the number will be meaningless.** Because the person you "held out" was one of the 308 used to decide which features to keep. **Their data helped choose the question before you asked it of them.** With 73 features and 308 people, you can get a respectable-looking score this way from pure noise.

**What this paper did instead.** **"Within each leave-one-out iteration, statistical feature screening and recursive feature elimination were performed using only the training participants; the held-out participant was used only to generate the corresponding out-of-fold prediction."** So for each of 308 rounds, the whole pipeline — the significance screening *and* the feature elimination — was rebuilt from the other 307, and the held-out person was shown only a model that had never seen them. **That is the correct design, and most papers that do it never say so in a sentence you can check.**

**Why "leave-one-out" and not the usual folds.** With 308 people and only 104... in this case 154 per group, splitting off a 30% test set would leave about 46 people per group to judge by. **Leave-one-out uses every participant as a test case exactly once**, which wrings the most out of a small sample. The cost is that the 308 training sets are nearly identical to each other, so the resulting estimate is **unbiased but noisy** — it bounces around more from sample to sample than a 10-fold estimate would. **For a study this size, that is a reasonable trade.**

**Now the dial that escaped.** The paper also says: **"the number of selected features was progressively increased until the AUROC converged."** **So the identity of the features was protected by the nesting, but the count — six — was chosen by watching the very AUROC that is reported.** That is a small leak: the 0.843 is, with respect to that one choice, the best of several looked at. **The fix is to nest that choice too** — pick the feature count inside each fold, or on a separate inner loop — which costs only computer time.

**The everyday version.** You want to know whether a student has learned the material, so you give them a mock exam, see which topics they did best on, build the real exam out of *those* topics, and then test them. **They will score brilliantly, and you will have learned nothing.** Nesting is the rule that the exam must be written without looking at the answers of the person sitting it. **And the dial that escaped is this: you wrote each student's exam fairly, but you decided how many questions to include by checking which length made the class average look best.**

**Watch out for:** any study with more measured features than it has people in the smaller group — genomics, radiomics, morphometrics, sensor panels. **Three questions. Was the feature selection inside the resampling loop or before it?** **Were the hyperparameters — including how many features — also chosen inside?** **And does the paper say so in a sentence you can quote, or must you infer it?** **The strongest tell is a paper that reports which features were "significant" on the whole cohort and then reports an accuracy: if those are the same features the model used, the accuracy is a selection score.** This paper reports both, which is why concern 1 notes the tension — and its model paragraph resolves it in the right direction.

**Normalised measurements can be algebraically locked together — so two "findings" may be one.**

This is the sharpest thing in this entry, and it took nothing but arithmetic.

**Why normalisation was necessary.** A photograph taken from 10 inches gives bigger measurements than one from 18 inches, and a bigger child gives bigger measurements than a smaller one. **Neither has anything to do with disease.** So the paper divided every horizontal distance by one reference span: **the distance between the two lateral canthi — the outer corners of the eyes.** Every horizontal feature becomes a proportion of total eye-to-eye width. Sensible, and standard.

**But look at what that span is made of.** Travelling across the face: outer corner of the left eye, then the left eye's width, then the left inner corner, then the gap between the inner corners, then the right inner corner, then the right eye's width, then the outer corner of the right eye.

**So: outer-to-outer width = left eye width + inner-corner gap + right eye width.**

**Divide everything by the outer-to-outer width, and you get: eye width + inner gap + eye width = 1.** Or, since the two eyes are measured together as one averaged value: **inner gap + 2 × eye width = 1. Exactly. Always. By arithmetic, not by biology.**

**Now the paper's two headline features.** The first-ranked is **"distance between medial and lateral canthi"** — one eye's width. The second-ranked is **"distance between medial canthi"** — the inner gap. **They are the two terms in that equation.** If one goes up, the other must come down. **It is not possible for both to be independent findings.**

**Checking it against the paper's own table.** Controls: inner gap **0.408**, eye width **0.298**. 0.408 + 2 × 0.298 = **1.004**. Cases: inner gap **0.433**, eye width **0.285**. 0.433 + 2 × 0.285 = **1.003**. **Both within four-thousandths of one.** And the fact that it is 1.003 rather than exactly 1.000 is itself confirming: these are **medians**, and medians do not add exactly the way individual values do. **A result of exactly 1.000 would have been suspicious; 1.003 is what the constraint looks like seen through median arithmetic.**

**So the honest finding is one measurement, not two: in sickle cell disease the eyes sit relatively further apart and are relatively narrower, as proportions of total eye-to-eye width.** The model understood this — it took the eye width and discarded the inner gap, which is what a well-behaved feature selector does with two inputs that carry the same information. **Table 1 does not, ranking them first and second as separate discoveries, and noting with apparent significance that the inner gap is "the only" feature larger in the disease group. It is larger because the other one is smaller.**

**The everyday version.** You survey a household's budget and report two findings: **"they spend a smaller share on rent"** and **"they spend a larger share on everything else."** Both are true, both will pass a statistical test, and **they are one fact.** Shares of a total must add to 100%, so the moment you express things as proportions, you have built dependencies in. **Any set of parts-of-a-whole has this property, and reporting the parts separately double-counts.**

**Watch out for:** any paper reporting several **normalised, ratio, percentage or proportional** measurements as separate findings. **The check is addition.** Write down what the normalising quantity is physically made of, and see whether the reported features are its components. **Three places this bites hardest: morphometrics normalised by a total span, as here; body-composition studies reporting fat percentage and lean percentage; and dietary studies reporting the share of calories from each macronutrient.** **And the specific tell to remember: when one feature in a long list of same-direction differences runs the opposite way, check first whether it is the arithmetic complement of one of the others.** Here it was.

**A one-to-one matched design sets prevalence to 50% by construction — so its PPV describes no real population.**

The paper reports a **positive predictive value of 0.788**: when the model flags someone, it is right about 79% of the time. **That number is a property of the study's design, not of the disease.**

**Why.** The study deliberately took **154 cases and 154 matched controls**. So in the data, **exactly half the participants have sickle cell disease.** But PPV depends on how common the disease actually is in the people being tested — and no real population is half sickle cell disease. **In the DRC, the figure is on the order of 1–2% of births.**

**The arithmetic, which the model's own sensitivity and specificity allow us to do.** Sensitivity 0.812, specificity 0.779. Of 100 flags raised:

- **At 50% prevalence** (the study): **PPV 78.6%** — about 21 of every 100 flags wrong.
- **At 5% prevalence**: **PPV 16.2%** — about 84 of every 100 flags wrong.
- **At 2% prevalence**: **PPV 7.0%** — about **93 of every 100 flags wrong**.
- **At 1% prevalence**: **PPV 3.6%** — about 96 of every 100 flags wrong.

**The model does not get worse. The haystack gets bigger.** At 2% prevalence, 100 people tested contains 2 with the disease and 98 without; catching 81% of the 2 gives you 1.6 true flags, while wrongly flagging 22% of the 98 gives you 21.7 false ones. **Sensitivity and specificity are unchanged; the mix of who you are testing is not.**

**The one number that survives is the negative predictive value**, which goes the other way: **99.5% at 2% prevalence.** A negative result would be genuinely reassuring. **That is often true of modest tests applied to rare conditions, and it is the half of the picture that gets forgotten.**

**In fairness to the paper, which matters here: it does not claim screening use.** It says the contribution is "not the replacement of established testing nor as a standalone clinical tool," and frames the work as "quantitative phenotype characterization... rather than clinical diagnosis," and the ambition is a **research** measure of disease-associated morphology. **So this calculation is not a correction of the paper. It is a guard against the headline the paper will attract.**

**The everyday version.** A test that correctly identifies 8 in 10 people with a condition sounds useful — and if you run it at an airport where one traveller in a thousand has the condition, **almost everyone it stops will be healthy**, and you will have created a queue of frightened people for every real case found. **The test did not change. The population did.**

**Watch out for:** any PPV, precision, or "when it flags someone it's right X% of the time" from a **matched, balanced, or case-control** design. **Two steps. Find the study's prevalence — in a 1:1 matched design it is 50% by construction.** **Then recompute PPV at the prevalence you care about, using the sensitivity and specificity, which do transport.** **And always ask for the NPV too**, because for a rare condition a modest test is often far better at ruling out than at ruling in — which may be the useful thing it does.

## 6. Jargon Translator

**Sickle cell disease (SCD)** — an inherited blood disorder in which a mutation makes red blood cells stiff and sickle-shaped, so they break apart and block small vessels. **About eight million people worldwide, with nearly half a million affected births a year.**

**Haemoglobin** — the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen. The mutation is in its beta-globin gene.

**Haemoglobin SS** — inheriting the mutation from both parents, the most severe form. **The paper could not determine which form its participants had: "The hemoglobin type of the confirmed SCD cases was unknown."**

**Sickle cell trait** — carrying one copy. Usually causes no illness, and **common in the DRC** — which is why the controls' untested status matters.

**Haemoglobin electrophoresis** — the standard laboratory test that identifies which haemoglobin a person makes. **Cases had it; controls did not.**

**Newborn screening** — testing every baby at birth. **Where it exists, babies found to have SCD start penicillin and extra vaccinations, which prevents many early deaths.** Its absence in much of Sub-Saharan Africa is the gap this paper is motivated by.

**Vaso-occlusion** — sickled cells jamming a small vessel, causing the severe pain crises typical of the disease.

**Bone marrow expansion** — chronic destruction of red cells drives the marrow into overdrive, and in the skull that expansion is known to reshape the midface. **The biological reason midface measurements are a sensible place to look.**

**Craniofacial morphometry** — measuring the shape of the head and face numerically rather than describing it.

**Facial landmarks** — specific anatomical points used as measurement anchors. **33 here, all placed by hand.**

**Canthus (plural canthi)** — a corner of the eye. The **medial canthus** is the inner corner, the **lateral canthus** the outer. **The distance between the lateral canthi is this paper's normalising reference, which is what creates the dependency in Section 5.**

**Nasion** — the bridge of the nose, where it meets the forehead. **Columella** — the strip of tissue between the nostrils. **Ala (plural alae)** — the flared outer wall of a nostril. **Philtrum** — the vertical groove between nose and upper lip. **Oral commissures** — the corners of the mouth.

**Normalisation** — dividing a measurement by a reference length so camera distance and body size cancel out. **Necessary, and the source of the algebraic trap in Section 5.**

**Local binary patterns** — a way of summarising texture by comparing each pixel with its neighbours; "sensitive to lines, shadows, and local contrast." **Computed here at three radii around each landmark, giving 60 texture features — none of which the model selected.**

**Support vector machine (SVM)** — an algorithm that finds the boundary best separating two groups. **A linear one was used here**, meaning the boundary is a straight line through the feature space, which keeps it interpretable.

**Recursive feature elimination** — repeatedly dropping the least useful feature and re-fitting, to find a small informative set.

**Leave-one-out cross-validation** — set one participant aside, train on the other 307, predict that one, repeat 308 times. **Uses every participant as a test case; the estimate is unbiased but noisier than ten-fold** — technique 355.

**Out-of-fold prediction** — a prediction for someone made by a model that never saw them.

**Mann-Whitney U test** — a test comparing two groups without assuming a bell-shaped distribution. Appropriate here.

**Benjamini-Hochberg / false discovery rate (FDR)** — the correction for having run many tests. **With 73 features tested and 14 called significant at q < 0.05, fewer than one false positive is expected.** **q-value** is the p-value after that correction.

**Effect size (rank-based r)** — how big a difference is, not just whether it is detectable. **Roughly: 0.1 small, 0.3 medium, 0.5 large.** **Five of this paper's 14 significant features reach 0.30; eight fall below 0.14.**

**AUROC** — the chance the model scores a person with the disease above one without. **0.843 here.**

**Sensitivity / specificity** — the share of real cases caught (**81.2%**) and the share of non-cases correctly cleared (**77.9%**). **These transport between populations.**

**PPV / NPV** — of those flagged, the share who really have it (**0.788 here**); of those cleared, the share who really do not (**0.811**). **These do NOT transport — they depend on how common the disease is** — technique 357.

**Balanced accuracy** — the average of sensitivity and specificity. **Determined entirely by those two, which is why the reported 0.799 cannot follow from 0.812 and 0.779.**

**Blinding** — keeping the person taking a measurement ignorant of which group the subject is in. **Not reported here, and the measurement was made by hand.**

## 7. What You Can (and Can't) Say

**You can say:**

"In 308 standardised front-facing smartphone photographs — 154 children and young adults with electrophoresis-confirmed sickle cell disease and 154 age- and sex-matched community controls in Kinshasa — 14 of 73 prespecified facial features differed between groups after false-discovery-rate correction."

"A linear support vector machine using six geometric measurements achieved an AUROC of 0.843 (95% CI 0.798–0.887) and an accuracy of 0.795 (0.750–0.840), with sensitivity 81.2% and specificity 77.9%."

"Feature screening and recursive feature elimination were performed inside each leave-one-out iteration using only the training participants."

"Seven geometric measures were smaller in the sickle cell group and one — the distance between the inner eye corners — was larger."

"Six texture descriptors differed between groups, but none was selected by the model, which relied on geometry alone."

"Differences were absent or smaller in the youngest age groups and more pronounced in several older groups; the authors state this indicates age-associated differences and not within-person progression."

"The authors describe the study as an exploratory feasibility analysis, state that performance was not interpreted as external or prospective validation, and state that the findings do not support standalone clinical use."

"Ethical approval came from the National Health Ethics Committee of Kinshasa, with consent in French or Lingala, verbal consent where literacy limited written documentation, and separate explicit authorization for public release of facial images."

**You cannot say:**

"Sickle cell disease produces fourteen distinct facial differences." **Two of the fourteen — the paper's top two by effect size — are algebraically locked together**, summing with the normalising span to 1 (verified at 1.004 and 1.003). They are one finding. And **nine of the fourteen have small effect sizes, with five sitting at q = 0.049.**

"A smartphone photo can detect sickle cell disease." **The landmarks were placed by hand**, so this is a research pipeline with a human in the loop, not an app. And the paper explicitly declines the diagnostic claim.

"The model is right 79% of the time when it flags someone." **Only at the study's 50% prevalence, which is an artefact of 1:1 matching.** At a realistic 2% prevalence the PPV falls to about **7%** — roughly 93 of every 100 flags wrong.

"This could be used to screen for sickle cell disease where testing is unavailable." **The paper does not claim this, and the age pattern argues against it** — the signal is absent in the youngest children, which is exactly where screening changes outcomes.

"The differences reflect sickle cell disease." **Cases came from a specialist clinic and controls from the community, with no chronically-ill control group and no nutritional data** — so disease-specific morphology cannot be separated from the effects of chronic childhood illness generally.

"Facial changes progress with age in sickle cell disease." **The design is cross-sectional and the paper says so explicitly** — different people at different ages, not the same people followed over time, with possible cohort and survival effects.

"The findings apply to sickle cell disease generally." **The haemoglobin genotype of every case was unknown**, so HbSS, HbSC and HbS-β-thalassaemia are pooled despite differing greatly in severity.

"Controls were confirmed disease-free." **They were judged non-SCD clinically and never had electrophoresis** — which the paper states, correctly noting this would tend to make the groups look more alike.

"Texture differences show skin changes in sickle cell disease." **Six texture features differed, none was selected by the model**, and the paper itself calls for standardised colour calibration before texture is interpreted.

"The model was validated." **Internally, by leave-one-out cross-validation at a single site.** The paper states six separate times that this is not external or prospective validation.

**The honest one-sentence version:** *a careful exploratory study in a population digital-health research almost always ignores, with the best-documented consent process in this reading list and the main statistical trap both avoided and explicitly documented — whose real finding is narrower than its table suggests: one midface measurement pattern, present in older children and young adults but not infants, measured from hand-placed landmarks without reported blinding, in cases drawn from a specialist clinic against controls drawn from the community.*

## 8. Bottom Line for Your Life

**If you or your child has sickle cell disease:** nothing here changes any care, and no photograph can diagnose or monitor this condition. **The facts worth holding onto are the ones in the paper's introduction, not its results.** Sickle cell disease is diagnosed by a blood test, and in places that test every newborn, the babies found early start penicillin and extra vaccinations — **and that alone prevents a large share of early deaths.** If you are in a setting where newborn testing is not routine and there is sickle cell disease in your family, **asking for the blood test is the thing that matters.** It is cheap, it is definitive, and nothing in this paper is a substitute for it.

**If you work in global health:** the interesting result here is the one the paper reports and does not underline. **The facial signal is absent in infants and strongest in young adults.** Since the entire life-saving value of early sickle cell detection is in infancy, **a facial measure is least useful exactly where a test is most needed** — and its best-measured signal is in people who were diagnosed years ago. **That does not make the work pointless;** the authors are explicit that they are characterising disease-associated morphology, not building a screening test. **But it should settle how this gets read.** If a tool like this has a future, it is more plausibly as a research measure of cumulative disease burden in people already diagnosed than as a substitute for a blood test in people who are not.

**If you build or evaluate models on small datasets, copy one thing from this paper and avoid one.** **Copy the nesting, and copy saying so.** When you have more measured features than people — morphometrics, genomics, radiomics, sensor panels — **the feature selection has to happen inside the resampling loop, and one sentence stating that is worth more to a reader than a paragraph of results.** This paper wrote that sentence. **Avoid its one leak: it chose how many features to keep by watching the cross-validated score converge**, which quietly turns the reported number into the best of several. **Nest that choice too.**

**And here is the two-minute check worth taking from this entry regardless of field.** When a paper reports several **normalised, proportional or percentage** measurements as separate findings, **write down what the normalising quantity is physically made of, and try adding the parts up.** Here, dividing everything by the distance between the outer eye corners meant that the eye width and the inner-eye gap were forced to sum to a constant — so the paper's first- and second-biggest findings were one fact. **I checked by addition: 1.004 in controls, 1.003 in cases.** **The specific tell to remember: in a list of differences that mostly run one way, if a single feature runs the other way, check first whether it is the arithmetic complement of one of the others.**

**One more, about tests and populations.** This model flags people correctly about 79% of the time — **in a study where half the participants had the disease, because the researchers chose 154 of each.** At a realistic 2% prevalence the same model, with the same sensitivity and specificity, would be **wrong on about 93 of every 100 people it flagged.** **The test did not change; the population did.** Whenever you see "when it flags someone, it's right X% of the time," find the study's prevalence first — **and in a matched case-control design it is 50% by construction, which is no population on earth.**

**A last word on something this paper got right that has nothing to do with statistics.** Photographing the faces of children in a low-resource setting for research is ethically fraught, and this study handled it about as well as it can be handled: **community information sessions before recruitment, consent in French or Lingala, verbal consent accepted where literacy was a barrier, every participant given a copy of their consent form, a separate explicit authorization required before any identifiable face was published, and an AI-generated face used for the methods figure rather than a real participant's.** **That is worth naming as a standard, not treating as a footnote.**

**If you are reading the coverage:** expect "AI spots sickle cell disease from a smartphone photo." **Four things will likely be missing.** That the landmarks were placed by hand, so there is no app. That two of the headline features are the same finding counted twice. That the signal is absent in infants, where detection would actually save lives. And that in a real population the great majority of its positive flags would be wrong — a consequence of a matched design the authors never claimed otherwise about.

**This is education, not medical advice.** Sickle cell disease is diagnosed by blood testing, and nothing described here is validated for clinical use. Questions about testing, screening or care belong with a clinician.

Table 1 reports both as separate findings: **"distance between medial and lateral canthi"** (eye width, effect size **0.402**, model-selected and ranked first) and **"distance between medial canthi"** (intercanthal, **0.395**, the only feature in the entire study reported in the opposite direction). **Checking: controls 0.408 + 2 × 0.298 = 1.004; cases 0.433 + 2 × 0.285 = 1.003.** **Both within four-thousandths of one — and the small shortfall is exactly what adding medians of ratios produces rather than ratios of medians, which confirms rather than weakens the point.** **So there is one midface finding in the eye region, not two.** The model behaved correctly, selecting one and dropping the other — **Table 1 does not.**

**Arithmetic verified today.** **Cohort:** 154 + 154 = **308 participants and 308 photographs — one image each**, so no within-person clustering. **Age groups** 18 + 38 + 92 + 74 + 86 = **308**. **Every confidence interval matches its denominator**: accuracy 0.795 [0.750, 0.840] is a binomial interval on 308 (±0.045, exact); sensitivity and specificity intervals are consistent with 154 each; and **AUROC 0.843 [0.798, 0.887] reproduces a Hanley–McNeil calculation on 154 against 154 (±0.044)**. **No inflated precision anywhere** — worth stating given how often this list has found the opposite. **PPV and NPV at 50% prevalence reproduce from the reported sensitivity and specificity.** **Multiplicity:** 73 prespecified features, Benjamini-Hochberg at q < 0.05, 14 discoveries, so **under one expected false positive** — the right instrument, correctly used. **Effect sizes:** 5 of 14 reach r ≥ 0.30; **9 fall below 0.22 and 8 below 0.14**; **5 sit at q = 0.049**; and **two of the six model-selected features are among the weak ones** (0.139 and 0.114).

**A one-patient inconsistency, and a correction to my own first pass.** Rebuilding the confusion matrix from the reported metrics, **the seven published figures cannot all derive from one set of counts.** **Sensitivity (0.812) and accuracy (0.795) correspond to 125 of 154 cases flagged with 120 of 154 controls cleared; PPV (0.788), NPV (0.811), F1 (0.803) and balanced accuracy (0.799) all correspond to 126.** **My first pass described this as "balanced accuracy is wrong"; the accurate statement is that the metrics split between two matrices differing by a single patient** — balanced accuracy is simply the one that cannot be reconciled on any integer counts, since it is fixed by sensitivity and specificity alone. **Immaterial to every conclusion: one patient in 308.** Recorded because this list checks (345), and the correction recorded because this list keeps its own slips visible.
