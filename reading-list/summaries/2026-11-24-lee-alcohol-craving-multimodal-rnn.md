## 1. The Gist

**Sixty people who drink problematically carried a phone app for four weeks, answering one question each evening about how much they had wanted a drink. The researchers also measured their brainwaves, their heart rhythms and their psychology once at the start. Then they built a small AI model to guess tomorrow's craving from the last four days. Adding the brain and heart measurements raised its hit rate from 45% to 56%. The study is careful in ways most papers on this list are not — and it is missing the one comparison that would tell you whether the model can forecast anything at all.**

**Start with why anyone would try this.** Alcohol craving — the pull toward a drink — predicts actual drinking, and it predicts relapse after a period of not drinking. The paper's own framing: craving *"was shown to predict subsequent alcohol use,"* and the association *"appears to be strongest over short time frames."* **So if you could see tomorrow's craving coming today, you could send help today** — a reminder, a coping exercise, a message to a clinician. That idea has a name in the field, **just-in-time adaptive intervention**, and the paper is explicit that this is what the model is for: *"when predicted craving exceeds a predefined threshold, the system may trigger personalized interventions such as behavioral prompts or clinician alerts."*

**What they did.** Seventy adults aged 19 to 59 who said they drank problematically were recruited in Seoul between July 2023 and July 2024 through an online research-recruitment platform. **None was in formal treatment.** Ten dropped out or were excluded, leaving **60 people**.

At a first visit, each person had **resting brainwaves recorded for ten minutes (electroencephalography, or EEG)**, **heart rhythm recorded for five minutes (heart rate variability, or HRV)**, an IQ test, a psychiatric interview, and a stack of questionnaires about drinking, mood, impulsiveness, sleep and readiness to change. **All of that was measured once and never again** — in the paper's language, these are **static** features.

Then an app went on their phone, and for **28 days** they answered a 17-item survey each evening: mood, meals, sleep, and what they drank. **One of those items is the thing being predicted: "How much did you want to drink alcohol today?", answered from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very much).** **Those daily answers are the dynamic features.** Across everyone, **1,673 daily craving reports** came in — which I checked against the reported average of 27.88 days each, and 60 × 27.88 is 1,672.8, so that reconciles.

**The model.** A **recurrent neural network** — a model built to read a sequence in order, carrying a running summary of what it has seen so far. The researchers call theirs **"tiny"** on purpose: one layer, 16 units, deliberately too small to memorise much, because 60 people is not many. It reads **four days** of app answers and guesses the fifth day's craving level, choosing among the five Likert levels.

**And here is the clever part, which is also the part that decides what the study does and does not show.** The model was run two ways. In the **unimodal** version, the running summary starts empty — the model knows nothing about the person except their last four days. In the **multimodal** version, the person's brainwaves, heart measures and questionnaire scores are first squeezed into a short numerical summary, and **that summary becomes the model's starting state.** In plain terms: **the model is told who this person is before it starts reading their week.**

**The result.** The multimodal model got **56%** of next-day craving levels exactly right, against **45%** for the unimodal. Its average miss shrank from **0.90 to 0.68** levels. **And 43 of the 60 people (72%) were predicted better by the multimodal model** — a paired comparison, person by person, which is the right way to do it.

**Now, the first thing worth saying, because it is a credit and this list has had to supply it five entries in a row.** **The paper tells you what the score would be if the model did nothing.** Verbatim: *"a random classifier would achieve an accuracy of approximately 20% in a 5-class classification setting, and a majority-class classifier would yield approximately 37%."* So: guess at random, 20%. Always guess the commonest answer, 37%. Unimodal, 45%. Multimodal, 56%. **A reader can immediately see that the gain over doing nothing is 19 points, not 56.** Most papers make you work that out yourself.

**The second thing is the gap at the centre of the paper, and it is a simple one.** For a next-day forecast of something that changes slowly, **the comparison everybody wants is: what if you just guessed that tomorrow will be the same as today?** This is called a **persistence baseline**, and in weather forecasting it is the first hurdle any model must clear. **The paper never computes it.** I searched the full text; there is no carry-forward, naive, or last-observation comparator anywhere. **And craving is exactly the kind of thing that persists** — the paper's own introduction says so.

**Why that matters so much is visible in the paper's own numbers.** The researchers checked which people the model handled well, and found that **the more someone's craving moved around, the worse the model did** — a correlation of **−0.55** for the unimodal model and **−0.67** for the multimodal, both highly significant. **Read that again: both models work best on the people whose craving barely changes.** Those are precisely the people for whom "same as yesterday" is a winning guess. **So the headline numbers may be measuring how still the target sat, rather than how well the model tracked it — and without the persistence comparison there is no way to tell.** In fairness, the paper also found that **the multimodal model's *advantage* was not related to how much craving moved**, which argues the extra brain-and-heart information is doing something real. Both facts belong in the picture.

**The third thing I found is about the precision the paper appears to report, and I checked it two ways.** Table 2 gives the multimodal accuracy as **0.56 with a 95% confidence interval of 0.552 to 0.560** — a span of eight thousandths. That looks like extraordinary certainty from 60 people. **It isn't, and the paper is honest about what it is: a "95% CI of the mean" across 100 runs of the model with different random starting values.** It measures whether the model lands in the same place when you restart it. **It says nothing about whether another 60 people would give the same answer.**

**You can recover the number that does.** The paper reports a paired t test across its 60 participants with **t = 5.00** for the multimodal gain of about 0.11. Working backwards, the standard error is 0.11 ÷ 5.00 = 0.022, so **the honest 95% interval for the gain runs from about 0.066 to 0.154 — a span of 0.088, roughly eleven times wider than the interval printed for the accuracy itself.** **And I checked that reconstruction against something independent:** it implies that about **15.6 of 60 people** should do worse with the multimodal model, and **the paper reports that 43 of 60 did better**, leaving 17 who did not. **Predicted about 15.6, observed at most 17 — the reconstruction holds up.**

**Fourth, and this one took a little arithmetic.** The paper reports the average size of the model's miss (**mean absolute error**) as 0.90 for the unimodal model. It never says what always-guessing-the-commonest-answer would score on that measure. Using the paper's stated craving distribution — mean 2.21, standard deviation 1.13, median 2, middle half from 1 to 3, commonest answer about 37% — **I reconstructed that a guess-the-mode predictor would miss by about 0.83 on average.** **Which means the unimodal model's 0.90 is *worse* than always guessing the same number**, even though its 45% accuracy is better than that baseline's 37%. **Two metrics, opposite verdicts about whether the unimodal model has any skill at all.** The multimodal model's 0.68 beats the baseline on both. (This is my estimate, not the paper's: the actual label distribution sits in an unreachable supplement, so I derived it from the stated summary figures, and five different plausible distributions all gave 0.83 or 0.84.)

**Fifth, the question of who the model works for.** The data were split **by time within each person**: each participant's last 5 days became the test, the 3 days before that the tune-up set, everything earlier the training data. **This is the right way to avoid cheating with the future**, and the paper names the choice explicitly. **But it means every single person in the test was also in the training.** The model never once had to make a prediction about a stranger. **So nothing here tells you whether it would work for a new patient** — and that specific limit is not among the six limitations the paper lists.

**Sixth, and the sharpest practical problem.** The paper proposes triggering an alert when predicted craving crosses a threshold. **The model is worst at exactly the high levels such a threshold would use.** Craving level 5 went from 0% correct to 33% correct — an improvement, and still two misses in three. Level 4 *"remained limited."* And the two summary scores tell the same story: **0.51 when common answers count more, 0.37 when every level counts equally — a 14-point gap, meaning the rare levels are predicted far worse than the common ones.** **The rare levels are the ones that matter.**

**What this paper is.** A genuinely careful small study with unusually good statistical housekeeping — baselines given, 100 restarts, four metrics each justified, a modality-by-modality breakdown, tight control of overfitting, and a limitations section that says in plain words that *"the overall predictive performance was modest."* **Its own stated claim — that adding brain and heart data beats app data alone — is properly tested and supported.** **The broader claim its title makes, that it forecasts next-day craving, is not, because the one comparison that would establish forecasting skill was never run.**

## 2. Study Snapshot

**Study type:** prospective longitudinal observational study with an internally validated multiclass forecasting model. **No randomisation, no intervention, no external validation.** The paper repeatedly frames the model as a component for future just-in-time interventions rather than a validated tool.

**Setting and recruitment.** Seoul, South Korea. Recruited **July 2023 to July 2024** via an online advertisement on a clinical-research recruitment platform, targeting **self-reported problematic drinking**. Baseline assessments at **Samsung Medical Center**. **Participants were not receiving formal treatment for alcohol use disorder during the study.** Age range **19 to 59**, with the upper limit set deliberately *"to minimize potential confounding effects of age-related neurophysiological and structural brain changes."* **Sample size was justified by reference to previous EEG and machine-learning studies in alcohol-related populations "with comparable sample sizes"** — a precedent argument, not a power calculation.

**Exclusions:** inability to consent, pregnancy or pregnancy plans, participation in another trial, use of more than two phones, and claustrophobia or MRI contraindications.

**Flow.** **70 recruited → 2 withdrew early → 7 excluded after initiation** (age, failure to enter alcohol information in the app, or a missed visit 1) **→ 1 excluded for "excessive discontinuities that prevented the construction of valid sequential windows" → 60 analysed.** **I verified 70 − 2 − 7 − 1 = 60.**

**Cohort.** Mean age **34.17 (SD 8.62)**; **25 male (41.7%), 35 female (58.3%)**; IQ **110.83 (SD 14.12)**; education **15.65 (SD 3.11)** years; married **19 (31.7%)**; smokers **14 (23.3%)**; **AUDIT score 18.15 (SD 6.67)** — above the usual threshold for hazardous drinking, so this is a genuinely affected sample. **Every percentage in Table 1 reproduces exactly from its count over 60.**

**Visits.** Visit 1: demographics, HRV, EEG, psychological and cognitive assessment, the **Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview** to rule out comorbid psychiatric diagnoses, and DSM-5 evaluation for alcohol use disorder. Visit 2: app installed with step-by-step instruction and reminders set. Visit 3, after four weeks: repeat HRV and psychometrics.

**The daily survey.** Once daily by app, **at a participant-selected time at the end of each day**, with an automated push notification, taking about 5 minutes. **17 items across four domains:** mood (overall mood, interest/pleasure), diet (meal consumption and quantity), **alcohol-related behaviours (craving, consumption, drinking time, beverage type, amount, companions, duration, location, motives, blackout)**, and sleep (duration, satisfaction, daytime interference). Some items were conditional. **"Surveys were completed daily regardless of whether craving was experienced; no event-contingent assessments were used"** — a deliberate design choice that avoids a well-known bias and is worth crediting.

**The outcome.** **A single EMA item: "How much did you want to drink alcohol today?", on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very much).** *"For modeling, this outcome was treated as a 5-class classification target, with the craving level reported on the following day serving as the prediction target."* **No reliability or test-retest estimate for this item is reported.**

**The predictors.** **Longitudinal** EMA features (mood, diet, sleep, alcohol consumption) plus **static baseline** features in four blocks. **Psychological:** Drinking Motives Questionnaire (four subscales), AUDIT, Beck Depression Inventory-II, Beck Anxiety Inventory, Barratt Impulsiveness Scale (total and three subscales, raw and transformed), Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (total and seven subscales), SOCRATES (three subscales), and IQ — **all validated for Korean use. EEG:** 64-channel resting recording in a sound-shielded room, 10 minutes (5 eyes-closed, 5 eyes-open), impedance below 5 kΩ, processed in MNE-Python with automated artifact rejection, **artifact-free 2-second epochs from the eyes-closed condition only**, relative power in five bands (delta 1–4, theta 4–8, alpha 8–12, beta 12–25, gamma 30–40 Hz). **HRV:** 5-minute seated resting electrocardiogram at 256 Hz, **with caffeine and nicotine abstinence for at least 2 hours beforehand**, yielding 18 named measures.

**Missing data, handled thoughtfully.** Adherence varied: **mean 27.88 days (SD 1.35; median 28, IQR 28–29; range 21–30)**; **29 (48.3%) gave exactly 28 days, 47 (78.3%) gave at least 28, and 18 (30%) gave more than 28** — **I verified that 47 − 29 = 18, so these three statements are mutually consistent.** **Four participants had non-consecutive reporting.** Crucially: *"we did not impute missing days to artificially construct continuous time series, as such imputation may introduce unrealistic temporal dependencies and bias the learning process."* Instead, windows with **gaps greater than one day inside the input sequence**, or **more than a two-day gap between the last input day and the target**, were dropped. Within-feature missing values got *"domain-informed imputation strategies tailored to each variable type"* — not further specified.

**Feature selection.** Starting point: **394 baseline features on 60 people, and 45 EMA time-series features.** The paper names the problem directly — *"the high-dimensional feature space relative to the limited sample size (ie, P>>N)"* — and applies **random-forest importance separately to the two blocks**. For EMA, a classifier *"trained exclusively on the training portion of the dataset, excluding the final 8 days per participant used for validation and testing to prevent data leakage"*, keeping features above mean importance. For baseline, a regression **onto each participant's average craving, "computed using only the training data."** Result: **11 EMA features and 42 baseline features, minus one EEG epoch-count feature manually removed as a preprocessing artefact, giving 41.** **I verified 42 − 1 = 41.** Named survivors include mood items, meal frequency, sleep quality and alcohol consumption from the app, and IQ, working memory, alcohol-use measures, DMQ, BIS, SOCRATES, EEG delta/theta/beta/gamma power and HRV metrics from baseline. **The complete lists are in unreachable supplementary tables — and notably, prior craving is not among the named selected EMA features, though I cannot confirm from the retrieved text whether it was a predictor.**

**The split.** *"To preserve the temporal structure of the data and prevent information leakage, a participant-wise time-series split was applied. For each participant, the last 5 days were used as the test set, and the preceding 3 days were used as the validation set."* And again, unambiguously: *"the dataset was split along the temporal dimension for each participant, rather than participant-level splitting."* **Lookback window of 4 days for the primary analysis, with a sensitivity analysis across 2-, 4-, 6- and 8-day windows in an unreachable supplementary table.** Standardisation was **fitted on training data only**.

**The models.** Both are **vanilla recurrent networks with a single 16-unit hidden layer**, chosen for smallness: *"it reduces the risk of overfitting while preserving the ability to capture meaningful temporal patterns."* The **unimodal** model initialises the hidden state as a **zero vector**. The **multimodal** model passes the 41 static features through a **one-layer feedforward network with ReLU activation and 20% dropout** and uses the result to **initialise the hidden state** — *"allowing individual-specific baseline characteristics to directly condition temporal predictions."* Five-class softmax output, Adam optimiser at learning rate 0.001, cross-entropy loss, minibatches of 32 with shuffling, **maximum 70 epochs with early stopping on validation loss**. **Each model was trained 100 times with seeds from 2026 to 2125, and results summarised as mean, SD and 95% CI of the mean across runs.**

**Results — performance.** Unimodal test: accuracy **0.45 (SD 0.01; CI 0.443–0.447)**, weighted F1 **0.38**, macro F1 **0.24**, MAE **0.90**. Multimodal test: accuracy **0.56 (SD 0.02; CI 0.552–0.560)**, weighted F1 **0.51**, macro F1 **0.37**, MAE **0.68**. **Training figures: unimodal accuracy 0.46, multimodal 0.59 — so train-to-test gaps of +0.01 and +0.03, with the largest gap anywhere being macro F1 at +0.06.** The paper states the random (20%) and majority-class (37%) baselines. **Correct classification of craving level 5 rose from 0% to 33%, while level 4 "remained limited."**

**Results — individual level.** **43 of 60 (72%) improved** with the multimodal model; **Wilcoxon signed-rank W = 176, P < .001; paired t(59) = 5.00, P < .001**, with both tests run *"motivated by uncertainty regarding the normality of the distribution."*

**Results — post hoc correlations, labelled exploratory.** Within-participant craving variability against accuracy: **ρ = −0.549 (unimodal) and ρ = −0.671 (multimodal), both P < .001.** Craving entropy and number of distinct craving levels likewise negative. **EMA response days: ρ = 0.035 (P = .79) and 0.085 (P = .52) — not associated.** Usable sequential samples: not associated. **None of the examined characteristics was associated with the size of the multimodal gain.** The paper states these *"were exploratory and should not be interpreted as causal."*

**Results — modality ablation.** Zero-initialisation **0.45**; **+EEG only 0.52; +HRV only 0.53; +psychological only 0.50; all three 0.56.** The paper draws the deployment inference itself: *"clinicians prioritizing resource-constrained deployment might first consider HRV monitoring as a complement to EMA, given its relative ease of collection compared to full EEG acquisition."*

**Limitations the authors state — six, and they are substantive.** Small sample, *"which may limit the generalizability."* **Single South Korean cohort "without external validation using an independent dataset."** Alternative architectures (LSTM, GRU, temporal convolutional networks, gradient boosting) *"were not systematically evaluated."* The study could not determine a minimum EMA-completion threshold. **"Fifth, the overall predictive performance was modest, as reflected in the accuracy and weighted and macro F1-scores, indicating the need for further model optimization and validation."** And next-day forecasting *"may not fully capture individual and circadian variability in craving dynamics."*

**Not reported or not retrievable:** **funding, conflicts of interest, data availability and author contributions**; **any persistence or carry-forward baseline**; **any evaluation on a held-out participant**; **any reliability estimate for the single-item craving outcome**; **any reporting guideline or registration number**; the confusion matrices (Figure S4); the craving label distribution (Figure S3); the window-length sensitivity results (Table S4); the full selected-feature lists (Tables S1, S2); and the post hoc correlation detail (Table S3).

## 3. How Strong Is This Evidence? (Grade 3 out of 5)

**Unusually good statistical housekeeping on a small, honestly-described prospective dataset, whose own stated claim is properly tested and supported — capped because the headline forecasting claim was never checked against the one comparator that would establish it, because no new person was ever tested, and because the intervals on display understate the real uncertainty by roughly elevenfold.**

**What is done well, and several items are better than anything this list saw in the past week.**

**The paper supplies its own base rates, unprompted.** *"A random classifier would achieve an accuracy of approximately 20%... a majority-class classifier would yield approximately 37%."* **This list has had to compute that comparison itself for five consecutive entries.** Here it is handed to the reader in the Results, which is exactly where it belongs — and it immediately reframes "56% accuracy" as a 19-point gain over doing nothing.

**Four metrics, each with a stated reason.** The paper explains why accuracy misleads under class imbalance, why the weighted F1 score *"may be biased toward the majority class"*, why the macro score is also reported so that *"minority classes also contribute to the evaluation and that the model is penalized if it performs poorly on them"*, and why mean absolute error suits an **ordinal** target where *"prediction errors of smaller magnitude are more acceptable than larger deviations."* **That is a teaching-grade justification of a metric set, and it is rare.**

**One hundred random restarts, with intervals.** *"Each model was trained 100 times with different random seed values (ranging from 2026 to 2125)."* Technique 321 in this list was prompted by a paper admitting its seeds were not fixed; **this paper not only fixes them but reports the spread across all 100 runs.** It is the right instrument for the question of whether a small neural network lands in the same place twice.

**Temporal leakage is handled correctly and named.** The split is by time within each person, standardisation is fitted on training data only, **feature selection excluded the final 8 days per participant**, and the baseline-feature selection target — each person's average craving — was *"computed using only the training data."* The paper states the principle it is following: *"only data available at the time of prediction are used for parameter estimation."*

**Overfitting is genuinely controlled, and the numbers prove it.** Train-to-test gaps are **+0.01 (unimodal accuracy), +0.03 (multimodal accuracy), and at worst +0.06 (multimodal macro F1)**. **For a setting with 394 candidate baseline features and 60 people, that is tight.** Set it beside entry 85, where a random forest scored **exactly 100%** on its own training data with a 26-point drop to test: **here the deliberately tiny architecture did the job it was chosen for, and the paper's reasoning for choosing it is stated in advance rather than after the fact.**

**The comparison that the paper actually claims is tested properly.** The multimodal-versus-unimodal question is settled **person by person, paired**, with **two tests** (one parametric, one not, with the reason given), **72% consistency across individuals**, and **a modality-by-modality ablation** showing which block contributes what. **That is a well-constructed answer to a well-posed question.**

**And the honesty is real.** Six limitations, including **"the overall predictive performance was modest"** — a sentence many authors would not write. The post hoc correlations are labelled exploratory and *"should not be interpreted as causal."* Missing days were **deliberately not imputed**, with the reason given. Surveys were completed daily **regardless of whether craving occurred**, avoiding a known bias. Compensation is disclosed. Two IRBs are named.

**Why this is not a 4.**

**There is no persistence baseline, and for a next-day forecast that is the comparator that matters.** "Tomorrow equals today" is the first hurdle in every forecasting field. **The paper never computes it**, and I confirmed its absence across the full text. **The paper's own data make the omission acute:** accuracy fell sharply the more a person's craving moved (**ρ = −0.55 and −0.67**), so **both models did best precisely where "same as yesterday" would also do best.** Whether the model tracks craving or merely benefits from craving sitting still cannot be determined from what is reported. **In fairness, the fact that the multimodal *gain* was unrelated to craving variability argues the added modalities contribute something genuine** — that half must be stated too.

**No participant was ever held out.** The split is temporal within person, which is right for leakage but means **every test participant's own history, brainwaves and heart measures were in the training data.** The architecture makes this consequential rather than incidental: the static features exist specifically to **initialise the model's state with who this person is**. **So the multimodal advantage and "the model has learned this particular person's usual craving level" are not separable here.** The paper lists single-cohort generalisation as a limitation but **never notes that generalisation to a new individual is untested**, which is the sharper version of the same problem.

**The reported intervals are roughly eleven times too narrow for the question most readers will ask.** Table 2's multimodal accuracy interval spans **0.008**. The paper labels it correctly as a CI of the mean across runs, **but never says which uncertainty it excludes.** Reconstructing from the paper's own paired t test (t = 5.00 on 60 participants) gives **a 95% interval for the multimodal gain of about 0.066 to 0.154, a span of 0.088.** **A reader who takes 0.552–0.560 as the study's precision is wrong by an order of magnitude.**

**The proposed deployment rests on the classes the model handles worst.** The paper suggests triggering alerts when predicted craving crosses a threshold — but **level 5 is 33% correct and level 4 "remained limited."** The **14-point gap between the weighted score (0.51) and the equal-weight score (0.37)** says the same thing more generally. **A high-craving alert built on this model would miss most high-craving days.**

**And on the ordinal metric, the unimodal model may have no skill at all.** Reconstructing a modal-class predictor's average miss from the paper's stated craving distribution gives **about 0.83**, against the unimodal model's **0.90**. **So the unimodal model is better than that baseline on accuracy (0.45 vs 0.37) and worse on error size** — two metrics with opposite verdicts, which the paper does not notice because it supplies a baseline for one metric and not the other. **This is my reconstruction, not the paper's figure**, since the label distribution is in an unreachable supplement; five plausible distributions consistent with the stated mean, SD, median and quartiles all gave 0.83 or 0.84.

**Also against it:** **n = 60**, with a test set of at most **300 person-days drawn from those same 60 people**, and the **independent unit is the person, not the day**; **no external validation** (stated); **no power calculation**, only a precedent argument from earlier studies; the outcome is **one Likert item with no reliability estimate**, and **the paper's own evidence that accuracy depends on how much that item moves makes its measurement properties load-bearing**; **feature selection ran once, outside the 100-seed loop**, so the intervals do not reflect selection variability; **compensation scaled to app use** acts directly on the main data stream; and **no reporting guideline or registration**.

**Why this is not a 2.** Entry 84 earned a 2 for a headline contradicted by its own sensitivity figure, a performance table that would not reconcile, and an explanation resting on a variable with no association to the outcome. **Nothing of that kind is here.** **Every number I checked reconciles** — the flow diagram, every demographic percentage, the person-day total, the internal consistency of three separate adherence statements, the feature counts. Claims are modest and hedged. The baselines are given. The limitations are honest to the point of calling the performance modest. **What is claimed is close to what is shown; the problem is that the title claims something broader than what was tested.**

**What would make this a 4, concretely.** **Report the persistence baseline** — accuracy and MAE for "tomorrow = today", which takes one line of code on data already in hand. **Report a leave-one-participant-out evaluation** so a new-person claim becomes possible. **Report the interval that varies people, not seeds**, or say plainly which uncertainty the printed one omits. **Report the modal-class baseline for MAE as well as for accuracy.** **And report performance at the threshold the proposed alert would use.** **All five use data already collected.**

## 4. The Editor's Concerns

**1. No persistence baseline — the central gap.** For a next-day forecast, *"tomorrow will be like today"* is the hurdle every model must clear, and in weather forecasting it is reported as a matter of routine. **This paper never computes it.** The paper's own introduction establishes that craving persists over short time frames, and its own post hoc analysis shows accuracy collapsing as craving becomes more variable (**ρ = −0.549 unimodal, −0.671 multimodal, both P < .001**). **Both models therefore perform best exactly where a carry-forward guess would perform best.** The absence is not a technicality: it is the difference between "this model forecasts craving" and "craving does not move much." **In fairness, the multimodal *gain* showed no association with craving variability**, which supports a real contribution from the added modalities — **but that defends the comparison the paper makes, not the forecasting claim in its title.**

**2. Every test participant was in the training data.** The split is **temporal within person**, correctly chosen and explicitly stated. But it means **no prediction was ever made about an unseen individual.** The architecture makes this decisive: the whole point of the static block is to *"initialize the hidden state"* so that *"individual-specific baseline characteristics... directly condition temporal predictions."* **So "the multimodal model uses brain and heart information" and "the multimodal model has learned each participant's typical craving level from their own earlier days" predict the same result, and this design cannot separate them.** **The paper lists single-cohort generalisation as a limitation but never names this one** — and a leave-one-participant-out run would settle it with no new data.

**3. The printed intervals answer a question almost no reader is asking.** Table 2's intervals are, correctly labelled, **95% CIs of the mean across 100 random seeds** — a measure of whether the network lands in the same place when restarted. **They are not sampling intervals**, and the paper never says so. Spans of **0.004 to 0.017** on 60 participants will read as extraordinary precision. **Reconstructed from the paper's own paired t test (t = 5.00, 60 participants), the 95% interval for the multimodal gain runs roughly 0.066 to 0.154 — a span of 0.088, about eleven times the printed accuracy interval and twenty-two times the unimodal one.** **And the reconstruction checks out independently:** it predicts about **15.6 of 60** participants doing worse with the multimodal model, and the paper reports **17** who did not do better.

**4. The deployment proposal rests on the classes the model handles worst.** The paper proposes that *"when predicted craving exceeds a predefined threshold, the system may trigger personalized interventions."* But **craving level 5 is 33% correct** (up from 0%, which is real progress) and **level 4 "remained limited."** The **0.51 versus 0.37 gap** between the majority-weighted and equal-weight summary scores says the rare levels are predicted far worse than the common ones. **The rare levels are the clinically actionable ones.** An alert built on this model, as proposed, would miss most of the days it exists to catch — **and no performance figure at any candidate threshold is reported.**

**5. Accuracy and error size disagree about whether the unimodal model has skill.** The paper gives a majority-class baseline for accuracy (**~37%**) and none for MAE. **Reconstructing one from the paper's stated craving distribution — mean 2.21, SD 1.13, median 2, IQR 1–3, modal share ~37% — a guess-the-mode predictor would miss by about 0.83 on average.** The unimodal model's MAE is **0.90**: **worse.** So on accuracy it beats the baseline (0.45 vs 0.37) and on error magnitude it does not. The multimodal model's **0.68** beats it on both. **The lesson is about reporting: supply the base rate for every metric, not just the convenient one.** **Flagged as my estimate**, since the label distribution is in an unreachable supplement; five distributions consistent with all four stated summary figures gave 0.83 or 0.84.

**6. The outcome is one Likert item, and its reliability is never examined.** *"How much did you want to drink alcohol today?"*, 1 to 5, answered once nightly. **This is the right measurement in principle** — craving is subjective, and a person's own report is the only reference standard there could be. **But a single self-report item has test-retest properties, and none is reported.** The stakes are specific: the model is being graded against this item, so **any night-to-night noise in it caps the achievable accuracy** — and the paper's own finding that accuracy falls as the item becomes more variable cannot distinguish genuine craving swings from measurement noise. **The reference-standard question in its hardest form, and it is not addressed.**

**7. Feature selection ran once, outside the loop.** **394 baseline features on 60 people, reduced to 41; 45 EMA features reduced to 11.** The selection was correctly kept away from the test *days*, but it was performed **once, on all 60 participants, before the 100-seed loop began.** The seeds re-randomise initialisation only. **So the reported spread across runs captures none of the variability in which features were chosen** — and with 394 candidates on 60 samples, that variability is where most of the instability lives. **The contrast with yesterday's entry is exact: entry 86 rebuilt its selection inside every resampling iteration; this paper has no participant-level resampling into which selection could be nested.** Each paper closed a different leak.

**8. The modalities overlap heavily rather than adding up.** Alone, EEG gives **+0.07**, HRV **+0.08**, psychological **+0.05** — **summing to 0.20 against a combined gain of only 0.11.** **So roughly half of each block's apparent contribution is information the others already carry.** The paper does not quantify this, but it draws the right practical conclusion anyway: **HRV alone delivers about 73% of the full gain** and is far cheaper to collect than 64-channel EEG, so *"clinicians prioritizing resource-constrained deployment might first consider HRV monitoring."* **Credit for an ablation that is actually used to say something useful.**

**9. The independent unit is the person, not the day.** **1,673 craving reports** sounds like a reasonable dataset; the test set is **at most 300 person-days, and they come from the same 60 people** whose earlier days trained the model. **Days within a person are not independent observations** — that is the premise of the whole sliding-window design. **So every uncertainty statement should be scaled by 60, not by 300 or 1,673.** The paper's paired, participant-level significance testing gets this right; the Table 2 intervals do not speak to it at all. **This is the seventh consecutive entry in which the headline number's real denominator is smaller than it first appears.**

**10. Compensation was tied to app use.** *"Participants received compensation according to their level of app-use participation, with compensation averaging approximately KRW 200,000 (US $149)."* **Disclosing this is good practice.** But the incentive acts directly on the study's main data stream, and **adherence is remarkably high** — 78.3% gave at least the full 28 days and 30% gave more. **That is a plausible reason the model's data are unusually complete, and a reason to expect worse completeness in unpaid real-world use**, where the paper's own finding that EMA response days did not predict accuracy within the observed 21–30 day range offers no reassurance about shorter records.

**11. No power calculation, and the sample-size justification is a precedent argument.** *"The sample size was informed by previous EEG and ML studies in alcohol-related populations with comparable sample sizes."* **That is honest but circular** — it establishes that 60 is conventional in this literature, not that 60 suffices for the question. With a reconstructed per-person standard deviation of the multimodal gain of about **0.17**, a sample of 60 detects a gain of roughly 0.045 or larger; the observed 0.11 clears that comfortably, **so the comparison the paper makes is adequately powered even though no calculation is reported.** The problem is elsewhere: in what was not compared.

**12. No reporting guideline, no registration — and a fifth consecutive distinct pattern.** Entry 83 named none and met TRIPOD's substance; 84 claimed STROBE and TRIPOD and missed TRIPOD's core items; 85 named none and landed between; 86 named none and met the modelling items while ignoring measurement validity. **Entry 87 names none and is different again: the data-handling and evaluation items are met unusually well** — leakage control, metric justification, stability across restarts, overfitting diagnostics, honest limitations — **while the comparator and generalisation items are not addressed at all.** **Five consecutive entries, five combinations of claim and substance. The rule stands: the checklist is a claim about intent. Read the methods.**

## 5. Statistics Spotlight

Three concepts, numbered from 361 in `queue.md`. The first is the most transferable thing in this entry; the second is the gap at the paper's centre.

**A confidence interval across random restarts measures the algorithm's stability, not the study's uncertainty — and you can usually reconstruct the right one.**

**This is the single most useful thing to take from this paper, and it is a reading skill, not a criticism.**

Table 2 reports the multimodal model's accuracy as **0.56, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.552 to 0.560.** That interval is **eight thousandths wide**. From 60 people. **If that were the study's precision, it would be among the most precise estimates in this entire reading list.**

**It isn't, and the paper says what it is — in words easy to skim past.** *"Each model was trained 100 times with different random seed values... Performance metrics were summarized across the 100 runs using the mean, SD, and 95% CI of the mean."*

**So here is what varied and what did not.** A neural network starts with random numbers in it; train it twice and you get slightly different models. The researchers trained theirs **100 times**, on **the same 60 people, the same split, the same 41 features**, and reported how much the score bounced. **The answer is: hardly at all.** That is genuinely worth knowing — it means the result is not a lucky restart. **But the people never changed.** **The interval answers "if I ran this again on these data, would I get the same number?" It does not answer "if I ran this on 60 different people, would I get the same number?"** And the second question is the one almost every reader has.

**Why the second interval is always much wider.** Seed-to-seed variation is one small source of noise. Person-to-person variation is a different and far larger one — different people, different craving patterns, different brains. **Averaging over 100 restarts shrinks the first kind toward zero while leaving the second untouched.** Report only the first and you can print an interval as narrow as you like simply by running more seeds.

**Now the useful part: you can often recover the honest interval from numbers the paper already gives.** This paper reports a **paired t test across its 60 participants, t = 5.00**, for a multimodal gain of about **0.11**. A t statistic is just the estimate divided by its standard error, so:

- standard error = 0.11 ÷ 5.00 = **0.022**
- 95% interval = 0.11 ± about 2.0 × 0.022 = **0.066 to 0.154**

**That span is 0.088 — about eleven times the printed interval for the accuracy, and twenty-two times the printed interval for the unimodal accuracy.** And the honest reading of the gain is not "11 points, give or take half a point" but **"somewhere between about 7 and 15 points."** Still a real gain. **Just not a precise one.**

**And you can check such a reconstruction, which is what makes this more than arithmetic.** The same numbers imply a person-to-person standard deviation in the gain of about **0.17**, which in turn implies that roughly **26% of participants — about 15.6 of 60 — should have done *worse* with the multimodal model.** **The paper independently reports that 43 of 60 did better, leaving 17 who did not. Predicted about 15.6; observed at most 17.** The reconstruction is sound.

**The everyday version.** You weigh yourself on the same scale ten times in a row and the readings agree to within 50 grams. **You have learned that your scale is consistent. You have learned nothing about how much a different person weighs.** Reporting the ten readings' spread as your uncertainty about human body weight would be exactly this error.

**Watch out for:** any paper reporting intervals or standard deviations "across runs", "across seeds", "across repetitions" or "across initialisations" — common in machine-learning papers, and increasingly in clinical ones. **Three steps. Ask what varied between the runs: if it was only the random numbers, the interval is a stability measure.** **Then ask whether a sampling interval appears anywhere, and if it does not, reconstruct one from any participant-level test statistic the paper reports.** **And treat an interval narrower than 1/√n of the outcome's spread as a warning sign by itself** — here, with 60 participants, nothing about accuracy can honestly be pinned to ±0.004.

**For a next-day forecast, the comparator is persistence: "tomorrow will be like today."**

**This paper's title is about forecasting, and the test that would establish forecasting skill was never run.**

**What persistence means.** The simplest possible forecast of a thing that changes slowly is **to predict no change.** Tomorrow's craving equals today's. It needs no model, no brainwaves, no neural network — just last night's answer. **In weather forecasting this is the standard first hurdle: a forecast that cannot beat persistence has not demonstrated skill, however sophisticated it is.** The same logic applies to tomorrow's blood pressure, next month's weight, next quarter's sales.

**Why it matters acutely here.** The paper's introduction says craving *"was shown to predict subsequent alcohol use"* and that the link *"appears to be strongest over short time frames"* — **i.e. craving persists.** And the paper's own post hoc analysis is almost a direct measurement of how much: **the more a person's craving varied, the worse both models did, at ρ = −0.549 and ρ = −0.671, both highly significant.**

**Turn that around.** **Both models did best on the people whose craving sat nearly still.** Those are exactly the people for whom "same as yesterday" is almost always right. **So part — possibly a large part — of the reported 45% and 56% could be the model learning to repeat a number that was not going to change anyway.** There is no way to tell from what is reported, and **a single line of code on data already collected would settle it.**

**The half that defends the paper, which must be said.** The paper also checked whether the **size of the multimodal advantage** related to craving variability, and **it did not — no participant characteristic predicted the gain.** If the whole effect were "the model learned each person's usual level", you would expect the advantage to be concentrated among the steady people. It is not. **So the extra brain and heart information is doing something real; what remains untested is how much either model beats doing nothing at all.**

**A related reporting lesson from the same paper, and a credit.** This paper **does** give base rates for accuracy — *"a random classifier would achieve approximately 20%... a majority-class classifier would yield approximately 37%."* **That is exactly right, and this reading list has had to compute that comparison itself for five entries running.** But it gives **no** base rate for its error-size metric. **Filling that in changes a verdict:** reconstructing from the paper's stated craving distribution, always guessing the commonest answer would miss by about **0.83** on average — against the unimodal model's **0.90**. **So the unimodal model beats the do-nothing baseline on accuracy and loses to it on error size.** Two metrics, opposite conclusions. **The multimodal model (0.68) beats it on both.**

**The everyday version.** Someone claims a system that predicts tomorrow's weather correctly 70% of the time in a city where it is sunny most days. **Impressive — until you learn that saying "same as today" is right 68% of the time there.** The number was never the question; the number *above the easy answer* was.

**Watch out for:** any "next-day", "next-week", "next-visit" or "short-term" prediction claim. **Two questions. What does the carry-forward guess score — and if the paper does not say, can you estimate it?** **And is a base rate given for *every* reported metric, or only for the flattering one?** **The specific tell: when a paper reports that performance was worse for cases with more variability in the outcome, it has handed you strong evidence that persistence would be a serious competitor — and that it did not run it.**

**A within-person time split prevents cheating with the future but tests nothing about a new person.**

**Two different things can go wrong when you split data, and this paper fixes one of them thoroughly while leaving the other untouched.**

**Problem one: leakage from the future.** If you train on days scattered through the month and test on days in the middle, the model has seen what happened *after* the day it is predicting. In real use that information does not exist. **This paper handles it correctly and says so:** *"For each participant, the last 5 days were used as the test set, and the preceding 3 days were used as the validation set"*, so that *"only data available at the time of prediction are used for parameter estimation."* The feature selection also excluded the final 8 days per person. **That is right, and many papers get it wrong.**

**Problem two: generalisation to a new person.** The paper is explicit that it split *"along the temporal dimension for each participant, rather than participant-level splitting."* **Every one of the 60 people in the test set also appears in the training set, via their own earlier days.** **The model has never once predicted for someone it has not already met.**

**Why that matters more here than it usually would.** The entire mechanism of the multimodal model is to **initialise its internal state with who this person is** — brainwaves, heart measures and questionnaire scores compressed into a starting summary, *"allowing individual-specific baseline characteristics to directly condition temporal predictions."* **So the model has both a description of the person and that person's own history to learn from.** **Two explanations for its advantage are therefore tangled together and cannot be separated by this design:** the modalities might carry genuine information about craving, **or** the model might simply have learned where each particular person's craving usually sits. **For a brand-new patient, only the first explanation would help** — and that case was never tested.

**What makes this fair rather than damning: it depends what you are claiming.** For a **personalised** tool that collects a few weeks of your own data before helping you, training on your own history is exactly the intended design, and the paper's just-in-time-intervention framing fits that. **For a tool that works on a new patient from day one, the evidence here is silent.** The paper does not distinguish these, and lists only *"single South Korean cohort without external validation"* — which is about a different population, not about a different individual within the same one.

**The everyday version.** A tutor who has taught your child for three weeks predicts next week's test scores well. **That does not tell you whether the same tutor can predict a new child's scores.** Both are useful things to know; they are not the same thing, and only one was measured.

**Watch out for:** any study with **repeated measurements per person**. **Three questions. Was the split by time, by person, or both?** **If by time only, has any new individual ever been predicted?** **And does the model receive person-level information — static features, an identifier, a person-specific starting state — that would let it learn individuals rather than the phenomenon?** **The gold standard where both matter is a nested split: hold out whole people, and within the people you keep, respect time.** This paper did the second half.

## 6. Jargon Translator

**Alcohol craving** — the subjective pull toward a drink. Measured here by one nightly question: *"How much did you want to drink alcohol today?"*, from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very much).

**Problematic alcohol use** — a spectrum from heavy binge drinking to diagnosable disorder. **These participants were not in formal treatment**, with a mean AUDIT score of 18.15.

**AUDIT (Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test)** — a 10-item screening questionnaire for hazardous drinking. **Mean 18.15 here, indicating a genuinely affected sample.**

**Relapse** — returning to drinking after a period of abstinence. The clinical event the whole exercise is aimed at preventing.

**Ecological momentary assessment (EMA)** — asking people about their state repeatedly in daily life rather than once in a clinic, to catch things that move day to day.

**Digital phenotype** — behavioural measures captured from a phone. Here the daily self-reports; in other studies also passive sensors like GPS or step counts.

**Just-in-time adaptive intervention** — help delivered at the moment it is needed, chosen from your current state. **The proposed use for this model.**

**Electroencephalography (EEG)** — recording electrical activity from scalp electrodes. **64 channels, 10 minutes, with only the eyes-closed portion analysed.**

**Relative power (delta, theta, alpha, beta, gamma)** — how much of the brain's electrical activity sits in each frequency band, as a share of the total. **Beta activity has been linked to alcohol craving in earlier work.**

**Heart rate variability (HRV)** — the variation in time between heartbeats, a window on the automatic nervous system. **Higher variability generally indicates better self-regulatory capacity.** The strongest single contributor here.

**Static versus dynamic features** — measured once (EEG, HRV, questionnaires) versus measured repeatedly (the daily app answers). **The paper's whole architecture is about combining the two.**

**Recurrent neural network (RNN)** — a model that reads a sequence in order, carrying a running summary called the **hidden state**. A **"vanilla"** RNN is the simplest kind; **LSTM** and **GRU** are more elaborate versions built for longer sequences.

**Hidden state initialisation** — what the running summary contains before any data arrive. **Zero in the unimodal model; a compressed description of the person in the multimodal one. This is the paper's key idea and the source of its main interpretive limit.**

**Dropout** — randomly switching off part of the network during training so it cannot lean on any one input. **20% here.**

**Early stopping** — halting training when performance on held-out data stops improving, to prevent memorisation.

**Sliding window / lookback window** — how many past days the model reads to predict the next one. **Four days here, with 2, 4, 6 and 8 compared in an unreachable supplement.**

**P >> N** — more measured features than people. **394 baseline features on 60 participants. The paper names this problem explicitly.**

**Random forest feature importance** — using an ensemble of decision trees to rank which inputs matter, then keeping the top ones.

**Data leakage** — when information the model should not have at prediction time sneaks into training. **Handled carefully here for time, not applicable for persons because no person was held out.**

**Five-class classification** — predicting which of five ordered levels, rather than a yes/no.

**Ordinal outcome** — categories with a natural order, where being off by one is better than being off by three. **Why mean absolute error belongs in this paper's metric set.**

**Accuracy** — share of predictions exactly right. **0.56 multimodal, 0.45 unimodal, against 0.37 for always guessing the commonest answer and 0.20 for random.**

**Weighted versus macro F1** — two ways to summarise precision and recall across five classes. **Weighted lets common classes dominate (0.51); macro counts every class equally (0.37). The 14-point gap is the rare classes being predicted badly.**

**Mean absolute error (MAE)** — how far off the prediction was on average, in craving levels. **0.68 multimodal, 0.90 unimodal.**

**Majority-class / base-rate baseline** — the score from always predicting the commonest answer. **The paper supplies it for accuracy; I reconstructed it for MAE (about 0.83).**

**Persistence baseline** — predicting no change from today. **Not computed in this paper, and the key omission.**

**Paired t test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test** — tests for a difference measured twice in the same individuals. **Both run here, with the reason stated.**

**Spearman correlation (ρ)** — a rank-based association measure, from −1 to +1. **−0.549 and −0.671 for craving variability against accuracy.**

**Random seed** — the starting random numbers. **100 different seeds here, which is good practice — but varying them does not vary the people.**

## 7. What You Can (and Can't) Say

**You can say:**

"In 60 adults with problematic alcohol use in Seoul, followed prospectively for 28 days with a daily app survey plus one-time EEG, HRV and psychological assessment, a small recurrent neural network predicting the next day's craving level achieved 56% exact accuracy when baseline features initialised its hidden state, against 45% using the app data alone."

"The authors report that a random classifier would achieve about 20% and a majority-class classifier about 37% in this five-class task."

"Average prediction error fell from 0.90 to 0.68 craving levels, and the macro F1 score from 0.24 to 0.37, with the multimodal model performing better in 43 of 60 participants (72%); both a Wilcoxon signed-rank test and a paired t test gave P < .001."

"In a modality ablation, HRV alone gave the largest single-block gain (0.45 to 0.53), EEG alone 0.52, and psychological features alone 0.50."

"Correct classification of the highest craving level rose from 0% to 33%, while performance at level 4 remained limited."

"Greater within-person craving variability was associated with lower accuracy in both models (ρ = −0.549 and −0.671, P < .001), though the authors describe these analyses as exploratory and not causal."

"Each model was trained 100 times with different random seeds, and performance was summarised across runs."

"The authors state that performance was modest, that the study was a single South Korean cohort without external validation, and that alternative architectures were not evaluated."

**You cannot say:**

"The model forecasts next-day craving." **Not demonstrated. No persistence baseline — "tomorrow equals today" — was computed**, and the paper's own finding that accuracy falls as craving becomes more variable means both models performed best where that trivial guess would also perform best.

"The model is 56% accurate at predicting craving, give or take half a percent." **The printed interval of 0.552–0.560 is a spread across 100 random restarts on one fixed split.** Reconstructed from the paper's own paired t test, the participant-level interval for the gain spans about 0.066 to 0.154 — **roughly eleven times wider.**

"This could identify high-risk moments for intervention." **The model is worst at exactly the high craving levels such a trigger would use** — 33% at level 5, limited at level 4 — and no performance figure at any threshold is reported.

"Brainwaves and heart measures predict craving." **In this design that cannot be separated from the model having learned each participant's own typical craving level from their own earlier days**, because every test participant was also a training participant.

"The model would work for a new patient." **Never tested.** No participant was ever held out; the split was by time within each person.

"Adding all three modalities gives the benefit of all three." **The single-modality gains sum to 0.20 against a combined gain of 0.11**, so about half of each block's apparent contribution is information the others already carry.

"The unimodal model beats doing nothing." **It depends which metric you read.** On accuracy, 0.45 against 0.37. On average error, 0.90 against a reconstructed modal-class baseline of about 0.83 — **worse.**

"1,673 craving reports means a large dataset." **The independent unit is the person: 60.** The test set is at most 300 person-days drawn from those same 60 people.

"The findings generalise to people with alcohol use disorder in treatment." **Participants were explicitly not receiving formal treatment**, were recruited by online advertisement, and were paid in proportion to app use.

"Craving was reliably measured." **It was one Likert item, with no test-retest or reliability estimate reported** — and the paper's own evidence that accuracy depends on how much that item moves makes its measurement properties load-bearing.

**The honest one-sentence version:** *a careful, honestly-reported small prospective study with unusually good statistical housekeeping — base rates supplied, 100 restarts, four justified metrics, tight overfitting control and a limitations section that calls its own performance modest — whose stated claim that baseline brain, heart and psychological data improve on app data alone is properly tested and supported, but whose title claim of forecasting is not, because the persistence comparator was never run, no new individual was ever predicted, and the intervals on display understate the real uncertainty roughly elevenfold.*

## 8. Bottom Line for Your Life

**If you are cutting down on drinking or worried about relapse:** nothing here is a tool you can use, and the researchers do not claim otherwise. **But one thing in this study is worth taking seriously regardless of the AI.** Sixty people answered one question every evening — *how much did you want to drink today?* — for four weeks, and those answers turned out to contain real structure: enough that the daily pattern alone beat guessing, and enough that how much someone's craving moved around was the strongest single thing determining whether anything could be predicted at all. **Keeping a simple nightly record of your own craving, on a 1-to-5 scale, costs almost nothing and gives you something no clinician can reconstruct from memory in a 15-minute appointment.** If you take one practical thing from this paper, that is it — and it does not require an app, a model, or anyone's brainwaves.

**If you are in recovery and someone offers you a craving-prediction app:** ask two questions. **Does it need a few weeks of your own data before it says anything?** If so, that is consistent with this kind of model and not unreasonable. **And what does it do when craving is high?** This model gets the top level right about a third of the time and the second-highest level worse than that — **so an alert built on it would stay quiet on most of the days that matter.** That is not a reason to dismiss the idea; it is a reason not to rely on silence from such a system as reassurance.

**If you work in digital mental health, two things from this paper are worth copying and one is worth avoiding.** **Copy the base rates.** The authors wrote, in the Results, what a random guess and a guess-the-commonest-answer would score. **That one sentence lets every reader convert 56% into "19 points above doing nothing", and this reading list has had to compute that comparison itself for five papers in a row.** **Copy the 100 restarts** — training a small network once and reporting the number is not a result, and this paper shows how little effort the fix takes. **Avoid the missing comparator.** For any next-day, next-week or next-visit prediction, **report what carrying the last value forward would score, in every metric you report.** It is one line of code, and without it a forecasting claim is not established.

**The reading skill worth taking from this entry, and it will serve you well beyond this paper.** When you see a confidence interval in a machine-learning paper, **check what was varied to produce it.** This paper's intervals span four to seventeen thousandths — astonishing precision from 60 people — because they describe what happens when you restart the same model on the same data. **Honest intervals for the underlying question are about eleven times wider here, and I could reconstruct them from the paper's own t statistic in two lines of arithmetic: standard error equals the estimate divided by t, and the interval is the estimate plus or minus twice that.** **A rough alarm that needs no arithmetic at all: with 60 people, no interval about an accuracy can honestly be narrower than a couple of percentage points.** If one is, it is measuring something else.

**And the forecasting question in general.** Anyone who claims to predict tomorrow's anything — blood sugar, mood, pain, sales, rainfall — should be asked what "tomorrow will be like today" would have scored. **For things that change slowly, that trivial guess is a formidable competitor, and beating it is the whole achievement.** **The specific tell, which this paper hands you: if a study reports that it did worse for people whose outcome varied more, it has just told you that persistence would be a serious rival, and that it did not test it.**

**A last word on what the paper gets right that is easy to overlook.** It says, in its own limitations, *"the overall predictive performance was modest."* **Authors rarely write that sentence.** It also declined to invent missing days rather than imputing them into a smooth-looking time series, collected the daily survey whether or not craving occurred rather than only when something happened, named both IRBs, disclosed what participants were paid, and explained why each of its four metrics was needed. **That is a paper trying to be read accurately rather than impressively, and it is why the concerns above can be stated so precisely — the numbers to check were all there.**

**If you are reading the coverage:** expect "AI predicts alcohol cravings from brainwaves and a smartphone." **Four things will likely be missing.** That the headline accuracy is 56% in a task where always guessing the same answer scores 37%. That no comparison was made against simply assuming tomorrow will be like today. That every person tested was also a person the model trained on. And that the model is least reliable at the high craving levels any real alert would be built on.

**This is education, not medical advice.** Alcohol use disorder is a treatable medical condition, and effective treatments exist. Nothing described here is validated for clinical use, and decisions about drinking, treatment or relapse prevention belong with a clinician.

**All three pre-registered questions, answered.** Set on 2026-11-23, written down before reading.

**(1) "Was the cross-validation split by PARTICIPANT or by observation — and if by participant, how many participants are there, as distinct from how many person-days?"** — **Neither, and the answer is more interesting than the question.** The split is **by TIME within each participant**, stated twice and unambiguously: *"For each participant, the last 5 days were used as the test set, and the preceding 3 days were used as the validation set"*, and *"the dataset was split along the temporal dimension for each participant, rather than participant-level splitting."* **This is the correct guard against future leakage, which is what the question was aimed at, and the paper also kept feature selection away from the final 8 days per person and fitted the scaler on training data only.** **But it means every test participant was also a training participant, so generalisation to a new individual is never tested** — and because the multimodal architecture exists specifically to initialise the hidden state with *"individual-specific baseline characteristics"*, the model's advantage and "it learned this person's usual craving level" cannot be separated. **The paper lists single-cohort generalisation as a limitation but never names the new-individual gap.** On the counting: **the independent unit is the participant, n = 60**; the test set is at most **300 person-days** and the whole dataset **1,673 craving reports**, all drawn from those same 60 people.

**(2) "What is the comparator: does the model beat simply carrying forward today's craving score?"** — **NO SUCH COMPARISON EXISTS.** I searched the full text for persistence, carry-forward, naive, last-observation and previous-day baselines; **the only comparator anywhere is multimodal versus unimodal.** **And the paper's own post hoc result makes the omission acute: accuracy fell as within-person craving variability rose, at ρ = −0.549 (unimodal) and ρ = −0.671 (multimodal), both P < .001 — so both models performed best precisely where "same as yesterday" would also perform best.** **The defence, which must be stated: the paper found no association between any participant characteristic and the SIZE of the multimodal gain**, so the gain itself is not obviously a level-learning artefact — but that defends the comparison the paper makes, not the forecasting claim in its title. **Credit where it is due: the paper DOES give base rates for accuracy** — *"a random classifier would achieve an accuracy of approximately 20%... and a majority-class classifier would yield approximately 37%"* — **which is technique 351 performed voluntarily, and the first time in this run that a paper supplied the base-rate comparison this list has had to compute for itself five entries running.**

**(3) "Is there any test-retest or reliability estimate for the craving measure itself, given that it is the outcome?"** — **NONE.** The outcome is a **single EMA item**, *"How much did you want to drink alcohol today?"*, on a 1–5 Likert scale, treated as a 5-class target. **No reliability, test-retest, or measurement-error estimate is reported anywhere.** The design is sound in principle — craving is subjective and a person's own report is the only reference standard there could be — **but single-item self-reports have measurement properties, and here they are load-bearing**: any night-to-night noise in the item caps achievable accuracy, and the paper's own variability-versus-accuracy correlation cannot distinguish genuine craving swings from noise in the instrument. **The hardest form of the reference-standard question (294, 317, 329), and unaddressed.**

**The decisive quantitative finding of the day, verified two ways.** **The reported confidence intervals are stability intervals, roughly eleven times narrower than the sampling intervals a reader will assume.** Table 2's multimodal accuracy reads **0.56 (95% CI 0.552–0.560)**, a width of **0.008** on 60 participants; the paper labels it correctly as the *"95% CI of the mean"* across **100 random seeds (2026–2125)** but never says which uncertainty it omits. **Reconstructing from the paper's own paired t test — t(59) = 5.00 on a gain of 0.11 — gives SE = 0.11/5.00 = 0.0220, a per-participant SD of 0.0220 × √60 = 0.170, and a 95% interval for the multimodal gain of [0.066, 0.154], width 0.088** — **11.0× the printed multimodal accuracy interval and 22.0× the unimodal one (width 0.004).** **And the reconstruction is independently corroborated: a gain distributed as N(0.11, 0.170²) implies P(gain < 0) = 0.259, i.e. about 15.6 of 60 participants doing worse — against the paper's separately reported 43/60 (72%) doing better, leaving 17 (28.3%) who did not.** Predicted ≈15.6, observed ≤17.

**The second reconstruction: accuracy and MAE disagree about whether the unimodal model has skill.** The paper gives a majority-class baseline for accuracy (~37%) and **none for mean absolute error**. From the stated craving distribution — **mean 2.21, SD 1.13, median 2, IQR 1–3, modal share ~0.37** — I reconstructed five label distributions satisfying **all four** moments simultaneously; every one gave a modal-class MAE of **0.83 or 0.84**. **Against the unimodal model's MAE of 0.90, which is WORSE than always guessing the same number**, while its accuracy of 0.45 beats the same baseline's 0.37. **The multimodal model's 0.68 beats it on both metrics.** **Flagged as my estimate, not the paper's figure** — Figure S3, which holds the actual distribution, is unreachable.

**Positive practice worth carrying forward.** **The base rates, supplied in the Results where they belong** (361's companion; technique 351 done voluntarily). **Four metrics, each with an explicit, pedagogically sound justification** — why accuracy misleads under imbalance, why weighted F1 *"may be biased toward the majority class"*, why macro F1 ensures *"the model is penalized if it performs poorly"* on minority classes, and why MAE suits an ordinal target. **100 random restarts with mean, SD and interval** — the direct remedy for technique 321. **Temporal leakage controlled at three separate points** (split, feature selection excluding the final 8 days, scaler fitted on training only) **and the principle stated**. **Missing days deliberately NOT imputed**, with the reason given: imputation *"may introduce unrealistic temporal dependencies and bias the learning process."* **Daily surveys completed regardless of whether craving occurred**, explicitly avoiding event-contingent sampling bias. **A modality ablation that is actually used to say something useful.** **A window-length sensitivity analysis** across 2, 4, 6 and 8 days (supplement unreachable). **Paired, participant-level significance testing with both a parametric and a non-parametric test, and the reason for running both.** **Post hoc correlations labelled exploratory and "should not be interpreted as causal."** **An architecture choice justified in advance on sample-size grounds, with LSTM and transformer alternatives explicitly declined for the stated reason.** **Dual IRB approval, pseudonymisation, and compensation disclosed in won and dollars.** **And six substantive limitations, including the sentence most authors will not write: "the overall predictive performance was modest."**
