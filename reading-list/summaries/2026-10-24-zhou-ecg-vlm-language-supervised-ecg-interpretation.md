## 1. The Gist

An electrocardiogram — an ECG — is the squiggly heart tracing with twelve different views of the same heartbeat. Reading one properly takes training, and there are not enough cardiologists to go round.

Most AI built for ECGs works one disease at a time: you want it to spot atrial fibrillation, you gather thousands of ECGs labelled for atrial fibrillation and train a model just for that. Want a second condition? Start again.

This team tried something different. Hospitals already produce ECGs with a written summary attached, by the million, as a by-product of normal care. So the researchers took **800,034 ECGs with their reports**, turned each tracing into a picture, and trained a model to learn which pictures go with which words. No disease labels. Just pictures and the sentences that happened to accompany them.

Afterwards you can ask it about a condition by typing the condition's name — and it will answer, even though it was never specifically trained on that condition.

It works, unevenly. For **rhythm problems** — the heart beating too fast, too slow, or irregularly — it was genuinely good, and stayed good on a completely separate database of 45,151 ECGs from different patients with diagnoses confirmed by doctors. For **shape-of-the-waveform problems**, the subtler kind, it was much weaker.

Two things are worth knowing before anyone gets excited. The selling point of a picture-based ECG model is that real hospitals keep ECGs as printouts, scans and phone photos — but **this model was never shown one of those.** Every image was cleanly generated by computer from digital data. And although the model is described as needing no task-specific training, **the final yes/no cut-off for each condition still had to be set using labelled examples of that condition.** Both of these the authors state themselves, repeatedly and without being pushed.

## 2. Study Snapshot

**Study type:** a **model development and external validation study** in machine learning for medical imaging. There is no patient cohort here in the clinical sense — no one was recruited, treated or followed. It is a retrospective analysis of two existing ECG databases. On the evidence ladder this is a methods study: it can show that a model distinguishes conditions on stored recordings, and nothing about whether using it changes care.

**Training and internal testing:** the **MIMIC-IV-ECG** database — **800,034 paired 12-lead ECG recordings and machine-generated summary reports from 161,352 unique patients**, each recording 10 seconds at 500 Hz. That is about **5 ECGs per patient**, which is why the next detail matters: **the dataset was partitioned at the patient level** into training, validation and internal test sets, so the same person's recordings cannot appear on both sides.

**External validation:** the **LSAS-ECG** database — **45,151 12-lead recordings** with **physician-adjudicated diagnoses** encoded in SNOMED-CT. A genuinely independent dataset, about 5.6% the size of the development corpus, with a younger population and broadly similar sex distribution.

**And a difference between the two that governs how they can be compared.** The internal labels were **extracted from the machine-generated reports by rule-based text matching** — looking for condition names, abbreviations and synonyms and converting them to yes/no. The external labels were **adjudicated by physicians**. So "ground truth" means two different things in the two halves, and the authors say so plainly.

**What was built:** **ECG-VLM**, adapted from **CLIP** — a model originally trained on internet image-and-caption pairs to learn which pictures match which words. Here it has a **Vision Transformer (ViT-B/32) image encoder** and a **Transformer text encoder** (12 layers, hidden dimension 512, 8 attention heads, 77-token limit). Both project into a shared space where matching images and texts land close together. The pre-trained CLIP weights were the starting point, and the whole network was fine-tuned on the ECG pairs.

**How the images were made — the study's central caveat.** Raw waveforms were downsampled to 100 Hz, bandpass-filtered (fourth-order Butterworth, 0.5–50 Hz), normalised lead by lead, and rendered as **224 × 224-pixel images**. These are clean computer-generated pictures of the signal, not photographs of paper, not scans, not phone snaps.

**Training:** symmetric contrastive loss over all image-text pairs in each batch of 256, with a learnable temperature; stochastic gradient descent, momentum 0.9, 30 epochs; checkpoint with the lowest validation loss selected.

**A self-audit worth noting:** the authors checked how often reports were cut off by the 77-token limit. Median report length 19 tokens, 99th percentile 68, and **only 2,648 of 800,034 reports (0.33%) were truncated** — which reconciles exactly.

**How diagnosis works:** for each condition, a positive prompt is set against the negative prompt "other", both encoded as text, and the ECG image's similarity to each is turned into a probability. **No retraining.** New condition, new sentence.

**The threshold, and the asterisk the authors put on their own headline.** For each condition the cut-off was chosen on the **validation set**: among thresholds reaching **sensitivity of at least 0.80**, take the one with the highest F1 score. The paper then states, unprompted: this "requires labeled validation examples for each cardiac condition and therefore constitutes condition-specific calibration, distinct from the zero-shot ranking evaluation reflected by AUROC and AUPRC."

**Statistics:** 95% confidence intervals by **stratified bootstrap resampling, 1,000 iterations**, preserving class distribution; fixed random seed.

**Funding / competing interests / ethics statement:** **not reported in the text provided.**

### The results

**Across 14 cardiac conditions**, internal test set: **AUROC 0.679 to 0.981**, AUPRC 0.130 to 0.877, sensitivity 0.791 to 0.936, precision 0.105 to 0.786, F1 0.186 to 0.838. External: **AUROC 0.691 to 0.991**, AUPRC 0.058 to 0.982, sensitivity 0.633 to 0.945, precision **0.016** to 0.952, F1 0.032 to 0.940.

**Notice the pattern.** The AUROC range barely moves between internal and external. The AUPRC, precision and F1 ranges blow open at the bottom.

**By category** (mean AUROC, internal then external):
- **Rhythm abnormalities** — atrial fibrillation, supraventricular tachycardia, sinus tachycardia, sinus bradycardia, premature ventricular and atrial complexes: **0.948 → 0.921.**
- **Conduction disorders** — atrioventricular block, first-degree AV block, right and left bundle branch block: **0.858 → 0.843.**
- **Morphological conditions** — left ventricular hypertrophy, ST-T change, prolonged QT, low QRS voltage: **0.741 → 0.750.**

Five of the six rhythm conditions cleared AUROC 0.90 on both datasets. Morphological conditions were the only category that did not fall externally — because they were already the lowest.

**Against other models.** Compared with the team's own earlier signal-based **ECG-CLIP**: comparable for rhythm, ECG-CLIP better for conduction disorders, ECG-VLM better (higher AUPRC) for several morphological conditions, most consistently left ventricular hypertrophy and ST-T change. Compared with **two supervised models** on six shared conditions: AUROC broadly comparable; AUPRC consistently lower internally but **closing on external validation**. Note that these comparison figures were taken from the previously published results rather than re-run here.

**Retrieval — the most interesting finding in the paper.** Asked to pull the matching report for a given ECG out of a pool of 80,684, recall@1 fell from **0.413 for rare reports to 0.073 for frequent ones**. That looks like failure on common cases. It is not. Scored instead by whether the retrieved report had **identical text** to the right answer, frequent-report recall@1 rose from **0.073 to 0.282** — nearly four times higher. The model was finding the right words attached to a different patient and being marked wrong for it.

**Subgroups.** AUROC declined modestly with age: **0.891 (12–44), 0.870 (45–74), 0.831 (75+)** — a spread of 0.060. Performance was **near-identical between men and women**. Slightly lower in ICU patients than non-ICU inpatients or outpatients. The paediatric subgroup (0–11, external only) had the **highest AUROC and the lowest AUPRC** of any age group.

## 3. How Strong Is This Evidence? (Grade 3/5)

**Grade: 3/5 — solid, carefully reported machine-learning work whose practical motivation is explicitly not tested.**

**What it genuinely supports.** That a contrastive vision-language model trained on ECG images paired with routine reports learns representations transferable across 14 conditions without per-condition training, that this holds up on an independent database with physician-adjudicated labels, and that performance is strongly patterned by condition type — excellent for rhythm, poor for waveform morphology — with that pattern reproducing across both datasets and across age, sex and care setting.

**What it does not support, and does not claim to.** That this works on real-world ECG images. That is the gap worth being clear about.

**Three things hold it at 3.**

**The motivation and the experiment do not meet.** The introduction's case for image-based ECG AI is that hospitals hold ECGs as printed reports, scanned documents and photographs, with no digital waveform available. Every image in this study was **rendered by the authors from clean digital waveforms** at 224 × 224 pixels. So the method was never tested on the problem it was designed to solve. The authors are scrupulous about this — they say it in the introduction, the discussion and the limitations, and frame the work as a foundation for future extension — but a reader who skims will take away the motivation and not the caveat.

**Internally, the ground truth is a machine's own report.** Labels came from rule-based text matching over machine-generated ECG summaries. So for 14 conditions the model is being scored against an automated reading of the ECG, not against a doctor's judgement or a clinical outcome. The authors name the resulting label noise and point out that this is a second reason internal and external numbers should not be compared directly. It also means the internal AUROCs measure agreement with the ECG machine rather than diagnostic accuracy.

**Precision is very low for several conditions, by design.** The sensitivity-first threshold rule guarantees that nothing is missed and accepts whatever false-alarm rate follows. Internally that means precision of **0.105 for atrioventricular block, 0.108 for supraventricular tachycardia, 0.118 for prolonged QT** — roughly nine or ten flagged for every real case. Externally the minimum is **0.016**, which is about 62 flagged per real case. The authors draw the right conclusion: the model "is better suited to prioritizing recordings for clinician review than to autonomous diagnosis."

**Two smaller points.** The external validation is a **single cohort**, which the authors list as a limitation. And the comparisons against ECG-CLIP and the two supervised models use **figures carried over from previous publications** rather than re-run head-to-head, so those comparisons are weaker than they look.

**What pulls it up to 3 and close to a 4.**

**Patient-level splitting** on a dataset averaging five ECGs per patient — getting this wrong is one of the commonest silent errors in medical machine learning, and they got it right. **Genuine external validation on an independent database with independent, physician-adjudicated labels.** **TRIPOD+AI** named. **Bootstrap confidence intervals** with a fixed seed. **Subgroup analysis across age, sex and care setting**, reported even where it was unflattering. A **comparison against both their own prior model and two supervised models**. A **self-audit of token truncation** that nobody asked for, reported to two decimals.

And the quality of the thinking in the discussion is unusually high. They **explain why AUPRC collapsed externally** — prevalence of right bundle branch block fell from 7.9% to 1.4% and left bundle branch block from 3.4% to 0.5%, and AUPRC depends on prevalence in a way AUROC does not. They **diagnose their own retrieval metric as the problem** rather than the model. They **separate what is zero-shot from what is not**. And they resist the obvious conclusion from their own model comparison, noting that the smaller external drop "raises the possibility" of better robustness while stating the study "cannot disentangle these effects."

**Where 3/5 sits.** Good machine-learning work, honestly reported, that establishes a feasibility result rather than a clinical one. It would be a 4 if the images had been real — scanned, printed or photographed — because that single change would make the practical claim testable, and the rest of the methodology is already there.

## 4. The Editor's Concerns

**Test it on at least a small set of real ECG images.** Even a few hundred scanned or photographed ECGs, with the performance drop measured, would convert the paper's motivation into a result. As it stands, the headline capability is asserted and the evidence is about a different input.

**Retitle or reframe towards what was tested.** "Language-supervised electrocardiogram interpretation with vision transformer" invites the reading that this is about ECG images as clinicians encounter them. The body says otherwise, carefully and often; the framing should match.

**Report the per-condition numbers in the main text.** Fourteen conditions are summarised as ranges and three category means. The condition-level table is the most useful thing in the paper for anyone deciding whether to use it, and it sits in a supplement.

**Re-run the comparison models rather than citing their published figures.** Comparing against numbers from a previous paper is not a head-to-head test, particularly when the comparison is the basis for a claim about relative robustness to dataset shift.

**Show what a fixed, condition-independent threshold would do.** The authors raise this themselves. Since the per-condition threshold is the part that is not zero-shot, quantifying the cost of dropping it would show how much of the framework's practical appeal survives.

**Add funding, competing-interests and data-governance statements.** None appears in the published record reached here, and both databases carry access conditions.

**Give the prompts in the main text.** For a model whose entire interface is a sentence, the exact wording of the positive prompts is a method, not an appendix.

### What this study did well

- **Partitioned at the patient level** on a dataset with ~5 recordings per patient — the error that silently inflates a great many ECG AI papers.
- **Validated externally** on an independent database with **physician-adjudicated** labels, and named the label-provenance difference as a reason not to compare the two halves naively.
- **Named and followed TRIPOD+AI.**
- **Separated the zero-shot part from the part that is not**, in its own words, without being asked.
- Used **stratified bootstrap confidence intervals** with a fixed seed.
- **Ran subgroup analyses** by age, sex and care setting, and reported the age gradient that went against them.
- **Audited its own token truncation** (0.33% of reports) rather than assuming.
- **Explained the AUPRC collapse correctly** via the prevalence shift, with the actual prevalence figures.
- **Diagnosed its own retrieval metric** as the cause of an apparently poor result, and showed the corrected figure.
- **Declined to over-read its own favourable comparison**, saying the study "cannot disentangle these effects."
- Stated repeatedly that the model suits triage for clinician review, not autonomous diagnosis.
- Listed six substantive limitations, including the one that most undercuts the paper.

## 5. Statistics Spotlight

### Concept 1: Why one accuracy measure survived the move to a new hospital and another collapsed

This paper gives you a clean natural experiment in the difference between two measures that are often treated as interchangeable.

**What happened.** Moving from the internal test set to the external database, the **AUROC range barely shifted** — 0.679–0.981 became 0.691–0.991. The **AUPRC range fell apart at the bottom** — 0.130–0.877 became 0.058–0.982. Precision's floor dropped from 0.105 to **0.016**.

Same model. Same conditions. What changed?

**The two measures.** **AUROC** asks: take one patient who has the condition and one who does not — how often does the model score the sick one higher? It is a pure ranking question, and crucially **it does not care how many of each kind there are.** **AUPRC** summarises precision across all cut-offs, and **precision is "of the people you flagged, how many really have it"** — which depends enormously on how common the condition is.

**The paper's own explanation, with its numbers.** Right bundle branch block affected **7.9%** of the internal set and **1.4%** of the external one. Left bundle branch block fell from **3.4% to 0.5%**. The conditions became five to seven times rarer.

**A worked example.** Take a test with sensitivity 0.90 and specificity 0.90 — fixed, unchanging — and run it on 10,000 people at three prevalences:

- At **7.9%**: 790 have it. You catch 711, and wrongly flag 921 of the 9,210 who do not. Precision = 711/1,632 = **0.436**.
- At **1.4%**: 140 have it. You catch 126, and wrongly flag 986. Precision = 126/1,112 = **0.113**.
- At **0.5%**: 50 have it. You catch 45, and wrongly flag 995. Precision = 45/1,040 = **0.043**.

**The test never changed. Its AUROC never changed. Precision fell by a factor of ten.** The reason is simply that the pool of people who can generate a false alarm grew while the pool of real cases shrank. (That arithmetic is my illustration, not the paper's; the prevalence figures are theirs.)

**An everyday analogy.** A metal detector is equally good at distinguishing coins from bottle tops wherever you take it. On a beach full of dropped change, most of its beeps are coins. On a beach that has been swept clean, most of its beeps are rubbish — not because the detector got worse, but because there are fewer coins and the same number of bottle tops.

**You can see the same effect inside a subgroup here.** The paediatric group had **the highest AUROC and the lowest AUPRC** of any age band. Those are not in conflict: children were ranked well and their conditions were rare.

**Watch out.** This cuts in two directions and both are common errors.

First: **a high AUROC tells you nothing about how many of a tool's alerts will be right.** Papers lead with AUROC because it is the number that travels best between populations — which is exactly why it cannot answer the question a clinician actually has.

Second, and less often said: **a low AUPRC in a new population is not automatically evidence the model got worse.** Before concluding that, check whether the prevalence changed. The right comparison is AUPRC against the prevalence in that same dataset — a 0.058 AUPRC where the condition occurs in 0.5% of people is about ten times better than chance, while a 0.058 where it occurs in 5% is a failure. This paper's authors make exactly this point.

### Concept 2: What "no task-specific training" does and does not cover

The paper's headline is that it detects conditions without per-condition training. That claim is real, and it has a boundary that the authors draw themselves. Understanding where that boundary falls is a transferable skill.

**What genuinely needs no labels.** The model produces a score for every ECG by comparing the picture against a sentence. Ranking all the ECGs by that score requires no labelled examples at all. **AUROC and AUPRC are computed from the ranking**, sweeping across every possible cut-off. So those two numbers really are achieved without task-specific supervision — that part of the claim holds.

**What needs labels.** Sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1 and accuracy all require a decision: above this score, say yes. Picking that line is where the labels come back in. Here the rule was: **on the validation set, among thresholds reaching sensitivity of at least 0.80, take the one with the highest F1** — and that requires knowing, for each of the 14 conditions, who actually had it.

The authors state this in their own methods: the procedure "requires labeled validation examples for each cardiac condition and therefore constitutes condition-specific calibration, distinct from the zero-shot ranking evaluation reflected by AUROC and AUPRC."

**Why it matters practically.** The selling point of this architecture is that a new condition costs a sentence rather than a dataset. For *ranking* — "show me the ECGs most likely to show this" — that is true. For a yes/no answer at a sensible operating point, you still need labelled examples of the new condition, which is most of the cost you were trying to avoid.

**An everyday analogy.** Imagine a wine expert who has never been told what "corked" means, but who can reliably sort any set of bottles from least to most of whatever you describe. Hand them a new description and they will sort the shelf — no training needed, genuinely useful. But ask "is *this* bottle corked, yes or no?" and somebody has to decide where on that sorted shelf the line falls. Drawing the line requires bottles whose true state you already know. The sorting is free; the verdict is not.

**Watch out.** "Zero-shot" and "no task-specific training" are among the most slippery phrases in current medical AI. **The test: look at which metrics are reported.** If a paper claims zero-shot capability and reports sensitivity, specificity or F1, ask how the threshold was chosen. If the answer is "optimised on a validation set" — which is usually correct practice — then the ranking was zero-shot and the decision was not, and the deployment cost is higher than the headline implies. This paper is the rare one that tells you so in its own methods section. Most do not, and on this reading list four of the last six papers either chose thresholds on the data they then reported, or did not say how they chose them at all.

### Concept 3: What a precision of 0.105 means in people, and why the authors chose it deliberately

The threshold rule here was not an accident, and reading it properly tells you what the tool is for.

**The rule:** among candidate cut-offs achieving **sensitivity of at least 0.80**, pick the one with the best F1. Sensitivity first, everything else second.

**What it bought.** Internally, **sensitivity never fell below 0.791** for any of the 14 conditions. Very little was missed.

**What it cost.** Internally, precision was **0.105 for atrioventricular block, 0.108 for supraventricular tachycardia, 0.118 for prolonged QT interval**. Externally the floor was **0.016**.

**In people.** A precision of 0.105 means that of every 100 ECGs the model flags for atrioventricular block, about 10 have it and about 90 do not — roughly nine false alarms for every real case. At the external floor of 0.016, it is about 62 flagged for every real one.

**Whether that is bad depends entirely on what happens next.** If a flag means "a cardiologist glances at this tracing", nine extra glances to avoid missing a case may be an excellent trade — the glance is cheap and the miss is not. If a flag means "start treatment" or "admit", it is indefensible. The authors land in the right place: the model "is better suited to prioritizing recordings for clinician review than to autonomous diagnosis."

**A worked comparison.** Suppose a hospital reads 1,000 ECGs a day and 2% have the condition — 20 real cases. At sensitivity 0.80 and precision 0.105, the model flags about 152 ECGs and finds 16 of the 20. A human now reviews 152 tracings instead of 1,000, and 4 cases still slip through. Whether that is progress depends on what the human was doing before. If every ECG was already being read by a cardiologist, the model has added work. If none were being read until the next morning, it has usefully reordered the queue.

**An everyday analogy.** An airport scanner set to catch every blade will also beep at belt buckles, keys and underwired bras. Nobody thinks the scanner is broken, because a second check is quick and a missed blade is catastrophic. Set the same sensitivity for something where the second check is an operation, and the calculus inverts completely.

**Watch out.** **A sensitivity figure quoted without its precision, in a setting where the condition is rare, is close to meaningless** — you can always reach 100% sensitivity by flagging everything. The question to ask of any screening or triage tool is not "how accurate is it" but **"how many does it flag, and what happens to the ones it flags?"** When a paper reports a sensitivity-first threshold, as this one does openly, it is telling you the intended use is triage. Believe it, and judge the tool against that use rather than against diagnosis.

## 6. Jargon Translator

- **Electrocardiogram (ECG)** — a recording of the heart's electrical activity. A **12-lead** ECG captures twelve views of the same beats from electrodes on the chest and limbs.
- **The 14 conditions here** — rhythm: **atrial fibrillation/flutter**, **supraventricular tachycardia**, **sinus tachycardia** (fast), **sinus bradycardia** (slow), **premature ventricular** and **atrial complexes** (extra beats). Conduction: **atrioventricular block**, **first-degree AV block**, **right** and **left bundle branch block** (delays in the heart's wiring). Morphological: **left ventricular hypertrophy** (thickened muscle), **ST-T change**, **prolonged QT interval**, **low QRS voltage** — all read from the shape and size of the waveform.
- **Rhythm vs conduction vs morphology** — is the beat regular and at the right rate; does the signal travel through the heart normally; and what shape are the waves. The model was good at the first, middling at the second, weak at the third.
- **MIMIC-IV-ECG** — a large public database of de-identified hospital ECGs with machine-generated reports. **LSAS-ECG** — a separate public arrhythmia database with doctor-confirmed diagnoses.
- **SNOMED-CT** — the international clinical terminology used to encode the external database's diagnoses.
- **CLIP (Contrastive Language–Image Pretraining)** — a model trained on internet pictures and their captions to place matching images and text close together in a shared space. The starting point here.
- **Vision Transformer (ViT-B/32)** — an image model that chops a picture into patches and processes them like words in a sentence.
- **Contrastive learning** — training by pulling matched pairs together and pushing unmatched pairs apart.
- **The false-negative problem in contrastive learning** — when two different patients have identical reports, the training treats them as a mismatch and pushes them apart, even though they mean the same thing. The paper's retrieval result is a direct measurement of this.
- **Embedding / shared embedding space** — the numerical space where images and text both land, so their closeness can be measured.
- **Temperature (τ)** — a learned number controlling how sharply the model distinguishes matches from non-matches.
- **Prompt** — the sentence naming the condition you want detected. The entire user interface of this model.
- **Zero-shot** — answering a question the model was never specifically trained on. Applies here to the ranking, not to the yes/no threshold.
- **Tokenization / byte-pair encoding / 77-token context** — how text is chopped into pieces the model can read, and the limit on how much it reads. Only 0.33% of reports exceeded it.
- **Rule-based text matching** — finding condition names and synonyms in report text to create yes/no labels. Fast, scalable, and noisy.
- **AUROC** — the probability a true case is ranked above a non-case. Independent of how common the condition is.
- **AUPRC** — area under the precision-recall curve. Depends heavily on how common the condition is, which is the point of Concept 1.
- **Precision (= PPV)** — of those flagged, the share who really have it. **Sensitivity (= recall)** — of those who have it, the share flagged. **F1** — their harmonic mean.
- **Recall@k / mean reciprocal rank** — retrieval measures: how often the right answer is in the top k, and how high up the list it typically appears.
- **Bandpass filter / Butterworth** — signal processing that strips out drift and high-frequency noise before the tracing is drawn.
- **Stratified bootstrap** — resampling the data many times while keeping the proportion of cases fixed, to estimate uncertainty.
- **TRIPOD+AI** — the reporting checklist for AI-based clinical prediction models, named by the authors.

## 7. What You Can (and Can't) Say

**Fair to say:**

- "A model trained on 800,034 ECGs paired with their routine reports — with no disease labels — could then detect 14 cardiac conditions when asked in plain language, without retraining for each one."
- "It was tested on an independent database of 45,151 recordings with doctor-confirmed diagnoses, and performance held up."
- "It was much better at rhythm problems (mean AUROC 0.948 internally, 0.921 externally) than at waveform-shape problems (0.741 and 0.750)."
- "Performance was near-identical between men and women, and declined modestly with age — mean AUROC 0.891 under 45 against 0.831 over 75."
- "The images were computer-generated from digital waveforms, not scans or photographs of real ECGs, so the paper does not show the method works on ECG images as hospitals actually store them."
- "The internal labels came from automated text-matching on machine-generated reports, so internally the model is being scored against an ECG machine's own reading."
- "Precision was as low as 0.105 internally and 0.016 externally for some conditions, because the threshold was deliberately set to prioritise catching cases over avoiding false alarms."
- "Setting that threshold required labelled examples of each condition, so the 'no task-specific training' claim applies to the ranking, not to the yes/no decision."

**Not fair to say:**

- ❌ "AI can now read photographs or scans of ECGs." No photograph or scan was used. Every image was rendered from clean digital data.
- ❌ "The model diagnoses 14 heart conditions without any labelled data." The ranking needs none; the threshold for each condition needs labelled examples.
- ❌ "It matches specialist supervised models." AUROC was broadly comparable on six shared conditions, but AUPRC was consistently lower internally, and the comparison used figures carried over from earlier publications rather than a fresh head-to-head run.
- ❌ "External AUPRC of 0.058 shows the model failed there." It mostly shows the condition became much rarer — prevalence of left bundle branch block fell from 3.4% to 0.5%. AUPRC must be judged against the prevalence in that dataset.
- ❌ "It performs equally well across all heart conditions." AUROC spanned 0.679 to 0.981. Morphological conditions were consistently the weakest.
- ❌ "This could replace a cardiologist reading ECGs." At precision 0.105, about nine in ten flags are wrong. The authors say it suits prioritising recordings for review, not autonomous diagnosis.
- ❌ "The model works equally well in children." The paediatric result is one subgroup in one external dataset, with the highest AUROC and the lowest AUPRC of any age group.
- ❌ "Retrieval performance was poor for common findings." Under instance-level scoring, yes — but that scoring marks a correct report wrong for belonging to another patient. Corrected, recall@1 for frequent reports rose from 0.073 to 0.282.

## 8. Bottom Line for Your Life

Nothing here changes anything for a patient. No one's ECG was read by this model in the course of care, and the authors are explicit that it is not ready to diagnose anyone. What the work points at is real though: hospitals generate millions of ECGs with written reports attached, and that pairing is free training data that nobody had to label. If that approach matures, places without a cardiologist on hand could get a tool that flags the tracings most worth a human look — which is a different and more modest ambition than replacing the human.

For anyone appraising research, this paper is worth keeping for two habits.

**Check whether the thing tested is the thing advertised.** The case for a picture-based ECG model is that real ECGs live as printouts and phone photos. The images here were generated cleanly by computer. The authors say so four separate times and frame the work accordingly — but the distance between the motivating problem and the tested problem is the single most important fact about the paper, and it is the kind of gap that disappears entirely by the time a result reaches a headline.

**When a number collapses between datasets, ask whether the disease got rarer before concluding the model got worse.** AUROC held steady here while precision fell to 0.016, and the reason was that bundle branch block went from 7.9% of patients to 1.4%. Same model, same skill, different denominator. That one check will save you from misreading a great many validation studies — in both directions, because it also stops you accepting an impressive precision that was earned only by testing on a population stuffed with cases.
