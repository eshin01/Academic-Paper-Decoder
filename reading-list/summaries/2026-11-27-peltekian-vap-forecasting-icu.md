## 1. The Gist

**When someone is on a breathing machine in intensive care, one of the things most likely to go wrong is pneumonia — an infection in the lungs that the machine itself helps to cause. This study asked whether the numbers already flowing through the hospital's computer system could see that infection coming days before anyone diagnosed it. It could: the model sorted patients correctly about 85 times in 100. Then the researchers took it to two other hospitals' data, and the honest thing about this paper is what they report about what happened next.**

**Start with the problem, because it is a real one.** A breathing tube bypasses the body's defences, so bacteria get into the lungs. **Ventilator-associated pneumonia** is common, it keeps people in intensive care longer, and more of those people die. The numbers in this study show it starkly: patients who developed it spent a median of **28 days** in intensive care against **9 days** for those who did not, and **46.0% of them died against 22.4%**.

**And catching it early is genuinely hard.** As the paper puts it, *"subtle physiologic changes may be masked by the complexity of critical illness."* Everyone in an intensive care unit is already abnormal. So doctors tend to notice pneumonia **after** the patient visibly deteriorates. The appeal of predicting it is not academic: if you knew which patients were heading for it, you could use preventive antibiotics on them specifically — rather than on everyone, which breeds resistance, or on no one.

**What makes this study unusually trustworthy, and it is worth understanding why.** Almost every study of this kind defines "pneumonia" from a billing code or a doctor's note — labels that are late, vague and often wrong. **This study did something much better.** At Northwestern, when a ventilated patient starts going downhill, the standard practice is to pass a camera into the lungs and wash out a sample — a **bronchoalveolar lavage**, or BAL. That sample goes to a laboratory, and then **a panel of reviewers decides, case by case, whether this was really pneumonia.** The paper calls this a *"gold-standard VAP diagnosis."*

**So the cohort is: 507 ventilated adults who all had this procedure. 261 (51.5%) turned out to have pneumonia. 246 (48.5%) did not.** I verified those add to 507, and so does every single row of the baseline table.

**And here is the design choice I want to single out, because it answers an objection I had written down before reading.** My worry was this: if the model is predicting "pneumonia within five days of the lung wash," and the lung wash happens when a **doctor decides to investigate**, then the model might just be learning to predict the doctor. **But look at who the comparison group is: patients who had exactly the same investigation and turned out not to have pneumonia.** The paper calls them *"negative control cases."* **The doctor's decision to investigate is held constant across both groups** — so what the model distinguishes is, among patients a doctor already suspected, who actually had it. **That is a genuinely clever use of an existing cohort.**

**What the model found.** Using **36 things measured every day** — vital signs, ventilator settings, blood tests, inflammation markers — the models reached an **AUROC of 0.866** for predicting pneumonia within 7 days and **0.843** within 5 days. An AUROC of 0.85 means: pick one patient who will get pneumonia and one who won't, and the model ranks them in the right order about 85 times out of 100.

**Now the three things I found that a reader should know, and the paper gets credit on all three for raising most of them first.**

**First, the strongest single predictor is how long the patient has already been on the ventilator — and that is partly circular.** To develop ventilator pneumonia, you have to be on a ventilator long enough to develop it. The patients who got pneumonia had been ventilated a median of **27 days**; those who did not, **6 days**. So "days on ventilator" is partly a *consequence* of when the outcome happened, not just a cause of the risk.

**The paper does the right thing here, and it is the test I would have asked for: they took the feature out and re-ran the model.** Performance fell from **0.843 to 0.734**. So about **0.11 of the AUROC came from duration alone** — and 0.734 is the more honest number for the question "among patients who have been ventilated a similar length of time, who is heading for pneumonia?" The paper also says plainly: *"Duration of ventilation likely captures cumulative time at risk."*

**Second — and this is the finding I think matters most — the model's ability to *rank* patients travelled to another hospital, but its ability to *decide* did not.** In **MIMIC-IV**, a database of 2,681 ventilated patients from a Boston hospital, the AUROC held up well: **0.817**. The paper highlights this, reasonably.

**But look at what the same model's accuracy was in that hospital: 20.9%.** Internally it was 78.9%. And pneumonia was much rarer there — about **9.4%** of patient-days instead of half of them.

**I worked out what an accuracy of 20.9% at 9.4% prevalence actually means, and the answer is stark: the model is flagging roughly 87% of all patient-days as high risk.** Essentially everyone. It would be right about the ~9% who do get pneumonia and wrong about almost everyone else. Simply saying "no pneumonia" for every patient would have been **90.6% accurate**.

**Both things are true at once, and that is the lesson.** The model still **ranks** patients usefully — the sickest-looking really are the likeliest. **But the dial it was shipped with, the cut-off that decides who gets flagged, is set for a ward where half the patients have pneumonia, and it does not survive the move to a ward where one in ten does.** The paper reports every number needed to see this. It does not point it out, and it reports no calibration analysis for the external data. **That is its one material omission.**

**Third, and here the paper is exemplary: they tried it on a third dataset, it failed, and they said so.** The **AMIKINHAL** trial had only **9 of the 36 features**. The model scored **0.607** — barely better than a coin toss. **And they explain *why* in a way that goes deeper than "data were missing":** days on ventilator had a strong effect in their own hospital (**Cohen's d = 0.736**) but essentially none, **pointing the opposite way**, in the trial (**d = −0.103**). **The features that were available were not merely fewer; they carried no signal there.** The paper's own verdict is that this analysis *"cannot support or refute model generalizability."* **Labelling your own failed test as uninformative, rather than quietly dropping it or spinning it, is rare.**

**One more thing they did that I have not seen before in this list.** Earlier research had claimed that a worsening organ-failure score predicts pneumonia. **They tested it. It did not work — AUROC 0.528, essentially a coin flip.** They report it. **Testing the published claim your own model is meant to beat, and publishing the result when it fails, is a practice worth naming.**

**What this paper is.** **The best-reported prediction-model study in this reading list.** It names its reporting guideline, shares its code, uses the strongest disease labels the list has seen, prevents data leakage in two separate ways, reports its scores against the right baselines, tests its dominant feature by removing it, reports an unflattering gap between men and women, refutes a prior claim, flags a biologically backwards finding with a mechanism, and names about a dozen limitations including the selection bias at its own foundation. **What it does not tell you is that the model, as it stands, would alarm on nine of every ten patient-days in a different hospital.**

## 2. Study Snapshot

**Study type:** prognostic model development and external validation, using a prospective observational cohort for development and two independent datasets for validation. **No intervention, no randomisation in the development cohort, no prospective deployment.** The paper's own framing: *"all model evaluations were retrospective, and prospective clinical performance remains unknown."*

**Development cohort — SCRIPT.** The *Successful Clinical Response In Pneumonia Therapy* study, *"a prospective observational study of patients on mechanical ventilation who undergo a BAL for suspected pneumonia"*, with data from **June 2018 to July 2023** at a single centre (Northwestern). Analysis used the **"CarpeDiem" dataset**, described as *"a structured, day-level ICU database that includes vitals, laboratory measurements, support measures, medications, BAL results, and outcome annotations"*, abstracted from the electronic record and study data-capture forms.

**The labels, and this is the paper's foundation.** *"At our hospital, clinicians routinely obtain BAL to evaluate for pneumonia early on in clinical decompensation, with an excellent safety profile. These were used to label VAP cases, with details of adjudication using a standardized multireviewer adjudication process."* **A laboratory-confirmed sample plus a multi-reviewer clinical adjudication is the strongest reference standard this reading list has assessed.** Labelling logic: *"As VAP can only develop in intubated patients, only intubated days were used for modeling. Intubated days occurring 3, 5, or 7 days before a confirmed VAP or non-VAP event were labeled as positive or negative, respectively."*

**Cohort.** **507 patients: 261 (51.5%) with BAL-confirmed VAP, 246 (48.5%) who had the same procedure and were adjudicated not to have it.** Median age **62.0 years (Q1–Q3 51.0–71.0)**; **189 female (37.3%), 318 male (62.7%)**; **310 White (61.1%), 103 Black or African American (20.3%), 15 Asian (3.0%), 79 unknown (15.6%)**; **94 Hispanic or Latino (18.5%)**; median BMI 28.2; **118 immunocompromised (23.3%)**; median admission organ-failure score 5.0. **I verified every row of Table 1 reconciles exactly** — each subgroup's two parts sum to its stated total, and each percentage reproduces from its count over its own group's denominator. That is rare enough to state plainly.

**Outcomes differed starkly between the groups, all at p < 0.001:** median ICU days **28.0 vs 9.0**; median intubation days **27.0 vs 6.0**; ECMO **33.7% vs 11.8%**; tracheostomy **51.0% vs 9.3%**; discharge home **13.4% vs 32.9%**; **death 46.0% vs 22.4%**. Most frequent pathogens: *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, then *Enterococcus faecalis*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae* group and *Escherichia coli*.

**Features.** **36 day-level clinical features** spanning *"demographics, vital signs, neurologic status, respiratory mechanics, blood gas values, hematology, blood chemistry, inflammatory markers, medications and interventions, and critical care flags"*, with outlier checks, unit harmonisation and quality checks. **Missingness is acknowledged as a property of the data, not hidden:** *"Data missingness is a characteristic of real-world EHR data."* Handling: models that tolerate missingness natively (XGBoost), or **quartile binning for continuous variables with mask encoding for missingness** and one-hot encoding for categorical variables.

**Models and leakage control.** Six configurations: **binned logistic regression, raw-feature random forest, XGBoost (raw and binned), and a Long Short-Term Memory network** on daily sequences. **Two separate leakage guards, both named.** A **temporal split**: *"data were temporally split into 90% for model development and 10% for held-out testing"*, with the figure caption specifying *"test = most recent patients."* And **patient-grouped cross-validation**: *"10-fold patient-grouped cross-validation"*, *"Within training, 10-fold GroupKFold CV prevents patient leakage"*, and for the sequence model, *"Patient-level splits identical to those used for the snapshot-based models were applied to prevent information leakage across sequences."* Hyperparameters by Bayesian optimisation with Optuna. **95% CIs by bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations.**

**Calibration, reported properly.** *"Calibration was assessed using calibration curves, calibration slope and intercept, and Brier scores."* **Reported as Brier SKILL scores — already divided by the base rate: 0.245, 0.298 and 0.271 for the 3-, 5- and 7-day models.** Plus a **Yates decomposition** for the primary 5-day model: *"minimal mean bias (−0.038; bias squared, 0.001), with residual error driven primarily by correlation deficit (0.095) and variance mismatch (0.055)."*

**Results — internal.** Best AUROCs: **0.806 at 3 days** (random forest, raw), **0.843 at 5 days** (random forest, raw) and **0.866 at 7 days** (LSTM). At a 0.5 threshold the 5-day random forest gave **82.4% sensitivity, 71.4% specificity, 86.2% positive predictive value and 65.2% negative predictive value.** **Baseline AUPRCs are given: 0.695, 0.683 and 0.618** — *"reflecting outcome prevalence in the training dataset."* **And an explicit non-comparability warning:** *"because prediction datasets differed across the 3-, 5-, and 7-day horizons, AUROC and AUPRC values across horizons are not directly comparable, as they were evaluated on nonidentical test sets."*

**Results — against conventional practice.** Best models beat binned logistic regression at every horizon: **0.806 vs 0.725 (3 d), 0.843 vs 0.716 (5 d), 0.866 vs 0.792 (7 d)** — with the honest caveat that *"because the models used different preprocessing approaches, the performance differences cannot be attributed solely to the choice of algorithm."*

**Results — a prior claim tested and refuted.** *"Previous work suggested that trends in Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores could predict the development of subsequent VAP. We examined this..."* Result: *"SOFA score changes did not differ significantly between VAP and non-VAP groups and showed poor discrimination for subsequent VAP (area under the curve [AUC], 0.528)."*

**Results — what the model uses.** *"Ventilator mechanics, inflammatory markers, and oxygenation variables emerging as key VAP predictors. Ventilator days, plateau pressure, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), and Fio₂ were among the most influential."* **And the ablation: *"After removing ventilator days, Random Forest (raw) performance declined but retained an AUC of 0.7338."*** Feature rankings matched in MIMIC-IV, with duration of ventilation again strongest.

**Results — unsupervised and temporal analyses.** Hierarchical clustering of patient-days found VAP days *"enriched in clusters characterized by elevated inflammatory markers, impaired gas exchange, and increased ventilatory support"*, with UMAP showing them localised *"despite labels being excluded from clustering."* In the days before onset, bilirubin, heart rate, temperature, lymphocyte count and mean arterial pressure rose, **while CRRT use, procalcitonin, d-dimer and lactate dehydrogenase declined** — and the paper flags that anomaly with a mechanism: *"The decline in procalcitonin should be interpreted cautiously because of substantial missingness and the inclusion of superinfection VAP occurring during treatment of an earlier pneumonia episode."*

**Results — the risk-trajectory showcase, and its size.** *"This exploratory analysis included 26 patients: 16 with VAP and 10 without VAP (VAP prevalence, 61.5%). Predicted probabilities in the VAP group remained above the classification threshold of 0.683 and increased modestly from approximately 0.74 on day –5 to 0.79 on day –1."* **A rise of 0.05, in 26 patients** — and the paper adds: *"The modest increase may partly reflect cumulative time on mechanical ventilation."*

**Results — subgroups, including the unflattering one.** On the 7-day test set, overall AUC **0.87** with Brier **0.17**. **AUC 0.90 in males versus 0.79 in females** (Brier 0.15 vs 0.20). *"Among patients with adequate ethnicity sample size, AUC was 0.86 in non-Hispanic/non-Latino patients; the Hispanic/Latino subgroup had an AUC of 1.00 but was small (n = 28)."*

**Results — external validation in MIMIC-IV (n = 2,681).** Admissions from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, 2008–2022, with ≥48 hours of continuous invasive ventilation. **Labels were heuristic, not adjudicated:** *"We labeled VAP cases using similar logic as Agard et al."* **Three features were simply unavailable** — d-dimer, procalcitonin and immunocompromised status — *"and were encoded as missing during external validation."* The cohort *"was older, predominantly male, and had lower VAP prevalence and higher mortality than SCRIPT."* **Baseline AUPRCs: 0.0675, 0.0944 and 0.1126.** Best AUROCs: **0.807 (3 d), 0.817 (5 d), 0.813 (7 d)** — all raw-feature models. The paper notes the trade-off: *"binned-feature models showed lower AUPRC and F1, particularly at longer horizons, likely reflecting distributional shift because thresholds derived from SCRIPT compressed MIMIC-IV values into fewer bins."* And computes the right ratio: *"model performance remained approximately five-fold (5×) above baseline."*

**Results — exploratory evaluation in AMIKINHAL.** A randomised trial of inhaled amikacin, with **centrally adjudicated** outcomes but *"only nine of our 36 features (WBC, CRRT, Fio₂, oxygen saturation, Pao₂, creatinine, PEEP, days on ventilator, and temperature)."* **AUC 0.607 (0.579–0.634) for VAP within 3 days.** And the mechanistic explanation: *"days on ventilator showed a large effect (Cohen's d = 0.736) in SCRIPT but a negligible effect (d = −0.103) in AMIKINHAL."* **The paper's verdict: *"The AMIKINHAL analysis was exploratory because only nine of 36 features were available and the data definitions differed substantially; therefore, it cannot support or refute model generalizability."***

**Limitations the authors state — about a dozen, and they name most of the real ones.** Single centre with *"a frequent BAL-sampling protocol, contrary to institutions with different sampling practices."* **The selection problem at the study's foundation:** *"Because SCRIPT enrolls mechanically ventilated patients undergoing BAL for suspected pneumonia, the cohort is enriched for VAP and does not necessarily represent the overall institutional incidence of VAP"*, and *"clinical variability in when BAL is pursued introduces selection bias"* — partly mitigated by *"the regularity with which BAL sampling is performed"* and the negative control cases. Patients with unclear adjudication were excluded. Glasgow Coma Scale values *"may reflect neurologic impairment, sedation, or both."* **The external label caveat, stated unprompted:** *"We used heuristic definitions of VAP/non-VAP in the MIMIC cohort; manual labels by physician experts may have identified different cases of VAP"*, and *"differences in outcome definitions mean that these findings do not establish equivalence of the VAP labels."* Also: *"It is challenging to find external validation cohorts that have both granular EHR data and clean VAP labels."* Substantial missingness that *"may reflect clinician testing practices."* Non-specific features. The AMIKINHAL limitation. All evaluations retrospective. And patients with both a VAP and a non-VAP episode in quick succession (*"fewer than 2.5% of cases"*) were unrepresented.

**And a conclusion that limits itself:** *"Because calibration varied across prediction horizons, these probabilities should not yet be interpreted as reliable individual-level risk estimates."*

**Not reported or not retrievable:** **funding and conflicts of interest**; **any calibration analysis for the external cohorts**; the entire Supplement including the feature list, missingness summary, cohort flow diagram and calibration curves; all figures as images; and the reason two different classification thresholds (0.5 and 0.683) appear in different analyses.

## 3. How Strong Is This Evidence? (Grade 4 out of 5)

**The best-reported prediction-model study this reading list has assessed. It names and follows a reporting guideline, shares its code, rests on laboratory-confirmed and multi-reviewer-adjudicated labels, guards against leakage in two independent ways, reports its scores against the correct baselines, tests its dominant predictor by deleting it, refutes a published claim it could have ignored, reports a sex gap that does it no favours, and names about a dozen limitations including the selection bias at its own foundation. It falls short of 5 because its external operating point collapses without comment, because its strongest predictor is partly time-at-risk, and because the population its headline AUROC describes is narrower than the headline suggests.**

**What is done well, and the first four items are each a first or near-first for this list.**

**The labels are the strongest this reading list has seen.** Not billing codes, not chart review, not a clinician's note: **a bronchoscopic lung sample sent to a laboratory, then a multi-reviewer adjudication of each case.** This list has raised reference-standard quality against paper after paper — entry 85's ICD-code outcomes, entry 87's single self-report item, entry 89's single-informant questionnaires. **Here the outcome is as close to ground truth as critical-care medicine offers**, and the paper's framing is accurate rather than inflated: *"gold-standard VAP diagnosis based on bronchoalveolar lavage fluid sampling and clinical adjudication."*

**TRIPOD+AI is named and followed, and the code is shared.** *"We followed the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis + Artificial Intelligence checklist."* **This is the first entry in nine to name a reporting guideline at all**, after six consecutive entries that named none. And the open-science statement is specific: code shared *"for evaluation and understanding of detailed decisions made during data processing."*

**Leakage is guarded twice, in two different ways, and both are named.** A **temporal split** with the most recent patients held out — which is the right split for a model intended to run forward in time — **and patient-grouped cross-validation inside the training set**, extended explicitly to the sequence model so that *"information leakage across sequences"* is prevented. **Most papers do one of these. Doing both, and saying so, is technique 331 and 355 satisfied together.**

**The scores are reported against their own floors.** **Baseline AUPRCs are given for both cohorts** — 0.695/0.683/0.618 internally and 0.0675/0.0944/0.1126 externally — and the paper computes the ratio where it matters: *"approximately five-fold (5×) above baseline."* **Calibration is reported as Brier SKILL scores (0.245, 0.298, 0.271), already divided by the base rate**, with a Yates decomposition on top. **This is the second time in ninety entries that a paper has done the base-rate division itself** rather than leaving this list to do it.

**The dominant feature was tested by removing it.** Duration of ventilation is the top predictor and is partly circular. **The paper deleted it and re-ran: 0.843 → 0.734.** That is precisely the analysis a sceptic would demand, reported without being asked, with the honest interpretation attached: *"Duration of ventilation likely captures cumulative time at risk for VAP"*, and *"These associations are predictive and should not be interpreted as causal."*

**A prior published claim was tested and refuted.** Earlier work had reported that organ-failure-score trends predict pneumonia. **The paper tested it and got AUROC 0.528 — a coin flip — and published that.** Testing the thing the literature says works, as part of your own paper, and reporting the failure, is a practice this list has not previously recorded.

**The comparison group is a genuine negative control, and it neutralises the objection I came with.** My pre-registered worry was that a horizon anchored to the date of a bronchoscopy would make the model predict the clinician's decision to investigate. **But both classes had that bronchoscopy** — the paper calls the negatives *"negative control cases"* — **so the decision to investigate is held constant.** That is a thoughtful use of an existing cohort, and it converts a potential fatal flaw into a design strength.

**The third dataset failed and the paper says so, in the right words.** **AUC 0.607** in AMIKINHAL, with the explicit verdict that the analysis *"cannot support or refute model generalizability."* **And the explanation goes deeper than missingness: Cohen's d for the top feature was 0.736 internally and −0.103 there — the effect flipped sign.** Most papers would have omitted this analysis. Reporting it, labelling it exploratory, and diagnosing *why* it failed is the behaviour this list exists to look for.

**Also to its credit:** the unflattering **0.90 versus 0.79 AUROC gap between men and women** is reported; an **explicit warning that the three horizons are not comparable** because they use different test sets; the **non-specificity of ventilator settings** discussed at length (*"these parameters are not specific to VAP and may increase in the setting of other causes of respiratory deterioration"*); the **anomalous procalcitonin decline flagged with a mechanism** rather than buried; the **ethics approval number and date**; **95% CIs on every metric** by bootstrap; the acknowledgment of **substantial missingness** as a property of real data; the honest note that algorithm differences **cannot be separated from preprocessing differences**; and the statement that **probabilities should not yet be read as individual risk estimates**.

**Why this is not a 5.**

**The external operating point collapses, and the paper does not say so.** In MIMIC-IV, the 5-day random forest holds an AUROC of **0.817** — and has an **accuracy of 0.209**. Internally it was 0.789. **Working out what that means at 9.4% prevalence: if sensitivity is near 1, specificity is about 0.13, so the model flags roughly 87% of all non-VAP patient-days.** My reconstruction implies an F1 of 0.193 against the paper's reported 0.202, so it checks out. **Predicting "no pneumonia" for every patient would have been 90.6% accurate.** The 3-day and 7-day raw models are the same or worse — accuracy **0.112** and **0.208**. **Every number needed to see this is printed in Table 3, and no sentence in the paper draws attention to it.** **And no external calibration analysis is reported at all** — the paper's calibration caveat refers to the internal data. **This is the gap between "a useful ranking tool that needs its threshold re-set per hospital" and "a deployable alarm," and the paper's framing — *"generalized to an external health system"* — invites the second reading.**

**The strongest predictor is partly the answer.** Patients who developed pneumonia were ventilated a median of **27 days** against **6**. A patient must remain ventilated long enough for ventilator pneumonia to occur, so duration is partly downstream of when the outcome happened. **The ablation is the right response and it is reported — but the resulting 0.734 is the number that answers the clinically meaningful question**, and the abstract leads with 0.866.

**The headline AUROC describes a narrower population than it sounds.** The cohort is patients a clinician suspected strongly enough to bronchoscope, at **51.5% prevalence by construction**. **So 0.843 means "among ventilated patients already suspected of pneumonia, the model ranks well"** — not "among ventilated patients." **The paper states this limitation clearly and without hedging**, which is why it costs the grade less than it otherwise would; but the abstract and Key Points do not carry it.

**The internal AUPRC is far less impressive than it looks, and the paper gives the floor without doing the division.** **AUPRC 0.931 against a baseline of 0.683 is only 1.36× above chance.** Externally, **0.486 against 0.0944 is 5.15× above chance.** **The "worse" number is the better result** — and the paper computes the external ratio while leaving the internal one unstated.

**The showcase trajectory analysis rests on 26 patients.** Sixteen with pneumonia, ten without, with predicted risk rising **0.74 → 0.79** across five days — **a change of 0.05** — and the paper itself attributing part of it to accumulating ventilator days. **The Key Points box summarises this as *"Predicted risk increased over the days preceding VAP."***

**Two small internal inconsistencies.** The Methods say Optuna ran *"50 trials per model per task"*; the Figure 1D caption says *"25 Optuna trials."* And the Methods describe *"patients with exactly 5 consecutive intubated days"* while the Results describe *"85 patients with 7 consecutive intubated days"*, both then plotting day −5 to day −1. **Neither changes a conclusion, but both are what a check catches.** Relatedly, **two different classification thresholds appear — 0.5 in Table 2 and 0.683 in the trajectory analysis — with no stated reason.**

**And an AUROC of exactly 1.00 is reported for a subgroup of 28.** The paper flags the size, which is correct practice. **But a perfect AUROC on 28 people carries no information**, and reporting it alongside real subgroup estimates gives it more standing than it deserves.

**Also against it:** **single-centre development** with an atypically aggressive bronchoscopy protocol; **external labels are heuristic**, which the paper names explicitly as not establishing label equivalence; **three features absent in MIMIC-IV**; **funding and conflicts not retrievable**; the **entire Supplement unreachable**, costing the feature list, the missingness summary and the calibration curves; and **no prospective evaluation**, which the paper states.

**Why this is not a 3.** Entries 85 through 89 each earned a 3 or 4 with some combination of weak labels, a missing comparator, an unaddressed dependence structure, or an untested central claim. **Here the labels are the strongest in the list, the comparator includes both a conventional regression and a published prior claim, leakage is guarded twice, the dominant feature is ablated, the scores are given against their floors, and the paper's own limitations section names most of what I would have raised.** The reporting quality is the highest this list has recorded. **The defects are in one unexamined set of numbers and in how the headline is framed, not in how the work was done.**

**What would make this a 5, concretely.** **Report the external operating-point metrics as a problem rather than a table entry** — and re-derive the threshold in MIMIC-IV to show what the model can do there once recalibrated. **Report external calibration** (slope, intercept, curve) as was done internally. **Lead with the 0.734 ablated figure** alongside 0.866, since it answers the question a clinician is actually asking. **State the internal AUPRC ratio (1.36×)** next to the external one (5×). **And reconcile the two trial counts and the two thresholds.** **Every one of these uses data already in hand**, and the first two are the difference between a ranking tool and a deployable alarm.

## 4. The Editor's Concerns

**1. Discrimination transported; the operating point did not — and this is unremarked.** MIMIC-IV, 5-day random forest: **AUROC 0.817, accuracy 0.209.** Internally the same model had accuracy 0.789. **Reconstructing from the reported numbers at 9.4% prevalence: specificity around 0.13, so roughly 87% of non-VAP patient-days get flagged** (my implied F1 of 0.193 against the reported 0.202 confirms the reconstruction). **The 3-day model's accuracy is 0.112.** **Predicting "no pneumonia" always would have scored 90.6%.** **No external calibration analysis is reported**, and the Discussion's phrase *"robust performance in an external validation cohort"* describes the ranking without noting that the decision rule failed. **The fix is not difficult — re-derive the threshold in the new cohort — but nothing in the paper tells a reader it is necessary.**

**2. The top predictor is partly time-at-risk.** Median intubation days **27 vs 6**. Ventilator duration is the strongest feature internally and externally. **A patient must survive on the ventilator long enough to develop ventilator pneumonia**, so duration is partly a consequence of the outcome's timing. **The paper ablates it — 0.843 → 0.734 — which is exactly right**, and states that duration *"likely captures cumulative time at risk."* **The remaining concern is emphasis: 0.734 is the figure that answers the clinically useful question, and 0.866 is the figure in the abstract.**

**3. The AUROC describes patients already under suspicion.** **51.5% prevalence by construction**, in a cohort of patients a clinician bronchoscoped. **The paper names this twice and does not soften it** — *"enriched for VAP"*, *"clinical variability in when BAL is pursued introduces selection bias."* **Credit for that.** But the Key Points box reads *"Can routinely collected ICU electronic health record data predict ventilator-associated pneumonia before clinical diagnosis?"*, which implies all ventilated patients. **The honest scope is: among ventilated patients a clinician has already decided to investigate.**

**4. The internal AUPRC is only 1.36× its floor.** **0.931 against a baseline of 0.683.** Externally, **0.486 against 0.0944 is 5.15×.** **So the external number that looks worse is nearly four times better relative to chance.** The paper reports both floors — which is correct and uncommon — and computes only the external ratio.

**5. The showcase trajectory is 26 patients and a 0.05 rise.** Sixteen with pneumonia, ten without; probabilities **0.74 → 0.79** over five days; and the paper concedes *"may partly reflect cumulative time on mechanical ventilation."* **The Key Points box states this as *"Predicted risk increased over the days preceding VAP"*, which is a stronger sentence than 26 patients and five hundredths support.**

**6. External labels are heuristic, and the paper says so.** MIMIC-IV labels came from published heuristic logic, not bronchoscopy plus adjudication. **The caveat is stated unprompted and precisely:** *"differences in outcome definitions mean that these findings do not establish equivalence of the VAP labels."* **That is the correct sentence, and most papers do not write it.** The consequence remains: **0.843 internal and 0.817 external are not measuring the same outcome**, so the apparent stability of discrimination is less informative than it appears.

**7. Three features were missing externally and nine of 36 in the trial cohort.** d-dimer, procalcitonin and immunocompromised status were encoded as missing in MIMIC-IV. **In AMIKINHAL only nine features existed, and the model scored 0.607.** **The paper's diagnosis is better than the usual one: the top feature's effect size flipped from d = 0.736 to d = −0.103**, so the available features were uninformative there rather than merely few. **The paper labels the analysis as unable to *"support or refute model generalizability"*, which is the right call.**

**8. A sex gap of 0.11 in AUROC, reported but not discussed.** **0.90 in males versus 0.79 in females**, with Brier scores 0.15 versus 0.20, and **women are 37.3% of the cohort.** The numbers are given; no explanation or mitigation is offered. **Reporting an unflattering subgroup result is good practice; leaving an 11-point gap without comment is a missed opportunity.**

**9. An AUROC of 1.00 on 28 people is reported as a subgroup result.** The paper notes the subgroup *"was small (n = 28)"*, which is correct. **But a perfect AUROC means only that no case was ranked below any non-case in a tiny sample, and it carries no information about performance.**

**10. The procalcitonin decline is biologically backwards.** Procalcitonin is an infection marker; it **fell** in the five days before confirmed bacterial pneumonia, alongside d-dimer and lactate dehydrogenase. **The paper flags it with a mechanism — substantial missingness plus superinfection cases arising during treatment of an earlier pneumonia — which is the correct handling.** It does, however, complicate the paper's own narrative that *"respiratory and inflammatory features begin diverging from baseline up to 5 days before diagnosis."*

**11. Two numerical inconsistencies and two thresholds.** Optuna trials: **50** in the Methods, **25** in the Figure 1D caption. Consecutive-day window: **"exactly 5"** in the Methods, **"7 consecutive"** in the Results, both plotting day −5 to −1. Thresholds: **0.5** for Table 2's operating-point metrics, **0.683** in the trajectory analysis, unexplained. **None changes a conclusion; all four are the kind of thing rebuilding a table catches.**

**12. A guideline named at last — and it changes what can be checked.** 83 named none and met TRIPOD's substance; **84 claimed STROBE and TRIPOD and missed TRIPOD's core items**; 85, 86, 87 and 89 named none; **88 named none but had a 2011 registry entry to check against.** **Entry 90 names TRIPOD+AI, follows it in substance, and shares its code — the first entry in nine where the claim, the practice and the verifiability line up.** **The contrast with entry 84 is the whole lesson: both named TRIPOD; one met it.** **Eight consecutive entries, eight combinations — and this is the first where naming the checklist turned out to mean something.**

## 5. Statistics Spotlight

Three concepts, numbered from 379 in `queue.md`. The first is the finding the paper reports without noticing.

**Discrimination and the operating point are two different things, and only one of them travels.**

**This paper's external validation is the clearest example of this distinction I have seen, and it is hiding in plain sight in its own table.**

**Two different questions a model can answer.** The first is **ranking**: given two patients, can the model put the sicker one first? That is what **AUROC** measures. The second is **deciding**: given one patient, does the model say yes or no? That needs a **threshold** — a cut-off probability above which the alarm fires.

**A model can be excellent at the first and useless at the second.** They are almost independent properties.

**Here is what happened.** In the hospital where the model was built, the 5-day random forest had an **AUROC of 0.817** and an **accuracy of 0.789**. Taken to a Boston hospital's data, the AUROC held: **0.817**. The paper highlights this, and it is a real achievement — **the ranking transported.**

**But the accuracy in Boston was 0.209.**

**Let us work out what that means, because the number is almost unbelievable.** Pneumonia occurred on about **9.4%** of patient-days in Boston, against roughly half in the development cohort. If the model is catching nearly all the real cases — which an AUROC of 0.817 plus a low threshold implies — then:

- accuracy = (cases caught) + (non-cases correctly cleared)
- 0.209 ≈ 0.094 + 0.906 × specificity
- so specificity ≈ **0.127**

**Which means the model flags about 87 out of every 100 patient-days that will not lead to pneumonia.** It is saying "high risk" about nearly everyone. **And for comparison: a model that simply said "no pneumonia" every single time would have been 90.6% accurate.**

**I checked this reconstruction against another reported number.** It implies a positive predictive value of about 0.107 and an F1 score of about **0.193**. The paper reports an F1 of **0.202**. **Close enough to confirm the picture.**

**Why this happens, and it is simple once you see it.** A threshold is a number tuned to a particular mix of patients. **In a ward where half the patients have pneumonia, "probability above 0.5" is a sensible bar.** In a ward where one in ten does, the same bar catches almost everyone, because the model was taught in a world where being on a ventilator and looking unwell made pneumonia roughly even odds. **The ranking is a comparison between patients, so it survives the move. The threshold is a comparison against an absolute number, so it does not.**

**The everyday version.** A teacher grades essays and her rankings are excellent — the best essay really is first, the worst really is last. **She also has a rule: "anything above 70 gets a prize."** That rule worked at her old school, where the class average was 68. **At her new school the average is 40, and she gives out almost no prizes — or, if her rule had been "below 70 needs help," she now flags the entire class.** Her judgement of quality transferred perfectly. Her cut-off did not.

**What should have been done, and it is not difficult.** Report the external **calibration** — the slope, the intercept, the curve — as this paper correctly did for its internal data. **Then re-derive the threshold in the new cohort** and report what sensitivity and specificity become. **The model is probably still useful in Boston; nobody can tell from what is printed.**

**Watch out for:** any external validation reported as an AUROC alone, or any paper whose external section leads with discrimination. **Three steps. Find the accuracy, sensitivity, specificity or F1 in the external cohort — not just the AUROC.** **Compare the external accuracy against the "always say no" accuracy, which is just one minus the prevalence.** **And check whether calibration was reported externally as well as internally** — most papers do it only internally, and that is where the threshold breaks. **The tell: an AUROC that barely moves between cohorts sitting next to an accuracy that falls off a cliff.**

**An AUPRC must be read against its own floor — and because that floor moves with how common the outcome is, a "worse" score can be the better result.**

**This paper reports its floors, which is uncommon and correct. The surprise is what happens when you do the division.**

**What AUPRC is.** The **area under the precision-recall curve** summarises how well a model finds the positive cases without raising too many false alarms. Unlike AUROC, **it has no fixed chance level.** An AUROC of 0.5 always means "no better than guessing." **An AUPRC's chance level equals the prevalence of the outcome** — so in a dataset where half the cases are positive, random guessing scores about 0.5; where 9% are positive, random guessing scores about 0.09.

**This means a bare AUPRC is uninterpretable.** You must know the floor.

**This paper gives you the floor, for both cohorts.** *"Baseline area under the precision-recall curves were 0.695 (3-d), 0.683 (5-d), and 0.618 (7-d), reflecting outcome prevalence in the training dataset"*, and externally *"0.0675 (3-d), 0.0944 (5-d), and 0.1126 (7-d)."* **That is exactly the right practice.**

**Now do the division, and the reading inverts.**

- **Internally**, 5-day AUPRC **0.931** against a floor of **0.683** = **1.36× above chance**
- **Externally**, 5-day AUPRC **0.486** against a floor of **0.0944** = **5.15× above chance**

**The internal 0.931 looks magnificent and is 36% better than guessing. The external 0.486 looks mediocre and is more than five times better than guessing.** The external result is the stronger one.

**Why the internal floor is so high.** The development cohort is **51.5% pneumonia by construction** — everyone in it had a bronchoscopy for suspected pneumonia, and about half turned out to have it. **In a dataset that is half positive, you can score 0.68 on AUPRC by guessing at random.** So 0.931 has only a narrow strip of possible improvement above it.

**The paper does the external division and not the internal one.** It states *"model performance remained approximately five-fold (5×) above baseline"* for MIMIC-IV — correct, and I verified it at 5.15× — but never notes that its own headline internal figure is 1.36× its floor.

**The everyday version.** Two students take different exams. **One scores 93% on an exam where the class average is 68% — impressive, 25 points above the room.** The other scores 49% on an exam where the class average is 9% — **which is extraordinary, five times the room.** Quoting the raw percentages makes the first student look far better. **Quoting them against their classes reverses the ranking.**

**Watch out for:** any AUPRC, average precision, or "precision-recall" figure. **Two steps. Find the outcome prevalence, which is the floor — and if the paper does not give it, compute it from the counts.** **Then divide.** **And beware the opposite trap too:** in a very rare outcome a tiny AUPRC can still represent large skill, which is why an AUPRC of 0.486 here is good news rather than bad. **The tell: an AUPRC quoted without a prevalence anywhere nearby — and the specific trap this paper illustrates, where the impressive-looking number and the impressive result are in different columns.**

**When the strongest predictor is time-at-risk, the model is partly measuring survival to the outcome — so demand the ablation.**

**The model's single most influential feature is "days on ventilator," and this needs thinking through carefully, because it is both perfectly legitimate and partly circular.**

**The legitimate part.** Every day on a ventilator is another day for bacteria to get into the lungs. **So duration genuinely is a risk factor**, and any sensible clinician weighs it. The paper says this: duration *"likely captures cumulative time at risk for VAP."*

**The circular part.** To be recorded as having **ventilator-associated** pneumonia, you must be on a ventilator when it happens. **A patient who is extubated on day 4, or who dies on day 3, cannot appear in the pneumonia group.** So the pneumonia group is automatically made of people who stayed ventilated longer. The numbers show it starkly: **median 27 intubation days in the pneumonia group against 6 in the non-pneumonia group.**

**So "long time on a ventilator" is partly a *description of having had the outcome*, not purely a *prediction of it*.** In the statistical literature this family of problems goes by names like immortal time bias: the exposure cannot be short in anyone who had the late outcome, because surviving long enough is a precondition for being counted.

**Why that matters for a clinician.** Standing at a bedside on day 3, you already know how long this patient has been ventilated. **A model that is largely reading that number back to you adds little.** The useful question is: *among patients who have been ventilated about as long as this one, who is heading for pneumonia?* **A model leaning on duration is not answering that question.**

**And this paper does the test that settles it.** *"After removing ventilator days, Random Forest (raw) performance declined but retained an AUC of 0.7338."* **So 0.843 with duration, 0.734 without — about 0.11 of AUROC came from duration alone.** **0.734 is the honest figure for the bedside question**, and the fact that it is comfortably above 0.5 means the other 35 features genuinely carry signal. **That is the right answer, obtained the right way, and reported without being asked.**

**The everyday version.** You build a model to predict which library books get damaged. **Your best feature turns out to be "number of days the book has been checked out."** True and useful in a sense — longer loans mean more chances for coffee spills. **But a book that was damaged on day 40 was necessarily checked out for at least 40 days**, so the feature is partly a restatement of when the damage happened. **To find out whether you can spot a doomed book on day 5, you have to take that feature out and look again.**

**Watch out for:** any prognostic model whose top feature is a **duration, a length of stay, a count of prior visits, a cumulative dose, or time since admission**. **Three questions. Could the feature's value have been high in someone who did not survive to the outcome? If not, it is partly downstream of the outcome.** **Did the paper refit without it — and if so, what is the number?** **And is the ablated figure or the full figure in the abstract?** **The tell: a feature-importance plot led by something that simply counts time, with no sensitivity analysis beside it.** This paper has the plot *and* the sensitivity analysis; most do not.

## 6. Jargon Translator

**Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP)** — a lung infection that develops in someone breathing through a tube in their windpipe, because the tube bypasses the body's normal defences. **Common, and in this cohort associated with 46.0% mortality versus 22.4%.**

**Mechanical ventilation / intubation** — breathing supported by a machine through a tube in the windpipe.

**Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL)** — passing a camera into the lungs and washing out a sample of fluid for laboratory analysis. **The basis of this paper's labels, and the reason they are trustworthy.**

**Adjudication** — a panel of reviewers deciding, case by case, whether a patient really had the condition. **"Multireviewer adjudication" here; the strongest reference standard in this reading list.**

**Reference standard / gold standard** — the measurement treated as truth, against which a model is judged. **Most prediction studies use billing codes; this one uses a laboratory sample plus human adjudication.**

**Electronic health record (EHR)** — the hospital's computer system, where measurements are recorded as care happens.

**Day-level data** — one row per patient per day, rather than one row per patient. **36 features per patient-day here.**

**PEEP (positive end-expiratory pressure) and FiO₂** — how much pressure the ventilator holds in the lungs between breaths, and what fraction of the delivered air is oxygen. **Both rise when lungs are failing — and the paper notes they are not specific to pneumonia.**

**Plateau pressure** — the pressure needed to hold the lungs inflated; rises when lungs stiffen.

**Procalcitonin, C-reactive protein, d-dimer, lactate dehydrogenase** — blood markers that generally rise with infection or inflammation. **Procalcitonin unexpectedly *fell* before pneumonia here, which the paper flags.**

**Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)** — a score of consciousness. **The paper notes it *"may reflect neurologic impairment, sedation, or both."***

**SOFA (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment)** — a score of how many organ systems are failing. **Prior work claimed its trend predicts pneumonia; this paper tested that and got AUROC 0.528.**

**ECMO** — a machine that oxygenates blood outside the body when lungs fail completely. **33.7% of pneumonia patients here.**

**CRRT (continuous renal replacement therapy)** — continuous dialysis for critically ill patients.

**AUROC (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve)** — the chance the model ranks a true case above a non-case. **0.5 is guessing, 1.0 is perfect. 0.843 here internally. It measures RANKING, not deciding** — technique 379.

**AUPRC (area under the precision-recall curve)** — how well the model finds positives without too many false alarms. **Its chance level equals the outcome's prevalence, so it must be read against that floor** — technique 380.

**Accuracy** — the share of all predictions that are right. **Depends entirely on where the threshold sits, and on how common the outcome is.**

**Sensitivity and specificity** — the share of real cases caught, and the share of non-cases correctly cleared. **These transport between hospitals; accuracy and predictive values do not.**

**Positive and negative predictive value (PPV, NPV)** — of those flagged, the share who really have it; of those cleared, the share who really do not. **86.2% and 65.2% here, at the development cohort's 51.5% prevalence.**

**Threshold / operating point** — the probability above which the model raises an alarm. **0.5 in Table 2 and 0.683 in the trajectory analysis here. The thing that broke in the external cohort.**

**Calibration** — whether a predicted 30% risk really means about 30%. **Reported internally here; not reported externally.**

**Brier score and Brier SKILL score** — the average squared error of predicted probabilities, and that error expressed relative to just predicting the base rate. **Skill scores of 0.245, 0.298 and 0.271 here, meaning about 25–30% better than the base rate** — and reporting it this way is the paper doing technique 351 itself.

**Yates decomposition** — splitting prediction error into bias, correlation deficit and variance mismatch. **Reported for the 5-day model.**

**Bootstrap resampling** — re-drawing the data many times to get confidence intervals. **1,000 iterations here.**

**Grouped cross-validation (GroupKFold)** — making sure all of one patient's days land on the same side of a split, so the model cannot memorise individuals.

**Temporal split** — training on earlier patients and testing on later ones, which mimics real deployment. **Used here alongside grouped cross-validation.**

**LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory)** — a neural network built to read sequences in order. **Best 7-day performer here at 0.866.**

**Random forest and XGBoost** — ensembles of decision trees. **XGBoost handles missing values natively.**

**SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)** — a method for attributing a prediction to individual features.

**UMAP and hierarchical clustering** — ways of grouping and visualising similar patient-days without using the outcome labels.

**Cohen's d** — a standardised effect size. **0.736 for ventilator days internally versus −0.103 in the trial cohort — the sign flipped**, which is technique 383.

**MIMIC-IV** — a public database of intensive care records from a Boston hospital. **Used here for external validation, with heuristic rather than adjudicated labels.**

**TRIPOD+AI** — the reporting checklist for prediction-model studies using AI. **Named and followed here, for the first time in nine entries.**

**Immortal time bias** — the family of problems arising when a patient must survive a certain length of time to be counted as having the outcome, so that duration becomes partly a restatement of the outcome — technique 382.

## 7. What You Can (and Can't) Say

**You can say:**

"In 507 mechanically ventilated adults who all underwent bronchoalveolar lavage for suspected pneumonia, 261 (51.5%) had laboratory-confirmed, multi-reviewer-adjudicated ventilator-associated pneumonia and 246 (48.5%) did not."

"Machine learning models using 36 day-level clinical features reached an AUROC of 0.866 for predicting pneumonia within 7 days and 0.843 within 5 days, with 95% confidence intervals from bootstrap resampling."

"At a 0.5 threshold the 5-day model achieved 82.4% sensitivity, 71.4% specificity, 86.2% positive predictive value and 65.2% negative predictive value in the development cohort."

"The best models outperformed binned logistic regression at all three horizons (0.806 vs 0.725, 0.843 vs 0.716, 0.866 vs 0.792), though the authors note preprocessing differed so the gap cannot be attributed to the algorithm alone."

"Duration of mechanical ventilation was the strongest predictor; removing it reduced AUROC from 0.843 to 0.734."

"A previously reported predictor — trends in organ-failure scores — showed poor discrimination here, with an AUROC of 0.528."

"In MIMIC-IV (n = 2,681), AUROC remained above 0.80 across horizons, with AUPRC about five-fold above the baseline set by the lower prevalence."

"In the AMIKINHAL trial cohort, where only 9 of 36 features were available, AUROC was 0.607 (0.579–0.634); the authors state this analysis cannot support or refute generalizability."

"Brier skill scores were 0.245, 0.298 and 0.271 for the 3-, 5- and 7-day models."

"The authors followed TRIPOD+AI, shared their code, used patient-grouped and temporal splits, and state that the probabilities should not yet be interpreted as reliable individual-level risk estimates."

**You cannot say:**

"The model could be deployed to alert clinicians to impending pneumonia." **In the external cohort its accuracy was 0.209 at the shipped threshold** — reconstructing from the reported figures, it flags roughly 87% of non-pneumonia patient-days. **The threshold would have to be re-derived per hospital, and no external calibration is reported.**

"The model generalised to another health system." **Its *ranking* generalised (AUROC 0.817). Its *decision rule* did not (accuracy 0.209 against 0.789 internally).** And the external labels were heuristic, which the authors state does not establish label equivalence.

"The model predicts pneumonia in ventilated patients." **It predicts it among ventilated patients a clinician already suspected enough to bronchoscope** — a cohort the authors describe as *"enriched for VAP."*

"AUROC 0.866 reflects the model's clinical value." **Its strongest feature is duration of ventilation, which is partly downstream of the outcome; without it the figure is 0.734.**

"An AUPRC of 0.931 shows excellent precision." **The baseline AUPRC internally was 0.683, so that is 1.36× above chance.** The external 0.486 against a floor of 0.0944 is 5.15× above chance — **the better result.**

"Predicted risk rises steadily in the days before pneumonia." **That analysis covered 26 patients and a rise from 0.74 to 0.79**, which the authors partly attribute to accumulating ventilator days.

"Inflammatory markers rise before pneumonia." **Procalcitonin, d-dimer and lactate dehydrogenase *declined*** in the five days before diagnosis, which the authors flag as likely reflecting missingness and superinfection cases.

"The model works equally well for men and women." **AUROC was 0.90 in males and 0.79 in females**, with no explanation offered.

"The model performed perfectly in Hispanic/Latino patients." **An AUROC of 1.00 on 28 people carries no information**, as the authors' note on sample size implies.

"This shows machine learning beats clinical judgement for VAP." **No clinician comparison was performed.** The comparator was a logistic regression and a published prior claim.

**The honest one-sentence version:** *the best-reported prediction-model study in this reading list — bronchoscopically confirmed and multi-reviewer-adjudicated labels, TRIPOD+AI named and followed, code shared, leakage guarded by both patient-grouped and temporal splits, scores reported against their own floors, calibration given as base-rate-normalised skill scores, the dominant feature tested by deletion, a published prior claim refuted, and a dozen limitations named including the selection bias at its own foundation — whose model ranks ventilated patients well (AUROC 0.843, or 0.734 without ventilator duration) among those a clinician had already decided to investigate, and whose ranking survived a move to another hospital while its decision threshold collapsed to an accuracy of 0.209, a fact printed in its own table and nowhere discussed.*

## 8. Bottom Line for Your Life

**If someone you love is on a ventilator in intensive care:** nothing here is available in any hospital, and the authors say prospective testing has not been done. **But two numbers from this study are worth knowing, because they describe the stakes rather than the technology.** Among 507 ventilated patients who were investigated for pneumonia, those who turned out to have it spent a median of **28 days** in intensive care against **9**, and **46% died against 22%**. Ventilator pneumonia is one of the main reasons an ICU stay goes from days to weeks. **The practical thing families can ask about is not an algorithm but the prevention bundle** — keeping the head of the bed raised, daily assessment of whether the breathing tube can come out, mouth care, and avoiding unnecessary sedation. **Those are established, they are checklist items in most ICUs, and "is my relative being assessed daily for whether the tube can come out?" is a reasonable question to ask.**

**If you work in critical care:** the honest summary is that this model **ranks** well and does not yet **decide** well. Its discrimination held up in a second hospital (AUROC 0.817), which is genuinely encouraging and more than most published ICU models manage. **But at the threshold it ships with, in that second hospital, it flagged roughly nine of every ten patient-days.** That is not a reason to dismiss it — it is a reason to understand that **a model like this needs its cut-off re-derived on your own patients before it can fire an alarm**, and that nobody can tell from this paper what its sensitivity and specificity would be once that is done. **Also worth knowing: the single strongest predictor was simply how long the patient had been ventilated**, which you already know at the bedside, and without it the model scores 0.734 rather than 0.843.

**The reading skill from this entry, and it is the most practically useful one in weeks.** When a paper reports external validation, **do not stop at the AUROC.** Find the accuracy, and compare it against the score you would get by always saying "no." Here: external accuracy **0.209**, against **90.6%** for always saying no. **The AUROC barely moved between hospitals; the accuracy fell off a cliff.** Those two facts coexist because **ranking is a comparison between patients, which travels, while a threshold is a comparison against a fixed number, which does not.** *The everyday version: a teacher's essay rankings transfer perfectly to a new school, but her rule that "above 70 gets a prize" does not, because the new school's average is 40.*

**A second one, about a metric that is easy to misread in both directions.** The precision-recall score (AUPRC) has **no fixed chance level** — its floor is simply how common the outcome is. **This paper reports its floors, which is unusually good practice. Do the division and the reading flips:** the internal 0.931 is **1.36×** its floor of 0.683, while the external 0.486 is **5.15×** its floor of 0.0944. **The number that looks worse is nearly four times the better result.** So whenever you see a precision-recall figure, find the prevalence and divide. **And do not dismiss a small AUPRC in a rare disease — it may represent enormous skill.**

**A third, about features that are really just clocks.** When a prognostic model's top predictor is a **duration** — days on a ventilator, length of stay, time since admission, cumulative dose — ask whether that value *could* have been small in someone who had the outcome. **Here it could not: to get ventilator pneumonia you must stay on a ventilator, so the pneumonia group was built from people ventilated a median of 27 days against 6.** The feature is partly a restatement of when the outcome happened. **The test is to delete it and refit** — and this paper did exactly that, which is why it earns credit rather than criticism on the point.

**And one word about why this paper is worth holding up as an example.** It names the reporting checklist it followed and actually follows it. It shares its code. It uses the hardest, most expensive kind of disease label there is. It guards against data leakage two separate ways. It reports its scores against the right baselines. It deletes its own best feature to see what is left. **It tests a claim from the prior literature and reports that the claim failed. It reports that its own third validation attempt failed, and labels that attempt as unable to support or refute anything. It reports that the model works worse in women. And its limitations section names, in its own words, the selection bias sitting at the foundation of the whole study.** **Compare entry 84 in this list, which also claimed to follow TRIPOD and missed its core items.** **Both papers said the same words; only one of them did the work — which is the whole reason this list reads methods instead of checklists.**

**If you are reading the coverage:** expect "AI predicts deadly ICU pneumonia a week early." **Four things will likely be missing.** That the patients studied had already been investigated for pneumonia, so about half of them had it. That the model's strongest signal is how long someone has already been ventilated. That in another hospital the ranking held but the alarm would have gone off for nearly everyone. And that the researchers' own third test failed and they said so.

**This is education, not medical advice.** Ventilator-associated pneumonia is a serious condition managed by critical-care teams, and nothing described here is validated or available for clinical use. Questions about a patient's care belong with their treating clinicians.

**All three pre-registered questions, answered — and the paper had already answered two of them itself.** Set on 2026-11-26, written down before reading.

**(1) "How were the 507 SCRIPT patients selected — all of them underwent BAL for SUSPECTED pneumonia, so the 'non-VAP' comparison group is patients suspected of pneumonia who did not have it, not ventilated patients generally; does the paper state which population the AUROC describes?"** — **Confirmed exactly, and the paper states it twice, without hedging.** Verbatim: *"261 (51.5%) experienced a BAL-confirmed episode of VAP, while 246 patients (48.5%) underwent BAL for suspected pneumonia but were adjudicated not to have VAP."* **So prevalence is ~51.5% by construction of a nested suspicion cohort, and the AUROC describes ranking among patients a clinician had already decided to bronchoscope.** The paper's own limitations say: *"Because SCRIPT enrolls mechanically ventilated patients undergoing BAL for suspected pneumonia, the cohort is enriched for VAP and does not necessarily represent the overall institutional incidence of VAP, or VAP rates across other populations"*, and *"clinical variability in when BAL is pursued introduces selection bias."* **Credit in full for naming it.** The remaining gap is one of emphasis: the Key Points box asks *"Can routinely collected ICU electronic health record data predict ventilator-associated pneumonia?"*, which implies all ventilated patients.

**(2) "The outcome is 'VAP within 3, 5 or 7 days before BAL-confirmed diagnosis' — is the horizon anchored to the BAL date, which is determined by a clinician's decision to investigate, and if so does the model partly predict the clinician rather than the pneumonia?"** — **Anchored to the BAL date, yes: *"Intubated days occurring 3, 5, or 7 days before a confirmed VAP or non-VAP event were labeled as positive or negative, respectively."* BUT the design neutralises the objection, and more elegantly than I expected.** **Both classes had the bronchoscopy.** The negatives are patients who underwent the same investigation and were adjudicated not to have pneumonia — **the paper calls them *"negative control cases"*** and cites them as mitigation: *"This bias is in part mitigated by the regularity with which BAL sampling is performed in our hospital, and the negative control cases where a BAL did not end up showing evidence of pneumonia."* **Because the clinician's decision to investigate is held CONSTANT across both groups, the model cannot simply be predicting that decision.** What it distinguishes is, among the suspected, who actually had it. **That is a genuinely clever use of an existing cohort and converts a potential fatal flaw into a design strength.**

**(3) "In MIMIC-IV the labels cannot be BAL-adjudicated the same way — what label definition was used externally, and is the 0.866 → 0.817 comparison therefore measuring the same outcome?"** — **Heuristic labels externally, and the paper states the consequence unprompted and precisely.** Verbatim: *"We labeled VAP cases using similar logic as Agard et al"*, and in the limitations: *"We used heuristic definitions of VAP/non-VAP in the MIMIC cohort; manual labels by physician experts may have identified different cases of VAP."* **And the exact sentence I would have wanted: *"Although model discrimination and feature importance were similar in MIMIC-IV, differences in outcome definitions mean that these findings do not establish equivalence of the VAP labels."*** The paper also names the structural difficulty: *"It is challenging to find external validation cohorts that have both granular EHR data and clean VAP labels."* **So the internal and external figures are not measuring the same outcome, the paper says so, and three features (d-dimer, procalcitonin, immunocompromised status) were additionally unavailable and encoded as missing.**

**The decisive finding of the day, which the paper reports in a table and never discusses.** **Discrimination transported; the operating point collapsed.** MIMIC-IV 5-day random forest: **AUROC 0.817** (internally 0.843) but **accuracy 0.209** (internally 0.789), with the 3-day model at **0.112** and the 7-day at **0.208**. **Reconstructing at the external prevalence of 9.4%: if sensitivity is near 1, specificity = (0.209 − 0.0944)/(1 − 0.0944) = 0.127, so the model flags about 87% of all non-VAP patient-days**, implying a PPV of 0.107 and an F1 of **0.193** against the paper's reported **0.202** — **the reconstruction checks out.** **Always predicting "no VAP" would have scored 90.6% accuracy.** **And no external calibration analysis is reported** — the paper's caveat that *"calibration varied across prediction horizons"* refers to internal data. **The trade the paper half-reports: the binned models kept usable operating points (accuracy 0.707–0.846) at the cost of lower AUROC (0.627–0.707).** **This is the paper's one material omission, and it is the difference between a ranking tool and a deployable alarm.**

**The second finding: the AUPRC reading inverts once divided by its floor.** **The paper reports baseline AUPRCs for both cohorts, which is correct and uncommon.** Dividing: **internal 5-day 0.931 / 0.683 = 1.36× above chance; external 5-day 0.486 / 0.0944 = 5.15× above chance.** **So the headline internal figure that looks magnificent is 36% better than guessing, while the external figure that looks mediocre is more than five times better — the "worse" number is the better result.** The paper computes the external ratio itself (*"approximately five-fold (5×) above baseline"*, which I verified at 5.15×) and never computes the internal one.
