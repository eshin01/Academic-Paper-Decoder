## 1. The Gist

**Doctors divide childhood mental health problems into boxes: this child has ADHD, that one has an anxiety disorder. But the boxes overlap badly, children in the same box look nothing alike, and knowing the box tells you little about which treatment will help. This study took 3,508 nine-to-eleven-year-olds who already had one of those diagnoses, measured their brain wiring and their behaviour, and let a computer find its own groupings. It found three. And here is the part that matters: when the researchers took those groupings to a completely different study — different city, different scanners, different children — the groupings showed up again.**

**Start with why anyone would do this.** ADHD and anxiety disorders are both common in children and often occur together. The paper's framing of the problem is the honest one: diagnostic manuals *"rely on symptom thresholds and may obscure shared mechanisms across disorders,"* and symptom-based groupings *"often align weakly with neuroimaging measures."* So two children with the same label can have different brains, different courses and different treatment needs. **The hope is that if you let the brain data speak, you get groups that mean something biologically.**

**What they did.** They used the **ABCD study** — a large American project that has followed over 11,000 children from around age nine. From it they drew three groups:

- **3,508 children who already had ADHD or an anxiety disorder** at the start — the discovery group
- **1,947 children with no diagnosis at any point in the study** — the comparison group
- **693 children with no diagnosis at the start who developed one later** — the "late-onset" group

For each child they had about **20 minutes of brain scanning at rest** — which they turned into a map of how strongly each pair of brain regions moves together, giving **4,950 connections per child** (a number I had to compute; the paper never states it) — plus **thirteen measures**: five of thinking ability (attention, working memory, executive function, processing speed and reasoning) and **eight parent-reported ratings of the child's behaviour and mood**.

**The method, in plain terms.** Brain scans are mostly full of ordinary variation that has nothing to do with any disorder. So they used a technique that **learns what is different about the diagnosed children by explicitly contrasting them against the undiagnosed ones**, setting aside the variation the two groups share. Then a second step **lined up that disorder-specific brain variation with the thirteen behaviour and thinking measures**. Then they looked for clusters.

**What came out: two dimensions, then three groups.** The first dimension linked **better thinking skills to less rule-breaking** — call it cognitive control. The second linked **anxious and depressed feelings at one end to attention problems at the other** — call it emotion-attention regulation. The three groups were labelled:

- **Externalizing-disinhibited (ED): 437 children, 12.5%** — the most severe, lowest thinking scores, most aggression and rule-breaking
- **Internalizing-distressed (ID): 1,257 children, 35.8%** — anxious and distressed, but with **thinking scores like undiagnosed children**
- **Mixed dysregulation (MD): 1,814 children, 51.7%** — in between, with marked attention problems

I verified those add to 3,508 exactly, and every percentage in the paper reproduces from its count.

**Now the strongest thing in this paper, and it is genuinely strong.** They took the finished model — **without retraining or adjusting it at all** — and applied it to **224 children from the Healthy Brain Network**, a separate study in New York with different scanners and different children. **Both dimensions showed up again** (the first weakened from 0.412 to 0.336, the second from 0.212 to 0.179), and the three groups reappeared with closely matching proportions and closely matching symptom patterns. **That is the hardest test in this field and most studies never attempt it.** It is also, notably, a much stronger test than yesterday's paper from substantially the same senior authors managed — and the contrast is instructive.

**And they checked whether the answer depends on an arbitrary choice.** The whole analysis rests on carving the brain into 100 regions. **They redid everything with a different atlas carving it into 268 regions and got the same two dimensions** (loadings correlating 0.96 and 0.95 with the originals) **and three groups again.** Most papers skip this.

**But here is the gap at the centre, and it is the question I wrote down before reading.** The method used to decide there were **three** groups was a pair of statistical criteria called BIC and AIC. **Those criteria compare two clusters against three against four. Neither one asks whether there are any clusters at all.**

That distinction is everything in this kind of study. **Imagine a crowd of people's heights.** Height is a smooth continuum — there are no natural "height groups." But if you hand a clustering program a list of heights and ask for the best number of groups, **it will cheerfully hand you three, and tell you they fit better than one.** The groups would be real cuts through a real continuum, but they would not be discoveries about human biology.

**And the paper's own analyses show those criteria are unreliable here.** When they ran the clustering on brain data alone, **BIC said two clusters and AIC said four** — flat disagreement. And when they clustered the thirteen behaviour measures alone, the result *"yielded no clear optimal cluster number."* **So "three" is a property of this particular combined representation, not something the brain announced.** A proper test — shuffling the data to destroy any grouping and checking whether three still looks better than one — is not reported.

**In fairness, the external validation does some of that work.** A partition that was pure noise should not reproduce in a different cohort with matching symptom patterns. So the three groups are at least **reproducible**. Whether they are **separate kinds of children, or three regions of one continuous landscape, is not settled here** — and the paper's language ("biotypes," "biologically grounded") leans towards the first reading.

**The second gap, and it is the one nobody mentions.** **ABCD deliberately recruited brothers, sisters and twins.** The words "sibling," "twin" and "relatedness" **do not appear anywhere in this paper**, and the statistical models treat all 3,508 children as unrelated individuals.

Why that matters more than usual here: the groups are described partly by **family circumstances** — the ED group is linked to *"low socioeconomic status, non-intact family structure, family conflict, parental psychopathology."* **Siblings share all of those by definition.** So two brothers landing in the same group is one family's worth of evidence, not two children's worth. It does not erase the finding, but it means the certainty attached to it is overstated.

**Third, how much of the "distinct symptom profiles" is actually news.** **Eight of the thirteen things fed into the model were the parent's ratings of the child's behaviour and mood.** So when the paper reports that the groups differ in behaviour and mood, it is substantially **describing its own inputs.** The paper is honest about this — it calls them *"the 13 training scales"* in the Results. And a real amount of the evidence **is** independent: personality measures, family and environmental risk, the brain network patterns, structural brain scans, pubertal development, four years of follow-up, and the separate New York cohort. **But the abstract's "three biotypes with distinct symptom profiles" reads as a discovery when part of it is a restatement.**

**Fourth, "detectable before symptom onset."** The 693 late-onset children — no diagnosis at the start, a diagnosis later — did show the brain patterns early. **But "no diagnosis" is not "no symptoms."** The paper's own phrasing is *"despite low baseline symptom severity"* — low, not zero. These children were below the threshold for a diagnosis, which is a different and much weaker claim than being symptom-free. **And the structural brain differences in this group were not significant once other factors were accounted for**, which the paper reports.

**What this paper is.** The best-validated unsupervised subtyping study this reading list has seen: a real held-out cohort tested without refitting, a real robustness check against an arbitrary analytic choice, high stability of the dimensions across folds, and a set of control analyses that could have embarrassed the authors and are reported with numbers anyway. **What it has not established is that its three groups are kinds rather than regions** — and for a paper whose central object is a set of groups, that is the question.

## 2. Study Snapshot

**Study type:** secondary analysis of two existing observational cohorts, using an unsupervised deep-learning framework (named **DeCoDE** by the authors) to derive brain–behaviour dimensions, followed by model-based clustering to define subgroups. **No randomisation, no intervention, no prespecified analysis plan.**

**Discovery cohort — ABCD.** Measures from **Release 5.1**; imaging from the **ABCD-BIDS Community Collection (Collection 3165)**. The parent study enrolled **over 11,000 children aged 9–10 from 21 US sites**. *"After excluding participants with incomplete rs-fMRI, missing diagnostic or behavioral data at baseline, or failed MRI preprocessing and quality control, 6,148 participants remained."* These split into:
- **Discovery: 3,508 diagnosed with ADHD or an anxiety disorder at baseline** — 1,631 female (46.5%), 1,877 male; mean age **9.95 years (SD 0.62)**
- **Controls: 1,947 with no diagnosis throughout the study** — 1,028 female (52.8%), 919 male; mean age 9.95 (SD 0.62)
- **Late-onset: 693 with no baseline diagnosis who developed ADHD or an anxiety disorder during follow-up** — 361 female (52.1%), 332 male; mean age 9.89 (SD 0.63)

**I verified 3,508 + 1,947 + 693 = 6,148, and every sex percentage reproduces exactly from its own group total.** Anxiety disorders included generalised anxiety, social anxiety, specific phobia, separation anxiety, panic disorder, agoraphobia and selective mutism.

**A selection point the paper does not flag.** Because the 6,148 **is** the three analytic groups, children with other psychiatric diagnoses were excluded from the analysis entirely, and the comparison group is defined as having **no diagnosis at any point across the whole study** — screened for future health as well as present health. **That is a deliberately clean comparison group, and the contrastive method uses it as the "background" against which disorder-specific brain variation is defined.**

**External cohort — HBN.** The parent dataset holds **over 4,000 participants aged 5–21** recruited mainly from New York, scanned at **four sites**. The paper applied *"the same imaging-quality and complete-behavior eligibility criteria used for ABCD, followed by restriction to the ABCD baseline age range and age matching,"* giving **224 participants with ADHD or anxiety** — 84 female, 140 male; mean age **9.90 (SD 0.60)**. Verified to sum.

**The thirteen inputs.** **Five cognitive:** four NIH Toolbox tasks (Flanker, List Sort Working Memory, Dimensional Change Card Sort, Pattern Comparison Processing Speed) plus **WISC-V matrix reasoning** for fluid intelligence. **Eight mental health, all from the parent-reported Child Behavior Checklist:** anxious/depressed, withdrawn/depressed, somatic complaints, social problems, thought problems, attention problems, rule-breaking and aggressive behaviour. **A stated and deliberate choice:** *"To preserve the full scope of brain–behavior relationships and avoid potential information loss from overadjustment, we used uncorrected behavioral data"* — uncorrected standard scores and unadjusted raw scores.

**Imaging.** ABCD: 21 sites on **Siemens Prisma, GE 750 and Philips** scanners, multiband sequence at **2.4 mm isotropic, TR 800 ms, TE 30 ms**, 60 slices, **approximately 20 minutes per child** viewing a fixation crosshair, baseline only, preprocessed through the DCAN Labs HCP-style surface pipeline. HBN: site-specific multiband protocols (**Staten Island at 2.5 mm, TR 1,450 ms; other sites 2.4 mm, TR 800 ms**), preprocessed locally with fMRIPrep plus ICA-AROMA and 6 mm smoothing. **Both datasets excluded runs with mean framewise displacement above 0.5 mm or fewer than 300 volumes.** **ComBat harmonisation was applied across HBN's four sites using the whole ABCD cohort as a reference, with sex and age as covariates**, and the paper is explicit that *"the ABCD cohort served solely as a reference for aligning HBN data; their original FC features were preserved."*

**Connectivity.** The **100-parcel Schaefer cortical parcellation**; regional time series averaged, connectivity as Pearson correlation between every region pair, Fisher transformed, then **demeaned within each run to remove global connectivity offsets**. **That gives 100 × 99 ÷ 2 = 4,950 connections — a figure the paper never states.** A **sensitivity analysis repeated the entire pipeline with the 268-node Shen atlas (35,778 connections)**.

**The model, in three stages.** A **contrastive variational autoencoder** splits each child's connectivity into *"salient"* variables *"encoding disorder-specific signals present only in target data"* and *"shared"* variables capturing variation present in both groups, trained jointly on the diagnosed and control groups with a total-correlation penalty to keep the two independent. **A candid technical disclosure follows:** because the two groups' terms are *"averaged separately and then summed, they receive equal group-level weight, giving observations in the smaller group greater per-sample contribution... although the smaller group's mini-batch estimate can have higher variance and greater sensitivity to individual observations."* Then **deep generalized canonical correlation analysis** finds a shared low-dimensional representation across the connectivity, cognitive and behavioural views. Then connectivity loadings are approximated by **orthogonal matching pursuit at a sparsity of 500 connections** — about 10% of the Schaefer edge set — chosen *"based on correlation trends."* **Ten-fold cross-validation throughout.**

**Results — the two dimensions.** **Cognitive–behavioural dysfunction: out-of-fold r = 0.412, P = 4.60 × 10⁻¹⁴⁴, Cohen's d = 0.905.** **Emotion–attention dysfunction: r = 0.212, P = 4.55 × 10⁻³⁷, Cohen's d = 0.435.** **I reproduced both test statistics from r and n = 3,508 and both are consistent.** In variance terms that is **17.0% and 4.5%**. The dimensions were uncorrelated, showed **no sex differences**, and representational similarity analysis found *"minimal association between the FC score representation and site, scanner, sex, age or mean framewise displacement."* **Loadings were stable across folds: connectivity ICC 0.89 and 0.91, behaviour ICC 0.99 and 0.99.** Ablations showed removing the integrative, contrastive or nonlinear components *"reduced correlations or impaired cross-modal alignment."* The first dimension was dominated by **frontoparietal control and dorsal attention** within-network connections; the second by **limbic, dorsal attention and ventral attention** contributions.

**Results — the three biotypes.** Clustering by **Gaussian mixture model** on connectivity scores: *"Bayesian information criterion, Akaike information criterion and stability supported 3 clusters."* Exact counts from the Fig. 4d caption: **ED 437, ID 1,257, MD 1,814**, summing to 3,508. DSM categories differed across biotypes (**χ² = 48.99, P = 5.8 × 10⁻¹⁰**) but **each biotype contained ADHD, anxiety and comorbid cases without any one dominating.** ED was linked to socioeconomic disadvantage, advanced puberty and higher BMI; ID was *"most similar to controls"*; MD had fewer female participants.

**Results — what distinguishes the groups.** On the **thirteen training scales**, ED had the lowest cognition and highest social problems, rule-breaking and aggression; ID higher cognition with elevated anxious-depressed symptoms; MD intermediate with pronounced attention problems. Beyond the training inputs: **personality** differences (reward responsiveness, drive, fun-seeking and urgency elevated in ED); **environmental and familial** differences (family conflict correlating with aggression and rule-breaking most strongly in ED at **r = 0.23–0.38, P_FDR_ < 0.001**; parental drug use, antisocial behaviour and mania linked to ED externalizing symptoms at **r = 0.14–0.29**); **distinct connectome-level network deviations** from controls for each biotype; **structural and diffusion differences** (ED with the most pronounced deviations, ID with elevated fractional anisotropy, MD with reduced fractional anisotropy and delayed pubertal maturation); and **four-year symptom trajectories** with biotype-by-age interactions for withdrawn/depressed (**P_FDR_ < 0.05**), attention problems (**< 0.01**), rule-breaking (**< 0.001**), aggression (**< 0.001**), externalizing (**< 0.01**) and total problems (**< 0.01**).

**Results — external validation, applied without refitting.** *"We applied the discovery-derived DeCoDE and GMM models without refitting to 224 age-matched HBN participants."* **Cognitive–behavioural r = 0.336 (P < 0.0001, d = 0.714); emotion–attention r = 0.179 (P = 7.39 × 10⁻³, d = 0.363).** Four similarity metrics: **prevalence 0.98, clinical profile 0.89, connectivity-score distribution 0.84, connectivity pattern 0.63 / 0.57 / 0.72** for ED / ID / MD. **Permutation tests confirmed these at P < 0.001.** **And a limit the paper reports itself:** across HBN age bins from 6.04 to 16.86 years, the first dimension held *"across all age bins,"* whereas **the second** *"reached significance only in the age-matched 9 to <11-year window."*

**Results — atlas sensitivity.** With the Shen 268 atlas: ABCD **r = 0.416 and 0.200**; HBN **r = 0.358 and 0.187**; behavioural loading correlations with the primary atlas **0.96 and 0.95**; **three biotypes again**; cross-atlas prevalence similarity **0.88 in ABCD and 0.79 in HBN**, clinical profile similarity **0.98 and 0.82**.

**Results — the late-onset group.** At baseline, the 693 showed **r = 0.442 (d = 0.985)** and **r = 0.162 (d = 0.328)** on the two dimensions. Applying the model assigned them to the three biotypes with profiles resembling the diagnosed groups *"despite low baseline symptom severity."* **Structural MRI differences were not significant after covariate adjustment.** In diffusion imaging the late-onset ID group showed elevated fractional anisotropy (**P_FDR_ < 0.001, d = 0.32**), mean diffusivity (**P_FDR_ < 0.05, d = 0.10**) and longitudinal diffusivity (**P_FDR_ < 0.001, d = 0.23**).

**Results — the control analyses, and they are the right ones.** Clustering connectivity **without** behavioural alignment: *"Bayesian information criterion indicated two clusters and Akaike information criterion four clusters"*, and HBN clinical profile similarity fell to **0.65, 0.32 and 0.35** for K = 2, 3 and 4 against **0.89** for the behaviour-aligned solution. Clustering the thirteen scales alone *"yielded no clear optimal cluster number."* Behaviour-score biotyping gave overlapping groups and HBN prevalence similarity **0.62** against 0.98. And against conventional diagnosis: **DSM groups** *"showed limited differentiation from controls across original FC, sMRI and DTI"*, while in HBN, DSM ADHD subtypes *"showed no significant symptom differences."*

**Longitudinal model specification.** Linear mixed-effects by restricted maximum likelihood, age as the time variable, all covariates baseline and time-invariant, **a random intercept per participant** for repeated visits, random slopes excluded for convergence; covariates were sex, parental education, family income, marital status, medication use and intracranial volume; **type III Wald χ² tests with FDR correction**, retaining only covariates significant after correction.

**Limitations the authors state — three, and all are about what was not included.** No genomic data, *"limiting our ability to disentangle"* genetic from environmental contributions. Training used baseline imaging and selected measures, so richer longitudinal behaviour and other modalities *"may further refine the canonical dimensions."* And *"future prospective studies are needed to establish the stability and clinical utility of these biotypes."*

**Not reported or not retrievable:** **funding, conflicts of interest, data availability, code availability and author contributions**; **the number of connectivity features**; **any test against a null of no clustering**; **the cluster-stability statistic** (Supplementary Fig. 13); **any treatment of sibling or twin relatedness**; **any site random effect in the longitudinal models**; **any reporting guideline or prespecification**; and all figures and supplementary material.

## 3. How Strong Is This Evidence? (Grade 4 out of 5)

**The best-validated unsupervised subtyping study this reading list has assessed: a genuinely held-out cohort tested without refitting, a full robustness check against the study's most arbitrary analytic choice, high cross-fold stability reported with numbers, and a set of control analyses that could have embarrassed the authors and are reported anyway. It falls short of 5 because the existence of the clusters is never tested against a null of no clustering, because relatedness in a deliberately family-based cohort is never addressed, and because the abstract's "distinct symptom profiles" and "before symptom onset" both outrun what was shown.**

**What is done well, and the first item is the hardest test in this field.**

**The external validation is real, and it was applied without refitting.** *"We applied the discovery-derived DeCoDE and GMM models without refitting to 224 age-matched HBN participants."* **Different study, different city, different sites, different scanners, different children, different preprocessing pipeline.** Both dimensions replicated with modest attenuation (**0.412 → 0.336** and **0.212 → 0.179**, about 18% and 16%), the three biotypes reappeared with prevalence similarity **0.98** and clinical profile similarity **0.89**, and **permutation tests confirmed the correspondences at P < 0.001**. **The contrast with yesterday's entry — from substantially the same senior authors, in the same journal — is instructive and worth stating: there, "generalization" meant correlating two weighted sums of the same scans; here it means a frozen model meeting new children.** This is what the word should mean.

**The atlas sensitivity analysis is a genuine robustness check on the study's most arbitrary choice.** Everything downstream depends on carving the cortex into 100 regions. Repeating the whole pipeline with a **268-region atlas** recovered both dimensions (**r = 0.416 and 0.200**), gave behavioural loadings correlating **0.96 and 0.95** with the originals, **found three biotypes again**, and preserved cross-atlas clinical profile similarity at **0.98 in ABCD**. Most neuroimaging papers pick one parcellation and never look back.

**Stability of the dimensions is high and reported in the main text.** **Connectivity loading ICC 0.89 and 0.91; behaviour loading ICC 0.99 and 0.99** across the ten folds. And the correlations themselves are **out-of-fold**, so they are held-out estimates rather than fitted ones.

**The confound checks cover the two things that actually worry people about ABCD imaging.** Representational similarity analysis found *"minimal association between the FC score representation and site, scanner, sex, age or mean framewise displacement."* **Site and head motion are the standard explanations for spurious ABCD connectivity findings, and both were examined.** Run-level scores were **averaged within participants**, so run-level pseudo-replication is avoided. Connectivity was demeaned within run to remove global offsets.

**The control analyses are the ones a sceptic would demand, and the numbers could have gone badly.** Clustering connectivity alone gave **BIC = 2 clusters and AIC = 4** and much worse external profile similarity (**0.65 / 0.32 / 0.35** against 0.89). Clustering behaviour alone *"yielded no clear optimal cluster number."* Behaviour-score biotyping gave overlapping groups and external prevalence similarity of **0.62**. **Reporting that your method only works when both views are combined is informative; reporting that one of your two model-selection criteria disagrees with the other on closely related data is the kind of thing most papers quietly omit.** Ablations of each architectural component are also reported.

**The right benchmark was used.** The comparison is not against chance but **against conventional DSM diagnosis** — and the paper reports that DSM groups *"showed limited differentiation from controls across original FC, sMRI and DTI"* while DSM ADHD subtypes in HBN *"showed no significant symptom differences."* **If the point of a new classification is to beat the existing one, that is the comparison to run.**

**And the paper is honest in several specific places.** It calls its inputs *"the 13 training scales."* It reports that **the second dimension reached significance only in the matched age window** across HBN age bins, and discusses why. It reports that **structural MRI differences in the late-onset group were not significant** after adjustment. It discloses a subtle technical consequence of its own loss weighting — that the smaller group's estimate *"can have higher variance and greater sensitivity to individual observations."* And it scopes its ethics statement correctly for a secondary analysis.

**Why this is not a 5.**

**The central object — the clusters — is never tested against a null of no clustering.** *"Bayesian information criterion, Akaike information criterion and stability supported 3 clusters."* **BIC and AIC choose among candidate cluster counts inside the Gaussian-mixture family. Neither asks whether the data are clustered at all.** A mixture model fitted to a single skewed or heavy-tailed cloud routinely prefers two or three components to one, because extra components fit the shape of the tails. **No gap statistic, no permutation of the data to destroy grouping structure, no comparison against a unimodal reference distribution is reported.** And the paper's own results show these criteria are shaky here: **on connectivity alone, BIC said two and AIC said four.** **The external validation does partly substitute** — a pure-noise partition should not transfer with matching symptom profiles — **but "reproducible partition of a continuum" and "three kinds of children" are different claims, and the word "biotype" asserts the second.**

**The cluster-stability statistic is unreachable.** *"Clustering stability via repeated subsampling"* is claimed, with the evidence in **Supplementary Fig. 13**. The dimension stability is given as ICCs in the main text; **the cluster stability is not, so there is no number to check** on the paper's headline object.

**Relatedness is never addressed, and it bites harder here than usual.** The longitudinal model's only random effect is a **participant** intercept for repeated visits. **There is no family random effect and no site random effect, and the words "sibling," "twin" and "relatedness" appear nowhere in the paper.** ABCD deliberately over-recruited siblings and twins. The cross-sectional tests treat all 3,508 children as independent. **And the consequence is specific: the biotypes are characterised partly by family-level variables — socioeconomic status, "non-intact family structure," family conflict, parental psychopathology — which siblings share by definition.** Two brothers in the ED group are one family's evidence that the group tracks family adversity, not two children's. **That the paper checked site effects on the representation but not family structure in its models shows it was thinking about dependence, just not this kind.**

**Part of the "distinct symptom profiles" restates the model inputs.** **Eight of the thirteen training measures are parent-reported CBCL subscales**, and the biotypes are then shown to differ on those subscales. **The paper is transparent, calling them "the 13 training scales," and a substantial amount of the supporting evidence genuinely is independent** — personality, environmental risk, connectome networks, structural and diffusion imaging, puberty, four-year trajectories, the HBN cohort, the DSM comparison. **But the abstract's "three biotypes... with distinct symptom profiles" reads as a finding when part of it is a description of what went in.**

**"Detectable before symptom onset" is stronger than what was shown.** The late-onset children had **no diagnosis**, not no symptoms — the paper's own words are *"despite low baseline symptom severity."* Being below a diagnostic threshold is a weaker condition than being unaffected, and the behavioural inputs are continuous. **Structural differences were null after adjustment**, and the diffusion effect sizes were **d = 0.32, 0.10 and 0.23** — small, with one reaching significance mainly because 693 children is a lot of children.

**The second dimension is thin.** **r = 0.212 is 4.5% of variance**, and it is the dimension carrying the internalizing and anxiety half of the transdiagnostic story. **It also failed to reach significance outside the matched age window in HBN** — which the paper reports honestly, but which limits it to a narrow developmental slice.

**And the comparison group is unusually clean.** Controls are children with **no diagnosis at any point across the entire study**, screened for future health as well as present. **In a contrastive method, that background defines what counts as disorder-specific** — so an unusually healthy background will push ordinary population variation into the "salient" space. The paper does not discuss this.

**Also against it:** the **feature space is never stated** (4,950 connections); **56% of the ABCD cohort was retained** and children with other psychiatric diagnoses were excluded entirely; **funding, conflicts, data and code are all not retrievable**; the **limitations paragraph has three items, all about modalities not included**, and names none of the concerns above; and **no reporting guideline or prespecification** is given.

**Why this is not a 3.** Entries 85, 86 and 87 each earned a 3 with a central claim tested only internally, or against a comparator that could not settle it. **Here a frozen model met an independent cohort and the result held; the headline survived changing the parcellation; the dimensions are stable at ICC 0.89–0.99; the two standard imaging confounds were checked; and the control analyses that could have undercut the method are reported with numbers.** The defects are in what the clusters *mean* and in how the abstract describes them, not in whether the analysis was done carefully.

**What would make this a 5, concretely.** **Test the clusters against a null:** fit the mixture to permuted or unimodally-resampled data and show three components still win. **Report the cluster-stability statistic in the main text**, as the dimension ICCs already are. **Add a family random effect** — ABCD ships family identifiers, and the re-analysis is a line of code. **State the connectivity feature count.** **And soften two abstract phrases** to "distinct profiles on the measures used for training and on independent measures" and "detectable below diagnostic threshold." **Every one of these uses data already in hand.**

## 4. The Editor's Concerns

**1. BIC and AIC do not test whether clusters exist — and the paper's own results show they are unreliable here.** *"Bayesian information criterion, Akaike information criterion and stability supported 3 clusters."* **Both criteria compare candidate cluster counts within the Gaussian-mixture family; neither asks whether the data are clustered at all.** Fitted to one skewed cloud, a mixture model will often prefer two or three components, because extra components absorb the tails. **No gap statistic, no permutation null, no unimodal reference comparison is reported.** And the control analyses demonstrate the problem: **on connectivity alone, BIC chose two clusters and AIC chose four**, and clustering the behavioural scales alone *"yielded no clear optimal cluster number."* **So "three" is a property of this particular behaviour-aligned representation.** The external validation is real evidence that the partition reproduces — but reproducibility of a cut is not evidence that the cut falls at a boundary.

**2. Relatedness in a family-based cohort is never addressed.** The only random effect is a participant intercept for repeated visits. **No family effect, no site effect, and "sibling," "twin" and "relatedness" appear nowhere.** ABCD over-recruited siblings and twins by design. **The specific consequence: biotypes are characterised by family-level variables that siblings share by definition** — socioeconomic status, *"non-intact family structure,"* family conflict, parental psychopathology — so sibling pairs inflate the apparent evidence that biotypes track family adversity. The cross-sectional tests (P = 4.60 × 10⁻¹⁴⁴, 4.55 × 10⁻³⁷) treat 3,508 children as independent. **Partial credit: run-level scores were averaged within participants, so run-level duplication was handled; family-level dependence was not.**

**3. The cluster-stability statistic is unreachable, on the paper's headline object.** Dimension stability is given as ICCs in the main text (0.89–0.99). **Cluster stability is claimed but lives in Supplementary Fig. 13.** For a paper whose contribution is three groups, the number quantifying whether those groups survive resampling belongs in the main text.

**4. Eight of thirteen inputs are the outcome measures, from one informant.** The mental-health inputs are all **parent-reported CBCL**, and the biotypes are then shown to differ on CBCL. **The paper is transparent — "the 13 training scales" — and the genuinely independent evidence is substantial** (personality, family and environmental risk, connectome networks, structural and diffusion MRI, puberty, four-year trajectories, HBN, DSM comparison). **But the abstract does not make the distinction, and a single-informant source for all eight mental-health inputs is itself a limitation**: a parent's rating style contributes to every one of them.

**5. The control group is screened for future health, which matters for a contrastive method.** Controls have **no diagnosis at any point throughout the study**. In a contrastive autoencoder, the control group **defines** what counts as ordinary variation; an unusually clean background pushes more of the population's normal variability into the "disorder-specific" space. **Combined with excluding children with other psychiatric diagnoses entirely, this makes the diagnosed-versus-control contrast cleaner than clinical reality.** Not discussed.

**6. "Detectable before symptom onset" overstates "below diagnostic threshold."** The late-onset children lacked a *diagnosis*, and the paper's own words are *"despite low baseline symptom severity."* The inputs are continuous symptom scores, so subthreshold symptoms were present and the dimensions are behaviour-aligned by construction. **Structural MRI differences were not significant after adjustment**, and diffusion effects were **d = 0.32, 0.10 and 0.23**. **A genuine strength to set against this: biotype assignment at test time uses connectivity scores only.**

**7. The second dimension explains 4.5% of variance and does not survive outside the matched ages.** **r = 0.212** in ABCD, **0.179** in HBN — and across HBN age bins it *"reached significance only in the age-matched 9 to <11-year window."* **This is the dimension carrying the anxiety half of a paper about ADHD and anxiety.** The paper reports the age limitation honestly and offers a developmental explanation, which is the right handling — but the transdiagnostic claim rests on a narrow slice.

**8. The feature space is never stated.** **100 regions means 4,950 connections**; the Shen sensitivity analysis implies 35,778. The sparse approximation retains **500 connections, about 10% of the Schaefer set**, at a sparsity level chosen *"based on correlation trends."* **Most of the multiplicity is handled legitimately** — cross-validated out-of-fold correlations, FDR on the correlational analyses, permutation tests — **but a reader cannot judge the sparsity choice without knowing the denominator.**

**9. Retention and exclusion are large and partly unexplained.** **6,148 of over 11,000 children were retained, about 56%**, and the 6,148 **is** the three analytic groups — so children with other psychiatric diagnoses were dropped entirely. The exclusion reasons are named (incomplete imaging, missing data, failed QC) but **no breakdown by group is given**, and differential attrition between diagnosed and undiagnosed children would matter for a contrastive method.

**10. The limitations paragraph names none of the above.** Three limitations are given — no genomic data, limited input modalities, prospective studies needed — **all about what was not included rather than about what the included analysis can support.** Nothing on the null-clustering question, relatedness, the single-informant inputs, the supernormal controls, or the gap between "no diagnosis" and "no symptoms."

**11. The clinical framing runs ahead of 437 children.** The paper proposes *"biotype-informed psychotherapy approaches, such as tailored emotion-regulation and impulse-control modules"* and *"early risk stratification."* **The ED biotype — the most distinctive and the one carrying the strongest neurobiological signal — contains 437 children**, and no treatment was given or tested. The paper does call for prospective trials, which is the right caveat.

**12. No guideline, no prespecification — a seventh consecutive entry, and the registry lesson does not apply.** 83 named none and met TRIPOD's substance; 84 claimed STROBE and TRIPOD and missed TRIPOD's core items; 85 named none and landed between; 86 met the modelling items and ignored measurement validity; 87 met the data-handling items and ignored the comparator; **88 named none but its parent trial was registered in 2011, so its question could be verified as prespecified.** **Entry 89 names none and has nothing to check against: it is a secondary analysis of open data with no registration, no analysis plan and no prespecification statement.** **That is the ordinary case for data-reuse studies, and it is why yesterday's registry check was a luxury rather than a routine** — most secondary analyses of shared cohorts leave no record of what was planned before the data were seen. **Seven consecutive entries, seven combinations.**

## 5. Statistics Spotlight

Three concepts, numbered from 373 in `queue.md`. The first is the question this paper's central claim turns on.

**Choosing the number of clusters is not the same as testing whether clusters exist.**

**This is the single most important idea for reading any subtyping paper, and it is the gap at the centre of this one.**

**What the paper did.** It fed each child's brain-connectivity scores to a **Gaussian mixture model** — a method that assumes the data come from a few overlapping bell-shaped clouds and works out where those clouds sit. To pick how many clouds, it used **BIC and AIC**, two standard criteria that balance how well a model fits against how complicated it is. Both pointed to **three**.

**What those criteria actually answer.** BIC and AIC answer: *given that I am going to describe this data as a mixture of bell curves, how many bell curves should I use?* **They do not answer: is this data a mixture of bell curves at all?** Every candidate they compare is a clustered model. One cluster is usually in the candidate set, but it loses for a reason that has nothing to do with real groups: **extra components also absorb skewness and heavy tails.**

**The everyday version, and it is the whole lesson.** Take the heights of ten thousand adults. **Height is a smooth continuum** — there are no natural "height groups," just a bell curve with a bit of a bulge from the mix of sexes. Hand that list to a clustering program and ask for the best number of groups. **It will give you two or three, and tell you confidently that they fit better than one.** The groups are real cuts through real data. They are not kinds of people.

**Why this matters so much here.** The paper calls its groups **"biotypes"** — a word that means *kinds*, with the implication of distinct underlying biology and, eventually, distinct treatments. **If the underlying landscape is a continuum of cognitive control and emotion regulation, then the three groups are three regions of one terrain**, and a child near a boundary could land either side on a different day. **If there are genuinely separate configurations, the groups are discoveries.** BIC and AIC cannot tell those apart.

**And this paper's own results show the criteria wobbling.** When the authors clustered connectivity **without** the behavioural alignment, **BIC said two clusters and AIC said four.** When they clustered the thirteen behaviour measures alone, the result *"yielded no clear optimal cluster number."* **Two criteria disagreeing by a factor of two on closely related data is a warning that neither is measuring cluster existence.**

**What a test of existence looks like.** Several exist, and none is reported here. **The simplest: destroy the structure and see if the answer survives.** Shuffle each feature's values independently across children — this preserves every variable's distribution while abolishing any joint grouping — then fit the mixture again. **If three components still look better than one on shuffled data, your criterion was never detecting clusters.** Other standard tools are the **gap statistic** (comparing your fit against data drawn from a deliberately cluster-free reference distribution) and **the dip test** (asking directly whether a distribution has more than one peak).

**What does count as evidence here, in the paper's favour.** The three groups **reproduced in an independent cohort** with prevalence similarity 0.98, clinical profile similarity 0.89 and permutation P < 0.001. **A partition of pure noise should not transfer with matching symptom patterns.** So the groups are reproducible and they carry real information. **That is genuine and it partly substitutes for the missing test — but a reproducible cut through a continuum is also reproducible.**

**Watch out for:** any paper announcing subtypes, biotypes, endophenotypes, phenotypic clusters or patient strata. **Three questions. Which criterion chose the number of groups — and does it compare cluster counts, or test cluster existence?** **Was there any null comparison: permuted data, a gap statistic, a dip test, or a unimodal reference?** **And do the reported groups overlap?** A figure showing clouds with heavy overlap and probabilistic membership — as this paper's Fig. 4a explicitly does, showing *"posterior membership probabilities"* — is itself evidence of a continuum being partitioned. **The tell that should make you cautious: a paper that reports the chosen number of clusters but never reports what one cluster would have scored.**

**Out-of-sample validation means a frozen model meeting new people — and this paper shows what that looks like.**

**Yesterday's entry in this reading list and today's come from substantially the same senior authors, in the same journal, and they use the phrase "independent cohort" to mean two quite different things. The comparison is the clearest lesson available on what the words should mean.**

**What this paper did.** *"We applied the discovery-derived DeCoDE and GMM models **without refitting** to 224 age-matched HBN participants."* **Nothing was re-estimated.** The connectivity weights, the dimension loadings and the cluster boundaries were all fixed in ABCD, then applied to children from a different study, in a different city, scanned on different machines with different settings, processed through a different pipeline. **Everything that could differ, did.**

**And the result behaved the way a true finding behaves.** The first dimension went from **r = 0.412 to r = 0.336**; the second from **0.212 to 0.179**. **Both weakened — by about 18% and 16% — and that attenuation is the honest signature of a real effect meeting new data.** A result that transfers *unchanged* to a new cohort should make you suspicious, not confident; some shrinkage is expected, because the original estimate was fitted on its own sample and the new sample differs in a hundred small ways.

**By contrast, the weaker form.** In yesterday's paper, "independent cohort" meant *the group of patients not used to derive a particular set of weights* — but **both quantities being correlated were weighted sums of the same brain scans of the same people.** That is a meaningful check on where weights came from. **It is not a frozen model meeting strangers.**

**Here is a ladder of what the phrase can mean, weakest to strongest.** Cross-validation inside one dataset. A held-out slice of the same dataset. **A different group of participants from the same study, same scanners.** A different study with different scanners — which is this paper. A different study in a different country or health system. **And strongest of all, a prospective test where the model makes its predictions before the outcomes exist.**

**The everyday version.** You tune a recipe in your own kitchen and it comes out beautifully twenty times. **That is cross-validation.** You then cook it, unchanged, in someone else's kitchen with their oven and their ingredients, and it comes out a little less good but recognisably the same dish. **That is external validation, and the slight falling-off is exactly what you should expect.** If it came out identical, you would wonder whether you had secretly brought your own oven.

**Watch out for:** the word "validation," which covers everything on that ladder. **Three questions. Was anything re-estimated on the new data — any refitting, rescaling, recalibration or threshold adjustment? If so, it is not a frozen model.** **What actually changed between development and evaluation: new participants, new site, new scanner, new time period, or only a new slice of the same table?** **And did performance drop?** A reported drop of 10–25% is reassuring. **No drop at all usually means something leaked.** A very large drop means the model learned something local — and this paper supplies that case too, in its own honest report that the **second dimension failed outside the matched age window**.

**A family-based cohort needs a family-level term, and the reason is about counting evidence.**

**Most statistical tests assume your observations are independent — that each one is a fresh, separate piece of evidence. ABCD is built in a way that breaks that assumption on purpose, and this paper does not account for it.**

**What ABCD did and why.** The study deliberately recruited **brothers, sisters and twins**, because comparing relatives is how you separate genetic from environmental influences. **That design choice means a large number of the 3,508 children here share a household, a family income, a neighbourhood, parents, and in the case of twins most of their DNA.**

**What the paper's model allows for.** The longitudinal model includes **a random intercept per participant**, which correctly handles the fact that each child was measured at several visits. **It includes no family term.** The words "sibling," "twin" and "relatedness" do not appear in the paper. The cross-sectional correlation tests treat all 3,508 children as independent.

**What goes wrong, in plain terms.** Suppose you want to know whether family conflict goes with aggression. **You survey 100 households and get one child from each: 100 independent pieces of evidence.** Now suppose you survey 50 households and get two siblings from each. **You still have 100 rows in your spreadsheet, but you do not have 100 pieces of evidence — you have something closer to 50**, because the two siblings in a household share the same family conflict and much of the same upbringing. **Treat them as 100 and your confidence intervals come out too narrow and your P-values too small**, by roughly the square root of the inflation.

**Why it bites unusually hard in this particular paper.** The biotypes are characterised partly by **family-level variables**: socioeconomic status, *"non-intact family structure,"* family conflict, parental psychopathology, parental drug use. **Siblings share every one of those by definition.** So when two brothers both land in the externalizing group and both have high family-conflict scores, that is **one family's worth of evidence that the group tracks family adversity, counted twice.** The reported correlations in that range — **r = 0.23 to 0.38 for family conflict with aggression** — are the ones most affected.

**How much does it change things?** For the headline dimensions, probably not the conclusion: **r = 0.412 with a P-value of 10⁻¹⁴⁴ has enormous room to spare.** For the small effects — the second dimension at r = 0.212, the family-level correlations, the biotype-by-age interactions sitting at P_FDR_ just under 0.05 — **it could matter a great deal.** The fix is cheap: ABCD distributes family identifiers, and adding a family random effect is one line.

**The everyday version.** A restaurant has 200 glowing reviews. **Then you notice 120 of them were written by members of four families who all ate there together.** The reviews are not fake and the food may be excellent. **You just have far less independent evidence than the number 200 suggested.**

**Watch out for:** any analysis of a cohort that recruited **families, households, twins, schools, clinics, villages or hospitals**. **Three questions. Does the recruitment design create groups of related observations — and does the paper say so?** **Is there a random effect, cluster-robust standard error, or sensitivity analysis restricted to one member per group?** **And are the key findings the small ones?** Large effects usually survive the correction; **the findings sitting just inside the significance threshold are exactly the ones that may not.** **The tell to look for: a paper that carefully adjusts for site or scanner but says nothing about family** — which is this paper, and it shows the authors were thinking about dependence, just not about this kind.

## 6. Jargon Translator

**ADHD (attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder)** — a condition involving inattention, impulsivity and hyperactivity. **Anxiety disorders (AXD)** — here including generalised anxiety, social anxiety, specific phobia, separation anxiety, panic disorder, agoraphobia and selective mutism.

**Comorbidity** — having two conditions at once. ADHD and anxiety co-occur often, which is the problem this paper addresses.

**Transdiagnostic** — cutting across diagnostic categories rather than respecting them. **The paper's central aim: find groupings that ignore the ADHD/anxiety boundary.**

**Biotype** — a subgroup defined by biological measurements rather than symptoms. **A word that asserts the groups are kinds, which is the claim Section 5 examines.**

**DSM-5** — the diagnostic manual whose categories the paper argues *"may obscure shared mechanisms."*

**ABCD study** — a US study following over 11,000 children from about age nine, with imaging, cognitive and psychiatric assessment. **Deliberately recruited siblings and twins** — technique 374.

**HBN (Healthy Brain Network)** — a separate New York study of over 4,000 participants aged 5–21, used here as the **external validation** cohort.

**Secondary analysis** — re-using data someone else collected. **No new participants, no new consent, compensation under the original protocols.**

**Resting-state fMRI** — scanning the brain while the person lies still doing nothing. **About 20 minutes per child here.**

**Functional connectivity (FC)** — how strongly two brain regions' activity rises and falls together. **100 regions here gives 4,950 region pairs — a number the paper never states.**

**Parcellation / Schaefer atlas / Shen atlas** — ways of carving the brain into regions. **The paper repeated everything with a second atlas, which is a real robustness check.**

**Framewise displacement** — how much the head moved between scan frames. **Runs above 0.5 mm were excluded.** Motion is the classic source of false connectivity findings in children.

**ComBat harmonisation** — a statistical adjustment for differences between scanners and sites.

**Default mode, frontoparietal control, dorsal and ventral attention, limbic, sensorimotor and visual networks** — standard groupings of regions that activate together.

**CBCL (Child Behavior Checklist)** — a **parent-reported** questionnaire about a child's behaviour and mood. **Eight of its subscales were model inputs here**, which is why Section 4 raises circularity.

**NIH Toolbox / WISC-V** — standard batteries of cognitive tests. **Five cognitive measures were model inputs.**

**Contrastive variational autoencoder (cVAE)** — a neural network that learns what is *different* about one group by explicitly comparing it against a background group, separating **"salient"** (disorder-specific) from **"shared"** variation.

**Canonical correlation analysis (CCA) / deep generalized CCA** — methods that find the combination of brain features most strongly related to a combination of behaviour features. **"Deep" means neural networks allow non-straight-line relationships.**

**Orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP)** — a way of approximating a complicated model with a small number of features. **500 connections retained per dimension, about 10% of the set.**

**Gaussian mixture model (GMM)** — assumes data come from a few overlapping bell-shaped clouds and estimates where they sit. **Produces probabilities of belonging rather than hard labels** — which is itself a hint about continuity.

**BIC and AIC** — criteria for choosing how complicated a model should be. **They compare cluster counts; they do not test whether clusters exist** — technique 373.

**Gap statistic / dip test / permutation null** — the tools that *do* test whether clusters exist. **None is reported here.**

**Cross-validation / out-of-fold** — training on part of the data and testing on the held-out part. **The reported dimension correlations are out-of-fold, which is the right way.**

**ICC (intraclass correlation)** — agreement across repeated estimates. **0.89 to 0.99 here across folds — genuinely high.**

**Cohen's d** — a standardised effect size. Roughly: 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large. **0.905 for the first dimension, 0.435 for the second.**

**r and r²** — correlation and the share of variance it explains. **r = 0.412 is 17.0% of variance; r = 0.212 is 4.5%.**

**FDR (false discovery rate) correction** — adjusting for having run many tests. **Applied to the correlational and longitudinal analyses here.**

**Permutation test** — shuffling labels many times to see how often chance reproduces a result. **Used for the external validation correspondences.**

**Mixed-effects model / random intercept** — a model allowing each participant their own baseline. **A participant intercept is included here; a family intercept is not** — technique 374.

**Fractional anisotropy / mean diffusivity** — diffusion MRI measures of white-matter structure.

**Late-onset** — here, children with no diagnosis at baseline who received one during follow-up. **"No diagnosis" is not "no symptoms"** — technique 378.

## 7. What You Can (and Can't) Say

**You can say:**

"In 3,508 children aged 9–11 with ADHD or an anxiety disorder from the ABCD study, a deep-learning framework contrasting their brain connectivity against 1,947 children with no diagnosis identified two brain–behaviour dimensions: cognitive–behavioural dysfunction (out-of-fold r = 0.412) and emotion–attention dysfunction (r = 0.212)."

"Clustering on these dimensions produced three subgroups — externalizing-disinhibited (437 children, 12.5%), internalizing-distressed (1,257, 35.8%) and mixed dysregulation (1,814, 51.7%) — each containing ADHD, anxiety and comorbid cases without one diagnosis dominating."

"The model and the cluster assignment were applied without refitting to 224 age-matched Healthy Brain Network participants, where both dimensions replicated (r = 0.336 and 0.179) and the subgroups showed prevalence similarity 0.98 and clinical profile similarity 0.89, confirmed by permutation tests at P < 0.001."

"Repeating the entire pipeline with a 268-region atlas recovered both dimensions, with behavioural loadings correlating 0.96 and 0.95 with the original, and again produced three subgroups."

"Dimension loadings were stable across cross-validation folds (connectivity ICC 0.89 and 0.91; behaviour ICC 0.99), and the connectivity representation showed minimal association with site, scanner, sex, age or head motion."

"The subgroups differed in personality, family and environmental risk, connectome network organisation, structural and diffusion imaging, pubertal development and four-year symptom trajectories."

"Conventional DSM groupings showed limited differentiation from controls across connectivity, structural and diffusion measures, and DSM ADHD subtypes in HBN showed no significant symptom differences."

"In 693 children who had no diagnosis at baseline but developed one later, the dimensions were already associated with behaviour at baseline, although structural MRI differences were not significant after covariate adjustment."

**You cannot say:**

"The study found three distinct kinds of children." **The number of clusters was chosen by BIC and AIC, which compare cluster counts and do not test whether clusters exist.** No permutation null, gap statistic or dip test is reported — and on connectivity alone the paper's own BIC and AIC disagreed (two versus four).

"The biotypes have distinct symptom profiles." **Eight of the thirteen model inputs were the parent-reported symptom scales**, so this partly restates the inputs. The paper calls them "the 13 training scales"; the abstract does not.

"Brain patterns are detectable before symptoms appear." **These children had no diagnosis, not no symptoms** — the paper's words are "despite low baseline symptom severity" — and the behavioural inputs are continuous.

"This could be used to assign treatments." **No treatment was given or tested**, and the paper itself calls for prospective trials.

"The findings hold across childhood and adolescence." **The second dimension reached significance only in the matched 9-to-under-11 window** across HBN age bins, which the paper reports.

"3,508 children provide 3,508 independent observations." **ABCD deliberately recruited siblings and twins, and no family-level term appears anywhere in the analysis.** The family-level correlations are the ones most affected.

"The brain data alone identified the subgroups." **Without behavioural alignment the clustering gave a different number of clusters and much weaker external replication** (profile similarity 0.65 / 0.32 / 0.35 against 0.89).

"The internalizing group is biologically distinct from controls." **That group was "most similar to controls"** in cognition and demographics; its distinguishing features were internalizing symptoms and a diffusion-imaging signature with small effect sizes.

"The subgroups explain the heterogeneity of ADHD and anxiety." **The two dimensions explain 17.0% and 4.5% of the brain–behaviour relationship respectively.**

"The comparison group represents typical children." **Controls were defined as having no diagnosis at any point throughout the study**, and children with other psychiatric diagnoses were excluded from the analysis entirely.

**The honest one-sentence version:** *the best-validated unsupervised subtyping study this reading list has assessed — a frozen model applied to an independent cohort with different scanners and different children, both dimensions replicating with the modest attenuation a real effect shows, the result surviving a change of brain parcellation, dimension loadings stable at ICC 0.89–0.99, and control analyses reported with numbers that could have embarrassed the authors — which nonetheless never tests whether its three groups are kinds rather than three regions of one continuum, never accounts for the siblings and twins that ABCD recruited on purpose, and describes as "distinct symptom profiles" a set of differences on measures that were fed into the model.*

## 8. Bottom Line for Your Life

**If your child has ADHD or an anxiety diagnosis:** nothing here changes their care, and no clinic can place a child into one of these three groups today. **But the paper's starting observation is worth knowing, because it may match your experience.** Two children with the same diagnosis can be genuinely different: one struggling mainly with impulse control and rule-breaking, another anxious and distressed while doing fine at school, a third mainly inattentive. **This study is evidence that those differences are not just impressions — they line up with measurable differences in how the brain is organised, and they predicted how symptoms changed over the following four years.** That is a reason to describe your child's particular difficulties to a clinician in detail rather than relying on the label, which is good practice regardless of whether these three groups turn out to be real categories.

**One finding deserves singling out, because it cuts against intuition.** The **internalizing-distressed** group — about a third of the diagnosed children — had **thinking and school-related scores like children with no diagnosis at all**, while carrying substantial anxiety and distress. The paper notes this group *"resembled controls in cognitive performance and demographics, potentially obscuring early recognition,"* and showed **less improvement over time with rising depressive symptoms**. **An anxious child who is doing fine academically is the one most likely to be overlooked, and on this evidence may be the one whose difficulties persist.** That is worth holding onto even if the three-group scheme never reaches a clinic.

**If you follow mental-health research, here is the reading skill this entry teaches.** Papers announcing **subtypes, biotypes or patient clusters** appear constantly, and almost all of them report how many groups they found without reporting **whether there were any groups to find.** Those are different questions. **The methods used here — BIC and AIC — pick the best number of clusters among candidates that are all clustered; they never compare against "no clusters at all."** The everyday check: **heights are a smooth continuum, but ask a clustering program for the best number of height groups and it will hand you three.** So when you read about newly discovered subtypes, ask: **did they test the data against a version with the grouping deliberately destroyed?** If not, the subtypes may be real regions of a real landscape — useful, even — but not kinds.

**And the skill that cuts the other way, because this paper gets it right.** The strongest thing here is that the finished model was applied to **224 children from a different study, in a different city, on different scanners, with nothing re-estimated** — and it held, with performance dropping about 18%. **That drop is the honest signature of a real effect.** When you see "validated in an independent cohort," ask what actually changed: new people, new scanner, new hospital, new time period — **or just a new slice of the same table?** And ask whether performance dropped. **No drop at all usually means something leaked.** The paper from essentially the same group decoded yesterday used "independent cohort" in the weaker sense; this one uses it in the strong sense. **Same authors, same journal, one day apart, two different standards — which is why the question is worth asking every time.**

**A third, about counting.** This study has 3,508 children, which sounds decisive. **But ABCD deliberately recruited brothers, sisters and twins, and nothing in this analysis accounts for that.** Siblings share a household, an income, a neighbourhood and parents — and the groups here are described partly by exactly those family features. **So two brothers in the same group are one family's evidence counted twice.** The general rule: when a study recruits **families, households, twins, schools or clinics**, the number of rows in the spreadsheet is larger than the number of independent facts. **The headline findings here are large enough to survive it. The small ones — and several sit just inside the significance threshold — may not.**

**If you are reading the coverage:** expect "AI finds three types of childhood ADHD and anxiety" or "brain scans reveal hidden subtypes." **Three things will likely be missing.** That the method chose *how many* groups without ever testing *whether* there are groups. That most of the "distinct symptom profiles" are differences on the very parent questionnaires fed into the model. And that "detectable before symptom onset" describes children who were below a diagnostic threshold, not children without symptoms. **What will probably also be missing is the paper's genuine achievement: a frozen model travelling to a different city and still working.**

**This is education, not medical advice.** ADHD and anxiety disorders in children are real, common and treatable, and effective treatments exist. **Nothing described here is available or validated for clinical use**, and decisions about a child's assessment or treatment belong with a clinician who knows them.
