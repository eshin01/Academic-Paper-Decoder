## 2026-10-03 — two reusable appraisal techniques, both proven today

**1. Deliberately pair papers that took opposite turns at the same
methodological fork.** Yesterday's Shimizu scorecard (entry 34) refused to
rebalance a rare outcome, explicitly to protect calibration, and its
calibration came out near-perfect. Today's Ye triage tool (entry 36) applied
SMOTE, and its calibration claim is the weakest thing in the paper — it
benchmarks its Brier score against 0.25, the no-information value for a
*balanced* set, when the no-information value at its real 19.9% prevalence is
0.159. Decoding the two back-to-back taught the rebalancing/calibration
trade-off far better than either alone could. **When a queued paper makes a
choice a recently-decoded paper refused, schedule it next and decode it as a
pair.** Keep a note of live forks worth pairing: rebalance vs refuse (done);
internal vs external validation (Lu J, entry pending, is the natural partner
for anything claiming internal performance); abstract screening vs full-text
screening in meta-analyses.

**2. Compute the trivial baseline from the paper's own Table 1, every time.**
For a binary rule, AUC = (sensitivity + specificity) / 2, and a baseline table
that reports a predictor by outcome group gives you both. Today "refer every
man" came to sensitivity 0.564, specificity 0.593, AUC 0.578 — against the
paper's nine-algorithm, twenty-one-predictor 0.672. The model is better, but
knowing by how much reframes the abstract entirely. This takes under a minute
and should be done before reading any Discussion section.

**3. Check where the decision threshold came from.** New failure mode seen
today, and distinct from the familiar feature-selection leak (which this paper
correctly avoided): Ye et al. state that "the optimal probability cutoffs were
derived from the validation dataset" — the same hold-out used to report the
headline recall. The threshold is a fitted parameter; tuning it on the test set
makes the reported sensitivity an upper bound. Look for the sentence naming the
threshold's provenance in every prediction-model paper from now on.

## 2026-10-08 — a fifth reusable technique, and the unit-of-analysis theme consolidating

**5. Check that every ratio's confidence interval is log-symmetric.** For a ratio
measure — odds ratio, risk ratio, hazard ratio, likelihood ratio, diagnostic odds
ratio — the point estimate should sit at the GEOMETRIC mean of its interval,
sqrt(low x high), because these intervals are computed on the log scale. Today
this caught a real error. Eleven of the twelve ratio intervals in Feng et al.
pass; the directly extracted positive likelihood ratio is printed as 40.2 (95% CI
9.3-79.9), where sqrt(9.3 x 79.9) = 27.3, off by 47% — far too much to be
rounding. Solving 40.2^2 / 79.9 gives 20.2, and sqrt(20.2 x 79.9) = 40.2 exactly,
so the lower bound is almost certainly 20.2 mis-transcribed as 9.3. (A second
tell: the printed upper bound 79.9 is identical to the row below it.) The four
negative-likelihood-ratio intervals appear to fail the same check but only because
they are printed to two decimals — allow for rounding before calling a mismatch.
**This takes thirty seconds per interval, needs only a square root, and catches
transcription errors, unit mix-ups and intervals computed on the wrong scale.**

**6. In a meta-analysis, find the number attached to each pooled estimate, not
the number in the title.** Feng et al. says "42 studies were incorporated in the
meta-analysis" in its Results and, several pages later in the risk-of-bias
section, "33 studies provided only limited outcome data and could not be directly
included." The pooled numbers come from 14 contingency tables from at most 9
studies, and the patient-level claim from 415 patients. Look for "k = " in the
forest plot or count its rows, then ask how many *patients* those studies hold.

**The unit-of-analysis theme has now appeared four days running, in four
different disguises, and is worth treating as a standing check.**
- Entry 38 (Akbal, 10-05): 1,626 lesions from 158 patients — a **clustering**
  problem, fixed with patient-grouped cross-validation and cluster bootstrap.
- Entry 39 (Yu, 10-06): two eyes per patient — a **sample-size** problem, where
  cluster-robust standard errors report precision honestly but cannot increase it.
- Entry 41 (Feng, 10-08): lesion-level versus patient-level — a **task-difficulty**
  problem, where specificity falls from 0.98 to 0.86 and false alarms rise
  sevenfold because a whole brain contains hundreds of chances to be wrong once.
- And entry 40 (MedGemma, 10-07) is the same idea again: per-question accuracy on
  multiple-choice benchmarks is not per-patient diagnostic accuracy.
**Standing question for every paper from now on: what is the unit the accuracy is
measured in, and is it the unit the clinical decision is made in?** It almost
never is, and the clinical unit is almost always the worse number.

**Techniques added to the toolkit from this decode:**

**7. With zero events, apply the rule of three before believing a safety claim.**
Zero events in n observations bounds the rate at roughly 3/n. Here zero safety
stops in 100 interactions bounds the true rate at 2.95%, i.e. up to about 3 in
100 — and ~3,000 supervised conversations would be needed to bound it below 1 in
1,000. Notably this paper computed binomial confidence intervals for its
diagnostic accuracy and its blinding check but **not** for its headline safety
result, which is the one everyone will quote.

**8. Check whether the blinding was verified, not just claimed.** The best method
in this paper: evaluators were asked to guess which output was AMIE's and which
the physician's. They were right 58 of 98 times, 59.18% (95% CI 49.45%-68.91%) —
an interval that contains 50%, so they were guessing and the blind held. Note the
inverted logic: a HIGH correct-identification rate would have been the bad
result. First paper in 43 to do this. From now on, for any "blinded assessment",
ask whether the assessors were asked to guess and what the interval was.

**9. Ask what k is in any top-k accuracy figure.** The abstract's "included the
final diagnosis in 90% of cases" is **top-seven**, unlabelled. Top-3 is 75% and
is labelled. Top-1 — the single best guess, which is what a patient would be
handed — is 55/98 = 56%, and appears in neither abstract.

**10. Watch for ceiling effects in rating rubrics.** "Seeking and addressing
concerns" scored very favourable in 98 of 98 cases; five more criteria sat within
three cases of the top. A saturated measure cannot discriminate and cannot detect
future regression. The informative criterion was the one that was NOT at the
ceiling: family history, where 43 of 98 were marked not applicable, leaving a real
denominator of 55 with 4 unfavourable (7.3%).

**11. Follow the denominator downhill in survey findings.** 98 encounters -> 60
physician surveys returned (61.2%) -> 44 who had actually read the transcript ->
33 who found it helpful. "75% found it helpful" is 33/44; across all encounters
it is 33/98 = 34%. Compare response rates within the same study: patients ~90%,
AI supervisors 100%, physicians 61.2%.

**A reference-standard contamination pathway worth watching for generally.** In
52 of 98 cases the "final diagnosis" was the physician's presumptive impression
with no confirmatory test — and that physician had read AMIE's transcript *and its
list of candidate diagnoses* before the visit. So in the majority of cases AMIE
may have shaped the standard it was scored against, which is the most
parsimonious explanation for the paper's own observation that accuracy trended
higher in exactly that subgroup. The authors flag the weaker standard and run the
subgroup analysis but do not name the pathway. **Generalised check: when a
reference standard is a human judgement, ask whether that human saw the model's
output first.** This is the fourth distinct flavour of reference-standard problem
in the list, after entry 37 (outcome partly caused by the decision under study),
entry 38 (gold standard only for transported patients) and entry 42 (gold
standard only when the patient attends).

paper never says how many decision points entered the primary analysis — the
most consequential omission in it, because that is the effective sample size of
the headline result. But it reports a Wilcoxon rank-sum test on the
intervention-to-outcome interval: W = 114,075, p = 0.518. R's W is the U
statistic, with expectation n1*n2/2 under the null, and p = 0.518 puts W almost
exactly at that expectation. So n1*n2 ~= 228,150; with 1:1 randomisation each arm
is ~478 and the total is **~955 analysed intervals across 42 participants, ~23
per person** — against a per-person maximum of 210, so about 11%. The estimate is
robust to the split (40/60 gives ~975). "Up to 210 decision points each" and "~23
analysed decision points each" are very different studies. **Generalised check:
reported test statistics carry information about the n they were computed on;
when an author omits an effective sample size, try to recover it and label the
recovery clearly as your own.**

**13. The unit-of-analysis question in its newest costume.** The standing question
— "what is the unit the accuracy is measured in, and is it the unit the clinical
decision is made in?" — has a sibling for within-person designs: **what is the
unit the effect is measured in, and is it the unit the uncertainty is driven
by?** Here the effect is measured in decision points (hundreds) but the
uncertainty is driven by participants (42), because observations within a person
are correlated. That is why hundreds of randomisations still leave the study
under-powered. `wcls()` exists precisely to handle this.

**14. Recompute realised power when a study under-recruits.** Planned: 60
participants, ~87% power for a standardised proximal effect of 0.10 at one-sided
alpha 0.025. Work backwards: z_crit = 1.96, z_power = 1.126, so planned
non-centrality = 3.086. Non-centrality scales with sqrt(n), so at 42 analysed it
is 3.086 * sqrt(42/60) = 2.582 and power = Phi(2.582 - 1.96) ~= **73%** (80% at
49). Not catastrophic, but it means the two null findings are weak evidence of
absence. Caveat: MRT power also depends on the availability rate, which this
paper does not state, so treat the figure as an approximation.

**15. Forecasting performance is not policy value.** The authors' own phrase, and
the sharpest idea in the paper. The model was trained and tuned to minimise EMA
*prediction* error, then deployed to *choose* among eight interventions. Those
objectives come apart: predicting your mood well says nothing about correctly
ranking counterfactual interventions. The authors write that the prediction
objective "was used as a pragmatic proxy for model selection; it does not
establish that the forecasting objective is optimally aligned with the causal
effect of the assignment policy." So the MRT validated **the policy, not the
model** — the right thing to test, but a positive result does not validate the
forecasting and a null would not have invalidated it. **Generalised check: when
an AI system is trained on one objective and deployed to make a decision, ask
which one was actually measured.** This is the cleanest statement of the
discrimination-vs-decision gap the list has collected so far, and it came from
the authors, not from a critic.

**16. Check the CI against the p-value, and prefer the version with more decimal
places.** B = 0.147, CI 0.004-0.290. Midpoint = 0.147 (symmetric, as a normal
interval should be), half-width 0.143, SE = 0.143/1.96 = 0.0730, z = 2.015,
two-sided p = 0.0439 — reproduces the stated 0.044 exactly, so estimate, interval
and p all come from one calculation with nothing mistyped. The paper then gives
the interval two ways: abstract 0.004-0.290, body text 0.01-0.29. But 0.004 to
two decimals is 0.00, not 0.01, so the body version makes the lower bound read as
a real small positive rather than essentially zero. **When two parts of a paper
disagree on a number, read the one with more decimal places.**

**Funnel arithmetic that does not close.** The distal analysis reports 44 of 49
with 4 excluded for incomplete baseline, but 49 - 4 = 45. It reconciles only by
routing through the 48 who completed the post-assessment (48 - 4 = 44), which the
text does not spell out. All other funnel steps check out exactly (58 - 9 = 49;
49 - 6 - 1 = 42; 42/49 = 86%, 44/49 = 90%, 48/49 = 98%, 7/49 = 14%). Also: the
abstract gives distress d = -0.23, the body d = -0.24; and 38 female + 10 male =
48 of 49, leaving one participant unaccounted for in the retrieved text.

**An exemplar worth keeping for contrast.** Graded 3/5 — sound design, thin
result — but the limitations section pre-empts nearly every criticism I would
have made, in the authors' own words: forecasting vs policy value; the resilience
effect "cannot be unambiguously attributed to accurate personalization" with four
named alternatives; the uncontrolled distal changes "should not be interpreted as
causal"; the exclusion comparisons "exploratory and underpowered and should not be
interpreted as evidence of equivalence"; and an explicit statement that the model
should not be assumed to detect "deterioration trajectories, or crisis contexts
that were absent from the training data." **Keep this one as the reference example
of what honest reporting of a modest result looks like** — it is the calibration
point for noticing when a paper claiming far more has done far less. Note also
what the honesty does not cover: no multiplicity correction and no mention of
multiplicity, no designated primary outcome, no registration number anywhere in
the retrieved text, no a priori feasibility thresholds ("no formal a priori
pass/fail thresholds were specified"), and the unreported decision-point count.
Candour in the discussion is not a substitute for prespecification.

**17. OPEN THE REGISTRATION RECORD. This is the highest-yield check in the whole list.**
The paper carries an NCT number, so I pulled the ClinicalTrials.gov record (NCT07359950,
"EAST-MUSIC") with the Clinical_Trials MCP tool and compared it line by line against the
paper. Six mismatches, one of them decisive:
- The registry names **a single primary outcome: change in state anxiety (STAI-S)**. In
  the paper that outcome is **null** (F(2,109)=0.68, p=.511). The abstract's one positive
  sentence is about **negative affect — a registered secondary outcome** — and the paper
  never tells the reader which outcome was registered as primary. Textbook outcome-
  reporting shift, visible only from the registry.
- Registry enrolment **123**; paper analyses **113**. The number 123 appears nowhere in
  the paper.
- Registry defines every outcome as a **change score**; the paper analysed **baseline-
  adjusted post scores (ANCOVA)**. That is a statistical *upgrade*, but it is an
  undeclared deviation from the registered plan.
- Registry exclusions (diagnosed psychiatric disorders, psychotropic medication,
  participation in another study within 30 days) are **broader than the paper's stated
  exclusions** (severe hearing impairment, cardiovascular disease).
- Registry study start **2025-10-01** vs the paper's recruitment window **23–25 January
  2026**. (The registration *posting* date is not in the retrieved record, so whether
  registration was prospective could not be determined — not guessed.)
- Registry title: "A Randomized Controlled Trial." Paper: "a pilot randomized exploratory
  study." The softening arrives after a null primary outcome.
**Make this a standing step: whenever a paper gives a registration number, fetch the
record and compare the primary outcome, the enrolment, the analysis definition and the
eligibility criteria.** It needs no statistics, takes one tool call, and here it changed
the reading of the paper entirely. Note the inverse too: the 10-11 paper (Rauschenberg)
had *no* registration number at all, so this check was unavailable — which is itself why
its "one of three outcomes" could not be audited.

**18. Confounded multi-component arms: ask what single thing differs.** The three arms
differed in music (Tan Dun's *Hero* / a clinical therapy recording / Hisaishi's
*Kikujiro*), in narration (AI Eastern-imagery / conventional / none) **and** in duration
(26m19s / 29m56s / 25m35s — the longest 17% longer than the shortest). Nothing isolates
the LLM script the title is about. **Randomisation makes the *people* comparable; only
design makes the *treatments* comparable.** The fix is factorial: hold the music fixed,
vary only the words. The authors name the flaw and recommend exactly that — acknowledgement
does not create the missing arm. This is the same family as the AMIE/MedGemma "what is the
comparison actually isolating?" question and it is now the most common defect seen in these
scans.

**19. Check whether the outcomes point the same way.** Negative affect favoured the AI-script
arm; heart rate favoured the *standard therapy* arm, with the AI arm statistically
indistinguishable from silent piano music. When one supposed mechanism produces opposite
rankings on two outcomes, the simplest explanation is that different ingredients of a
confounded bundle are driving different outcomes.

**20. A null is a claim about your own sensitivity.** With N=113, 3 arms, 1 covariate, the
design had ~80% power to detect ηp² ≈ .081 (roughly medium). Achieved power for the observed
state-anxiety effect (ηp²=.012) was **~16%**. So the two nulls rule out medium-or-larger
differences only. Always restate a null as "we could rule out effects larger than X."
Computed with a noncentral-F power calculation; λ = f²N with f² = ηp²/(1−ηp²).

**21. Partial eta squared reproduces from F, and Holm's monotonicity step explains repeated
p-values.** ηp² = df1·F/(df1·F + df2) — all four of the paper's values and the sensitivity
analysis reproduce exactly. Separately, both significant negative-affect contrasts report
p = .026, which looks like a typo and is not: raw p's recovered from the CIs are .0084,
.0094, .895; Holm gives .0251, then .0188 — but **Holm enforces monotonicity**, so the
second is raised to .0251 and both round to .026. All six Holm adjustments across both
outcomes reproduce. **Note which family was corrected:** Holm was applied to the three
pairwise contrasts *within* each outcome, not across the four outcomes.

**22. ANCOVA beats change scores in a randomised trial — and check the slopes.** Two reasons:
fairness (a group starting worse has more room to improve) and, less obviously, **precision**
(a change score carries the noise of two measurements; ANCOVA uses baseline as a predictor
without importing its full error). The assumption to verify is **homogeneity of regression
slopes** (the group × baseline interaction). These authors tested it — non-significant in the
main models (p = .113–.709), but **significant (p = .010) in the state-anxiety sensitivity
analysis**, which they correctly flag. Watch out: ANCOVA is safe here *because of*
randomisation; in observational data "adjusting for baseline" can reverse or manufacture an
effect (Lord's paradox).

**Credit where due.** The statistical execution is better than average: complete data with no
dropout, every Cronbach's α ≥ 0.84 reported, homogeneity-of-slopes tested, Levene and
Shapiro–Wilk run, HC3 robust SEs as a cross-check, a pre-stated outlier rule with sensitivity
analysis, and Holm applied correctly. The authors also state the confound, decline to claim
efficacy, refuse to call the one-arm cultural-resonance rating a manipulation check, and
recommend the right follow-up design. The 2/5 is for the design and the registry shift, not
for sloppiness.

**23. Weighted kappa, and always ask which weighting.** New to the list, and the right metric
for any ordered scale (cancer stage, NYHA class, biopsy grade, 0–3 synovitis). Plain kappa
corrects accuracy for chance agreement but treats all disagreements alike; **quadratic**
weighting penalises by the square of the distance (off by 1 → 1, by 2 → 4, by 3 → 9), so a
0-vs-3 blunder dominates a 1-vs-2 quibble. Two watch-outs: quadratic weights are **generous**,
so frequent off-by-one errors still score well (linear weights score the same predictions
lower) — a paper that says "weighted kappa" without naming the weighting is uncomparable; and
kappa's chance term depends on the **grade distribution**, so kappas from different populations
are not directly comparable.

**24. "The AI beat expert agreement" is usually two different measurements.** The sharpest
finding in this paper, and the paper does not notice it. Humans: quadratic-weighted kappa
**0.81**, raw pairwise, reader A vs reader B each working alone. Model: **0.842**, against the
**third-expert-adjudicated consensus** — a label with much of the individual noise removed,
i.e. an easier and more stable target. Like comparing two gymnastics judges to each other, then
comparing a fourth observer to the panel's agreed score. The fair comparison (model vs reader A,
alongside reader B vs reader A) was computable from data the authors already had and was not
reported. **Standing question: when a paper says the model matched or beat experts, ask what it
was compared against — one human, or a cleaned-up consensus of several?**

**25. The shared-labeller circularity, and the six-rung external-validation ladder.** The same
two sonographers labelled *every* image, the "external" set included, so the model was trained
to reproduce their habits and then scored against their habits. The rungs, weakest to strongest:
(1) random split; (2) temporal split; (3) different operators; (4) different devices;
(5) different institution; (6) **different labellers** — the only rung that breaks the
circularity. This paper's title claims rung 5 and delivers rung 2 plus parts of 3 and 4. Verify
it from the baseline table without reading the methods: **Overall 312 = Development 218 +
Internal 47 + External 47**, one consecutive cohort sliced three ways. **Tell: if the "external"
set is a neat fraction of the total cohort, it is a split, not an independent collection.** Note
also that a small internal→external drop (0.809 → 0.771) measured between two slices of one
hospital says little about the drop at a second hospital — the literature is full of models that
survived temporal splits and collapsed on geographic ones.

**26. Recompute one reported number from another.** Internal test: 152 correct + 30 off-by-one +
6 off-by-more = 188 ✓, and 152/188 = 0.8085 = the reported 0.809 ✓. Those same counts give
MAE = (30×1 + 6×2)/188 = **0.2234**, matching the reported 0.223 exactly — which independently
confirms the breakdown *and* pins every large error at exactly two grades. But within-one-grade
accuracy from those counts is 182/188 = **0.968**, while the paper reports **0.957** (= 180/188).
Those cannot both describe one confusion matrix; since the MAE matches, 0.957 looks like the slip.
Consequence-free here, but the habit is the point: a paper whose numbers reconcile has probably
been handled carefully.

**27. Credit the leakage precaution when you see it.** Splitting **at the patient level** so all
of one person's images stay in one subset is the most commonly botched step in imaging AI — two
views of the same knee landing on opposite sides lets the model recognise the knee rather than
the disease. These authors did it and said why. Worth noting as a positive marker, alongside
their reporting of inter-reader reliability (most papers omit it), their deliberate refusal to
harmonise across vendors, and their plain statement that no sample-size calculation was done.

**28. The missing analysis in every reader-assistance study: automation bias.** Juniors improved
(accuracy 0.702 → 0.782, weighted kappa 0.692 → 0.801) and read faster (21.4 → 15.8 s). Neither
answers the safety question: **how often did a reader who would have been right get pulled to the
model's wrong answer?** The data to answer it exist in the study (each reader's unassisted and
assisted grade per case, crossed with whether the model was right) and one table would settle it.
Also note faster reading with the answer displayed is partly anchoring, not comprehension. And
the reader study never reports **how many cases were read**, names no test, and gives only
"p < 0.05" — with 3 readers, whether the unit of analysis is readers or image-level readings
changes the p-value by orders of magnitude.

**Also unreconciled in this paper, worth remembering as a pattern:** the methods say "a senior
sonographer provided the reference standard" while the results score a "senior reference reader"
at accuracy 0.809 — a reader scored against their own labels would be 1.000, so these cannot be
the same person. And 27.6% of patients had grey-scale only with no Doppler, yet a Doppler-only
model is scored and a fused model is the headline, with no statement of how those ~30% were
handled.

## 2026-10-14 run — Tong et al., AI renal pathology meta-analysis

**Source:** the entry held over from the 10-13 screen. Renal Failure 2026;48(1):2729643,
PMID 42838512, PMC13644454. Full text retrieved; figures and all supplementary tables
unretrievable. Graded **3/5** — and the grade undersells the conduct: this is close to
4/5 reviewing of an evidence base that cannot support more than 3.

**Daily reachability re-check.** EAGLE (PMID 42773211) — **still no `pmc` field on day 23**.

**29. Accuracy for an uncommon disease starts high before anyone does any work. THE cleanest
published example this list has found.** The paper's own table: MsPGN pooled accuracy **0.96**
with pooled sensitivity **0.61**. Computed MsPGN prevalence from the validation sets the paper
itself lists for the largest included study (311/5330, 70/1226, 48/875, 48/933, 51/962,
52/1024 → 580/10,350 = **5.6%**). A model that says "not MsPGN" every single time scores
accuracy **0.944**. The reported 0.96 beats doing nothing by **1.6 percentage points** while
missing two in five real cases. Decompose with **accuracy = p·Se + (1−p)·Sp**: solving gives
implied specificity ≈ 0.98, so the number reflects what the models are good at (ruling out)
because 94% of slides need ruling out. **The structural tell needed no arithmetic: accuracy was
the highest of the four main metrics for ALL EIGHT diseases** (MN .96/.96, LN .79/.97,
DN .78/.96, MsPGN .61/.96, MPGN .60/.91 as Se/Acc). When one metric is the most flattering in
every single condition, it is measuring how the number is built, not how good the model is.
**Standing step: for any accuracy figure, get the prevalence and compute 1−p first.** This is
technique #4 (the trivial baseline) in its most quotable form yet.

**30. Confidence interval vs prediction interval — new to the list, and the single most useful
column in any meta-analysis.** CI = where the *average of past studies* sits. PI = what *the
next study* should expect. They diverge when heterogeneity is high, because the CI shrinks with
more studies while the PI must keep carrying the real between-study spread. Worked examples from
this paper: IgAN accuracy CI (0.92, 0.97) vs PI **(0.82, 1.00)** — 3.6× wider; IgAN precision CI
(0.84, 0.95) vs PI (0.62, 1.00); LN precision CI (0.78, 0.97) vs PI **(0.50, 1.00)**; MsPGN
sensitivity CI (0.45, 0.76) vs PI (0.19, 0.95); and MPGN precision 0.58 with CI **(0.00, 1.00)**
— two datasets, no information at all, and the most honest number in the paper. Context: median
I² across the 38 pooled estimates = **92.8%**, with 28/38 ≥ 75% and 24/38 ≥ 90%. **If a review
reports high I² and no prediction interval, you are being shown a precise-looking average of
things that disagree.** Note also what I² is not: not a measure of test quality, and not evidence
of bias — it means the estimate is *not transferable*. These authors say that in their methods.

**31. A pooled AUC of 1.00 is a red flag, and check whether each metric in a row shares a k.**
MCD AUC = **1.00 (95% CI 0.99–1.00) with I² = 0.0%** — perfect discrimination replicated
identically across six datasets, which real diagnostic tests do not do; it is the fingerprint of
small curated datasets. It also sits oddly beside MCD's own sensitivity of 0.92: AUC 1.00 means a
threshold existed catching every case with no false alarms, yet 8% were missed at the threshold
used. **The resolution is the k:** MCD sensitivity rests on 11 datasets, MCD AUC on **6**. Same
pattern elsewhere — MN sensitivity k=22 but AUC k=10; IgAN k=14 vs k=11; FSGS and MPGN have no
AUC row at all. Studies reporting AUC are a non-random subset (they need probability outputs and
a threshold sweep, which tracks with better-resourced work). **Reading across a row invites the
assumption that the metrics describe one body of evidence; verify the k on each.**

**32. Datasets are not studies, and "units" are not patients — a three-layer version of the
standing question.** (a) 15 studies produced ~26 validation datasets (16 'IV' + 10 'EV' tokens
in Table 1); MN alone appears in **22**. One study (Ma et al.) contributes six on its own —
IV + EV1..EV5 — all sharing one model, one training set, one architecture, one group. The authors
*did* guard against patient overlap and took only the main model where several were tested on one
cohort, which is better than most reviews; but non-overlapping patients ≠ independent evidence.
(b) 39,536 validation "units" decomposes to **16,223 patient-based + 9,505 image-based +
9,988 glomerulus-based + 3,820 lesion-based** (sums exactly), so **only 41% are patients**. (c)
The authors entered unit of analysis as a meta-regression covariate and **found it mattered** —
for IgAN, precision p=0.002, F1 p=0.001, AUC p=0.02 — then still reported one pooled estimate
across units. **Ask for a second column, "number of contributing studies," next to every k.**

**33. "Higher accuracy than senior pathologists" rested on ONE study.** Comparative data existed
in only 4 of 15 studies: one reporting junior pathologists, one senior, two unspecified. The
abstract says "AI also showed higher accuracy than senior pathologists for IgAN, MN, and MCD" —
three diseases, one study's senior readers, compared by **Z-test between pooled estimates from
different studies on different slides**, i.e. an indirect comparison, not an experiment. The body
text calls it exploratory, descriptive, hypothesis-generating and explicitly "not head-to-head"
— three hedges the abstract sentence will outrun. Pairs with technique #24 (the AI-beat-experts
comparison is usually two different measurements) and adds a new variant: **the comparison may
also be two different sets of patients.**

**34. When a paper's conclusion is more cautious than its abstract, believe the conclusion.**
Almost every criticism in this decode is one the authors made first: that the totals are analysis
units not patients; that **"a bivariate or HSROC model could not be applied"** and the pooled
metrics are "complementary reported performance measures rather than a complete paired
sensitivity-specificity diagnostic accuracy model"; that **specificity was not pooled and they
refused to impute it** because that "could introduce bias"; that 7/15 studies are at high risk of
bias on model evaluation; that GRADE certainty is moderate at best and "substantially lower" for
five of eight diseases; that all 15 studies are retrospective. Their conclusion — "current
head-to-head evidence is insufficient to establish superiority over pathologists" — is the
correct reading of their own data and the sentence to quote. **The gap between a conscientious
methods section and one quotable abstract sentence is where most misleading citations are born.**

**Also worth keeping as positive markers of a careful review:** prediction intervals for *every*
estimate; REML random effects with the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman adjustment (widens intervals
appropriately with few studies); a **prespecified 0.80 relevance threshold** with the summary
figure colour-coded by whether each CI excludes it; leave-one-out and Egger's restricted to k≥10
with the reason stated ("would be unstable and difficult to interpret"); duplicate screening with
agreement reported (kappa 0.87 / 0.89 / 0.91); PRISMA-DTA, PROBAST+AI and GRADE all applied and
their uncomfortable results printed.

**35. Registration numbers can be malformed — check the format, and say what you could not
verify.** The paper gives PROSPERO **CRD420261278316**, which has 12 digits. PROSPERO identifiers
follow CRD + '4' + YYYY + 6 digits = 11 digits (e.g. CRD42026123456); this reads as
4 + 2026 + 1278316, a seven-digit final group. One digit too many, and it defeats the entire
point of registering. **Recorded as a formatting discrepancy, NOT as a claim that the
registration does not exist** — the record could not be verified with the tools available, and no
link to it was published in the summary. Contrast 10-12 (Li et al.), where the registry *was*
reachable and changed the reading of the paper entirely: the lesson from #17 holds, but it
depends on the identifier being correct.

**Also noted in this paper:** the reference standard is not uniform — the inclusion criteria say
pathology confirmation, while the study table records roughly half the entries as "expert
consensus," which raises the same circularity question as #25 when the comparison is AI vs

**36. Nested cross-validation, and the leak it does NOT close.** Ordinary CV used for both tuning
and reporting is optimistic — you are quoting the maximum of many noisy numbers. Nested CV puts an
**inner** loop in charge of every choice (hyperparameters, components, calibration, threshold) and
touches the **outer** test fold once, so the estimate describes the *whole procedure*. This paper
did 10 repetitions × 5 outer folds = 50 splits with a 4-fold inner loop, and fitted smoothing,
scaling, SNV, PCA, component count, regularisation, class weighting, calibration **and** the
threshold inside the training folds. **But nested CV protects tuning within a family, not the
choice between families.** Five families were compared and the hybrid was carried forward "because
it had the highest pooled patient-grouped out-of-fold AUROC" — chosen on the same predictions then
reported. The authors say it in the abstract: the estimate "does not represent independent
validation of a prospectively fixed final model." **Tell to watch for: "we compared N models and
the best achieved X." X is the maximum of N noisy estimates; the bias is upward and unquantified.**

**37. THE LEAKAGE PENALTY, MEASURED INSIDE ONE PAPER. Keep this as the canonical number.** Same
model, same 274 nodes, two ways of splitting:
  - patient-grouped (correct): **AUROC 0.966**, TP 90 / **FN 6** / TN 170 / FP 8, Se 93.8 Sp 95.5 Acc 94.9
  - node-grouped only (the usual shortcut): **AUROC 0.989**, TP 93 / **FN 3** / TN 172 / FP 6, Se 96.9 Sp 96.6 Acc 96.7
**The shortcut buys +0.023 AUROC, +1.8 points of accuracy, and halves the missed cancers from 6 to
3.** Nothing about the model changed; only the bookkeeping. Every operating characteristic in both
rows reproduces exactly from the confusion matrices. This is the quantified version of technique
#27 (credit the patient-level split) and the sharpest answer yet to "does grouping really matter?"
Structure here was three deep: **45 patients → 280 nodes → 840 spectra**, median 6 nodes/patient.
Note also the aggregation gain for contrast: patient-grouped at *spectrum* level AUROC 0.944 →
node level 0.966, i.e. **+0.022 from legitimately averaging 3 readings — almost exactly the same
size as the +0.023 from cheating.** A real improvement and a bookkeeping error can move a number
identically; only one is earned.

**38. Clustering shows up in the ERRORS, not just the splits.** All **8 false positives came from
just 2 pathology-negative patients**, both with florid reactive change (follicular hyperplasia,
sinus histiocytosis). Treating nodes as independent would score that as 8 independent errors in
178 chances; really it is ~2 errors repeated across two people's nodes. Which is why this paper
also used **patient-clustered bootstrap** (2,000 resamples, whole patients with all their nodes)
and **within-patient permutation** (10,000 permutations preserving each patient's own positive/
negative mix). **Getting the splits right and then bootstrapping over observations undoes half the
work** — most papers that group their folds still compute intervals as if every node were a person.

**39. Calibration slope and calibration-in-the-large** (new to the list; Brier was covered 10-03).
Brier 0.063 against its prevalence reference p(1−p) = 0.350 × 0.650 = **0.228**, so ~72% reduction
in squared error — *a Brier score means nothing without that reference*. **Calibration-in-the-large
−0.03**: is the average predicted risk right? (0 = yes.) **Calibration slope 0.94**: is the spread
right? (1 = ideal; **<1 = predictions too extreme**, the signature of mild overfitting — and the
paper's own "modest overprediction at the highest probabilities" matches). Discrimination says the
ranking is good; calibration says the displayed numbers mean something. Calibration is also the
first property to break when a model moves hospital.

**40. Report the no-result rate.** 6 of 280 nodes (**2.1%**) produced no analysable reading and were
excluded from the denominator. STARD requires this and almost nobody reports it: a test that fails
to answer 2% of the time is a different proposition from one that always answers. Note the knock-on
— all 6 were pathology-negative, so removing them nudges prevalence from 34.3% to 35.0%.

**41. Keep this paper as the reference example of GOOD methodology**, the counterpart to 10-12
(registry-checking) and 10-14 (believe the conclusion over the abstract). Markers worth recognising:
QC rules prespecified and applied **blind to the label**; pathologists blinded to the spectra; nodes
selected for the index test **before** final pathology was known; 8 optical features prespecified;

**52. A curated test cohort inflates specificity, and prevalence is how you spot it.** EAGLE's

**24.5% prevalence**, case-control-like, not screening. Real screening populations in the same

**53. The calibration-cycle template — what an honest engineering response to failure looks like.**
EAGLE's middle section is the model to copy: (1) deploy on an unselected consecutive series, (2)
report the degradation rather than bury it, (3) identify the hard cases and re-annotate them,
(4) merge them into training, (5) test the improved model on a **different** consecutive series
from the same centres, (6) report the improvement with the arithmetic shown. Every number
reproduces: RW2 n=14,644 with 163 cases; EAGLE 146 TP / 432 FP → PPV 25.3%; EAGLE-Plus 144 TP /
118 FP → PPV 55.0%; false-positive reduction (432 − 118)/432 = **72.7%**. **The tell of a
reanalysis masquerading as a fix is step 5** — if the improved model is tested on the same series
that generated the hard cases, there is no evidence, only a fitted curve.

**54. Bootstrap confidence intervals break down at tiny event counts.** EAGLE's real-world low-dose
CT result is **8 positive predictions, PPV 12.5%** — one true positive — with a reported 95%
interval of **7.1–25.0%**. No valid method produces that. An exact (Clopper–Pearson) interval for
1 of 8 runs roughly **0.3% to 52.7%**, and a bootstrap resampling eight observations can only ever
produce PPVs at multiples of 1/8, so its percentiles are an artefact of the grid, not an estimate
of uncertainty. **Check: whenever an interval looks implausibly tight, back out the denominator.
Below roughly 20–30 events, demand an exact binomial interval.** A suspiciously narrow interval on
a small count is evidence the pipeline applied a default method outside its range — the point
estimate may still be fine, the interval is not.

**55. "Prospectively enrolled" does not mean the events were real.** EAGLE's most quotable claim —
AI triage would roughly triple endoscopic detection — rests on a cohort of 530 prospectively
enrolled people containing **six HGIN and zero invasive cancers**. To run the analysis the authors
**resampled three cancers from a different, retrospective cohort**. They say so plainly in the
Methods; the abstract says only "exploratory analyses of a prospectively enrolled cohort."
**Check: find the event count, not the enrolment count.** A prospective design with too few events
to analyse is a pilot, however it is described, and any number derived from injected events is a

selection optimism. Pairs with #36 (nested CV and the leak it does not close): the leak moves up a

## 2026-10-19 run — Toh et al., CheXNeXt open-source AI on after-hours ED chest radiographs

Decoded from **full text** (PMC13663711, retrieved 2026-10-18 and read today). Graded **2/5**.
Checked the week's new literature first for anything landmark: the only candidate was the PM Heart
trial of an EHR-integrated AI prognostic tool for ischaemic heart disease (PMID 42893738, Am Heart
J), and it is a **protocol and rationale paper with no results**, so it does not displace the queue
entry. Noted for later — if PM Heart's results publish, they are worth decoding, since randomised
trials of AI tools embedded in live clinical workflow are rare.

**Why this paper earned its slot.** It is the exact counterpart to yesterday's EAGLE, which fixed
its thresholds in cross-validation before touching any test data. Toh et al. did the opposite in
both directions at once, and that is what makes it instructive: Table 1 uses thresholds chosen by
somebody else on somebody else's data (methodologically clean, and the sensitivity is terrible),
while Tables 2 and 3 use thresholds swept across the same 1,006 films they are then scored on
(the attractive numbers, never validated). **The clean table is the one with the bad result.**

**Everything reconciles.** All three tables are internally consistent — F1 matches 2·PPV·Se/(PPV+Se)
and Youden matches Se+Sp−1 everywhere, to the last decimal. Working backwards from Se, Sp, PPV and
NPV on n=1,006 recovers counts the paper never prints: **consolidation ~191 positives (19.0%), 45
TP / 8 FP / 146 FN; pneumonia ~183 (18.2%), 32 TP / 4 FP / 151 FN; pleural effusion ~136 (13.5%),
85 TP / 44 FP / 51 FN.**

**58. A high AUC with a useless sensitivity is a threshold problem, not a model problem — and the
two can be told apart.** AUC 0.900 for consolidation alongside sensitivity 0.234, same films, same
model. AUC measures ranking and is threshold-free; sensitivity and specificity are properties of
one point on the curve. The diagnostic: **if AUC is high and sensitivity is low, the model is fine
and the dial is wrong; if AUC is low, no dial setting will save it.** The proof in this paper is
that AUC is byte-identical across all three tables (0.900 / 0.856 / 0.916) while sensitivity moves
from 0.234 to 0.894 — moving a cut-off cannot change a ranking. **Standing check: when a paper
leads with AUC, find the sensitivity and specificity at the threshold the authors propose to use;
if no threshold is named, you have not been told how the tool behaves.**

**59. Thresholds do not travel — but not uniformly, which is why you must check each one.** Stanford's
published F1-maximised cut-offs against Sengkang's locally optimal ones: consolidation **0.744 →
0.22 (F1) / 0.11 (Youden)**, roughly 3× to 7× lower; pneumonia **0.831 → 0.31**, nearly 3× lower;
pleural effusion **0.731 → 0.73**, essentially identical, with "optimisation" buying an F1 gain of
**0.003**. One of three transferred perfectly. The mechanism is **discrimination surviving while
calibration drifts**: ranking is robust to a change of scanner, population and processing pipeline,
but the numeric scale is not, so scores get systematically squashed and a fixed cut-off lands in
the wrong place. **"We used the published threshold" sounds like the non-cherry-picking choice, and
in one sense it is — but it is an untested assumption that the new population's score distribution
matches the developer's.** Pairs with #44 (a threshold quoted to two decimals) and against yesterday's
EAGLE, which prespecified and locked its thresholds in cross-validation.

**60. Quantify the in-sample threshold-selection optimism instead of dismissing the result.** The
reflex "they tuned on the test set, so the number is worthless" is as wrong as ignoring the problem.
Simulated it: a model with AUC 0.900, a sample shaped like this one (191 positives / 815 negatives),
pick the Youden-maximising threshold on one draw and apply it to a fresh draw, 2,000 repetitions →
**mean Youden 0.651 in-sample vs 0.630 out-of-sample, optimism ≈ 0.021.** Sanity check: the paper's
own optimised Youden for consolidation is 0.659, close to the simulated in-sample mean, so the model
of the situation is about right. **Two points of Youden — real, modest, and driven almost entirely by
sample size.** At 30 positives instead of 191 it would be several times larger. **The bigger problem
is not the bias but that every CI in the optimised tables is conditional on a threshold estimated
from the same data, so they answer "how uncertain is performance at 0.11?" rather than "how uncertain
is performance at a threshold chosen the way we chose ours?"** Keep the simulation script;
`verify_chexnext2.py` generalises to any (AUC, n_pos, n_neg).

**61. Do not carry yesterday's suspicion into today's paper — check it.** Technique #54 (bootstrap
CIs break down at tiny event counts) was earned on EAGLE's 1-of-8 PPV. It would have been easy to
apply it here by reflex. It does not hold: this paper's bootstrap intervals match exact
Clopper–Pearson intervals closely (consolidation PPV reported 0.744–0.941 vs exact 0.724–0.933;
pneumonia sensitivity reported 0.124–0.233 vs exact 0.123–0.238). With dozens to hundreds of events
the bootstrap works as advertised. **The machinery was fine; what it was pointed at was the problem.**
Recorded because a technique applied without its precondition is just a prejudice.

**62. When an IRB waiver strips the demographics, generalisability becomes untestable, not merely
uncertain.** This paper reports **no patient characteristics whatsoever** — no age, no sex, no
indication, no comorbidity — because the waiver's terms forbade extracting them. You cannot assess
transportability to another population when the study population is undescribed, and you cannot
check representativeness even within the same hospital. **Check: look for the baseline-characteristics
paragraph before reading any accuracy figure; if it is absent, the numbers describe an unidentified
group of people.** Compounded here by one month of collection (October 2024) in a tropical climate —
one season of infection prevalence.

**63. Count whether the "multiple conditions" are actually multiple.** Consolidation labelled on
19.0% of films and pneumonia on 18.2% of the same 1,006 films. Either more than a third of
after-hours ED chest X-rays at this hospital showed one or the other, or — far more likely — these
are largely the same radiographs labelled twice because the report read "consolidation, likely
pneumonia". **The overlap is never reported.** It decides whether the paper validated the model on
three conditions or on roughly two, and whether the three sets of confidence intervals are
independent of one another. **One line of text would settle it; its absence is a reporting gap, not
a trivial one.** Related to #51 (count the operating points before combining numbers) — here, count
the outcomes before counting them as independent.

**Also noted.** The reference standard is report text, not disease: no microbiology, no pathology,
no follow-up — so the study measures agreement with local reporting style, which the authors state
plainly and justify as realistic. A **single** senior trainee extracted every label from 1,006 free-text
reports with no second reader and no kappa. No external validation. No funding, competing-interests
or data-availability statement in the PMC rendering, and the analysis ran on a commercial platform

abstract's one real sin is reporting that sensitivity rose "while maintaining acceptable specificity"

more than half of all flags wrong, which is the operationally decisive number for a triage tool.

**64. A significant p-value and a confidence interval containing zero, in the same sentence — and
why both are correct.** Table 12, Kenya: **ΔF1 = 0.0054, 95% CI −0.0156 to +0.0260, McNemar
χ²=7.224, p=.007, "Significant."** The authors print the interval, note in the text that it "spans
zero", and then conclude the result "confirms the advanced ensemble's structural advantage". Neither
statistic is wrong; **they answer different questions.** The bootstrap CI asks *how big is the
difference in F1*. **McNemar asks only whether the pattern of disagreements is lopsided** — it
discards every case both models got right and every case both got wrong, and tests the discordant
pairs against 50/50. A two-to-one split of discordant pairs (Cohen's g 0.1716 → ~67%) is very
unlikely by chance even when the *net* change in the summary metric is negligible. **Check: when a
paper reports both and they point different ways, the CI answers your question; when it reports only
one, notice which.** The giveaway phrase is "statistically significant improvement" attached to a
difference whose magnitude is never given in countable units.

**65. Significance and effect size can rank two results in opposite directions — here they do.**
Kenya ΔF1 **0.0054**, **2** extra true positives of 337, p=**.007**, "Significant". Uganda ΔF1
**0.0059**, **8** extra true positives of 301, p=**.86**, "Not significant". **Uganda's improvement
is larger on both measures that matter and it is the one called not significant**, because its
discordant pairs split near-evenly (Cohen's g 0.0333 ≈ 53/47) while Kenya's were consistent. One
country had a consistent tiny difference; the other an inconsistent slightly larger one. **The
canonical example for this list.** Both traps are live: reading "significant" as "worth doing"
(the paper's error) and reading "not significant" as "no effect" (the reader's). **The question that
cuts through: significant improvement of what, in what units, by how much?** Here the answer is
two children.

**66. A paper can hand you the evidence against itself, and that is worth grading up for.** These
authors printed the CI that undermines their headline, stated the Uganda null in the abstract AND
twice in the text, wrote that "algorithmic complexity does not universally guarantee statistical
superiority", said explicitly that SMOTE came after the train/test split and why, reported fold-level

than direct epidemiological drivers". **None of that is required and most papers omit it.** The grade
is 2/5 for what the design can carry, not for candour — and the distinction is worth keeping

**70. When the outcome is a change score, check whether the baseline is a top predictor — then
separate the floor effect from regression to the mean.** Rapid decliners' baseline FEV1 **1,645 mL**
vs non-rapid **1,340 mL** (305 mL higher in the group *defined* by falling further), and baseline
FEV1 carries SHAP 0.389, **2.1× the next predictor**. The authors name the **floor effect** (low
baseline → no room to fall) and cite SPIROMICS agreeing — that part is real. **They do not address
regression to the mean, which is the other half and is pure artefact:** a single noisy baseline puts
lucky-day patients in the high group, and their second reading returns to normal, recording a fall
that never happened. **Worked check: 100 people with identical true FEV1 of 1,500 mL and ±100 mL
measurement error, split by first reading — the top group averages ~1,580 then ~1,500, an apparent
80 mL decline in a group where nobody declined.** That is exactly this paper's threshold. **Fix:
ANCOVA (model follow-up adjusted for baseline), or report the model with baseline removed.** Related
to the ANCOVA/Lord's-paradox note from 10-12 but this is the prediction-model form of it. **Standing
check: "those who started worst improved most" and "those who started best declined most" are the two
most over-interpreted findings in medicine, and both are what noise alone produces.**

**71. Compare the measurement error against the threshold — and remember that errors ADD when you
subtract.** Inclusion tolerated FEV1 reproducibility up to **0.2 L (200 mL)** within a session; rapid
decline was **≥40 mL/year over 2 years = 80 mL total**. The tolerated within-session wobble is
**2.5× the change being detected**. Worse, **the variance of a difference is the sum of the
variances**, so a two-point change carries ~**√2 = 1.41×** the error of one measurement: at ~100 mL
per reading, ~141 mL on the difference, **larger than the 80 mL cut-off itself**. **This is very
likely why the AUC stalls at 0.712 regardless of algorithm, and why all six models land within
0.047 of each other — a large part of the label is noise, and noise is unpredictable by
construction.** **Standing check: when every model performs about the same, suspect the labels before
blaming the models.** The measurement-error figure usually hides in the exclusion criteria, as it did
here.

**72. Find the follow-up funnel and ask who came back.** 2,193,579 screened → 45,965 diagnosed →
38,863 cross-sectional → **1,215 with both baseline and 24-month spirometry**. The prediction model
rests on **3.1% of the cross-sectional cohort and 0.055% of those screened**, and **the paper never
compares the 1,215 who returned with the 37,648 who did not** — on variables it already has. Who
returns for a voluntary repeat lung-function test two years later is bound up with symptoms,
distance, income and whether anyone chased them. **Check: in any longitudinal sub-study, divide the
analysed n by the enrolled n, then look for the table comparing the two groups. If it is absent, the
headline cohort size is advertising.** Pairs with #62 (undescribed study population).

0.365 → Se 95.5%, Sp 23.3%, NPV 86.4%. Worked out on the stated test set (365 patients, 164 events):

the paper's 86.4% exactly). **Flagging every patient catches 164/164 at PPV 44.9%.** So the model's

smaller than 0.027, but 0.011 is still no basis for choosing an architecture — especially when the

**75. Clustering can relabel what you already knew.** Three latent classes, separated almost entirely

analysis found sex, smoking and severity.** Also: AIC and BIC "consistently decrease" as classes are

any age band. Extends #42 (predictive values depend on prevalence) to the summary-curve metrics.

**90. "Zero-shot" covers the ranking, not the threshold — and this paper draws the line itself.**
AUROC and AUPRC are computed by sweeping every cut-off, so they need no labels and the zero-shot claim
genuinely holds for them. Sensitivity, specificity, precision and F1 all need a decision boundary, and
here it was chosen on the **validation set** by: *among thresholds reaching sensitivity ≥ 0.80, take the
highest F1*. The authors state it unprompted — the procedure "requires labeled validation examples for
each cardiac condition and therefore constitutes condition-specific calibration, distinct from the
zero-shot ranking evaluation reflected by AUROC and AUPRC." **Practical consequence: the architecture's
selling point is that a new condition costs a sentence instead of a dataset — true for ranking, false
for a yes/no answer at a sensible operating point, which is most of the cost you were avoiding.**
**Standing check: if a paper claims zero-shot and reports sensitivity or F1, ask how the threshold was
set.** This is the **sixth** paper on the threshold axis in eight days and the most subtle: the issue is
not tuning on test data but that the *framing* implies no labels while the *metrics* required them.

**91. A sensitivity-first threshold is a declaration of intended use — read it as one.** Rule:
sensitivity ≥ 0.80 first, F1 second. Result internally: **sensitivity never below 0.791** across all 14
conditions, but **precision 0.105 (AVB), 0.108 (SVT), 0.118 (PQTI)** — about **9–10 flagged per real
case**; externally the floor is **0.016**, about **62 flagged per real case**. Whether that is bad
depends entirely on what a flag triggers: nine extra cardiologist glances to avoid a missed block is a
good trade; nine extra admissions is not. The authors land correctly — the model "is better suited to
prioritizing recordings for clinician review than to autonomous diagnosis." **Check: a sensitivity
figure quoted without its precision, for a rare condition, is close to meaningless — you reach 100%
sensitivity by flagging everything. Ask how many it flags and what happens to them.** Pairs with #73
(the trivial baseline) and #29.

**92. Patient-level splitting is the silent error to check first in any multi-record dataset.** 800,034
recordings from 161,352 patients is **~5 ECGs per person**. A random record-level split would put the
same patient's tracings on both sides of the wall and inflate every number — and nothing in the
reported results would look wrong. **This paper partitioned at the patient level and said so.**
**Standing check: whenever one subject can contribute several records — serial ECGs, repeat imaging,
multiple lesions, both eyes — find the sentence saying the split was by subject. If it is absent,
assume it was not.**

**93. When a metric makes a model look bad, check whether the metric is measuring the right thing.**
Retrieval recall@1 fell from **0.413 (rare reports) to 0.073 (frequent reports)** — apparently terrible
on common findings. It was the scoring. Under instance-level matching a retrieval counts as correct
only if it returns *the very report paired with that ECG*; routine ECG reports are highly templated, so
the model kept returning **identical text belonging to another patient** and being marked wrong.
Rescored by text content, frequent-report recall@1 rose **0.073 → 0.282, nearly 4×**. The authors
diagnose the cause properly: under CLIP's symmetric InfoNCE objective **every unpaired combination in a
mini-batch is a negative regardless of semantic identity**, so semantically equivalent pairs are
actively pushed apart — the "false-negative problem" in medical vision-language pretraining. **Check:
before accepting that a model failed on a subgroup, confirm the metric can express success there.**
Companion to #83 (the winner changes with the metric) — here the metric does not change the winner, it
manufactures a loser.

**94. Check whether the thing tested is the thing advertised.** The introduction's case for image-based
ECG AI is that real hospitals hold ECGs as **printed reports, scanned documents and phone
photographs** with no waveform available. **Every image in the study was rendered by the authors from
clean digital waveforms at 224 × 224 pixels.** None was a scan or a photo. So the method was never
tested on the problem it exists to solve. **To their considerable credit the authors say this in the
introduction, the discussion AND the limitations, and frame the paper as a foundation for future
extension** — but the gap between motivating problem and tested problem is the single most important
fact about the paper, and it is exactly the kind of gap that vanishes by the time a result reaches a
headline. **Standing check: write down in one line what the paper says the problem is, and in one line
what it actually measured, and compare them.**

**Also noted.** Internal labels are **rule-based text matching over machine-generated reports**, so
internally the model is scored against an ECG machine's own reading rather than a clinician's; external
labels are **physician-adjudicated SNOMED-CT**, and the authors flag this difference as a second reason
internal and external figures should not be compared naively. Performance by category reproduces across
both datasets — **rhythm 0.948/0.921, conduction 0.858/0.843, morphology 0.741/0.750** — and morphology

declines with age (**0.891 / 0.870 / 0.831** for 12–44 / 45–74 / 75+, spread 0.060), **near-identical by
sex**, slightly lower in ICU. Comparisons against ECG-CLIP and two supervised models use **figures
carried over from prior publications rather than a fresh head-to-head run** — weaker than they look.
Single external cohort. **No funding, competing-interests or ethics statement in the PMC rendering**,

**95. When the outcome is a score, read its items against the predictor list — then ask what a
circularity sensitivity analysis can and cannot settle.** FRAIL scale items: fatigue · weight loss ·
cannot climb one flight · cannot walk 500 m · **more than five illnesses** (≥3 of 5 = frail). Final
predictors: age · grip strength · **number of comorbidities** · **CCI score** · albumin · pro-BNP. So
one fifth of the outcome is also an input, twice over. **To their real credit the authors anticipated

published it: external AUC 0.917 → 0.904 (drop comorbidity count) → 0.902 (drop CCI) → 0.891 (drop

points. (a) All four sensitivity models RETAIN grip strength**, which measures the same functional
capacity as the stairs and 500-m items, recorded at the same visit — one leak closed, another left
open. **(b) Dropping a predictor tests whether that COLUMN mattered, not whether the INFORMATION
mattered**: albumin, pro-BNP and grip strength all track the same illness burden, so a small drop is
equally consistent with "comorbidity was not doing the work" and "the other columns quietly took
over." Settling it needs a model built only from variables that cannot restate the outcome — here age,
albumin and pro-BNP. **Standing check: find the outcome scale's components (usually in a reference,
not the paper) and compare them to the predictor list; if a sensitivity analysis exists, ask whether
it removed ALL the overlapping variables.**

**96. Identification is not prediction — and the cost question almost no paper asks itself.**
Predictors and outcome were measured at the **same visit**; the paper says so ("assessed
contemporaneously") and, unusually, states outright that it "does not establish a model for predicting
future frailty incidence." Credit for the discipline. **But an identification model only earns its
place if measuring the outcome directly is hard, and here it is trivial.** The comparison the paper
never makes: **FRAIL scale = five yes/no questions, no equipment, no blood, under a minute,
patient-answerable. The model = age + comorbidity count + CCI (chart review) + grip strength
(dynamometer) + albumin + pro-BNP (two blood tests).** The model is substantially more expensive than
the thing it identifies, and right 85% of the time rather than by definition — yet the paper proposes
an online calculator for patients and families to self-assess. **Standing check: for any model whose
outcome is a questionnaire or bedside test, write down what it costs to run the model and what it
costs to measure the outcome, and compare.** The legitimate use case sitting unclaimed here is
patients who *cannot* self-report (dementia, no speech) — grip strength and albumin need no answers.

**97. Read the table, not the sentence about the table.** The paper justifies XGBoost over KNN on four

**98. Credit DeLong when you see it — then notice what the paper did with the answer.** This paper ran

came back **p = 0.768**, i.e. indistinguishable, and the authors say so. **That is the right test,
honestly reported.** The gap is what followed: having established statistically that the AUCs cannot

— which points at **logistic regression, external AUC 0.909, within 0.008 of the chosen model** — not
"ours wins on these four numbers against this one competitor." **A correct test whose answer is then
worked around is worth noting as its own pattern.**

**99. The train-to-external drop ordering reproduces for the sixth time.** MLP **−0.084** (0.989 →

**101. Discrimination can transport while calibration collapses — and a prevalence shift predicts the
direction.** The cleanest example this list has found. **AUC 0.7508 → 0.7549 (moved 0.004). Brier
0.1558 → 0.2553 (+0.0995). Calibration intercept −0.29 (95% CI −1.07 to 0.49, CONTAINS zero) → +0.61

learned; move it somewhere the event is twice as common and every probability is too low, while the
ordering is untouched because everyone shifts together. **Read the SIGN of a calibration intercept:
positive = risk UNDERSTATED, negative = overstated.** Worked illustration: four patients told 10/20/30/40%

detecting this.** Completes the arc of #84 (how to read intercept and slope) and #89 (AUROC vs AUPRC):
#89 was prevalence moving a discrimination-adjacent metric; this is prevalence moving calibration while
discrimination sits still.

cohort — and the paper says so in those words. Every data-driven selection decision is fitted to one
sample and evaporates on new data; skipping the step means there is nothing to evaporate. **This is
the structural reason the development-to-validation gap appeared once here and then stopped.** Contrast
with the four preceding papers, all of which ran LASSO and/or Boruta inside the development cohort.
**Standing check: find the sentence saying how the predictor list was arrived at. "Prespecified from

## 2026-10-27 — Lin S et al., paediatric urine culture positivity (Ann Med; PMID 42841462) — graded 2/5

581 children at Fujian Children's Hospital, 2023–2025, 42.17% culture-positive, seven same-day labs,
random forest AUC 0.814 (0.742–0.875) on a 175-patient internal holdout, **no external validation**, and
a public web calculator shipped on it. Reported honestly in its limitations; the three problems it does
not see are all detectable from numbers it prints.

**105. Compare confidence-interval WIDTHS before you let a paper compare the numbers beside them.**
The best technique this list has acquired in weeks, because it is ten seconds and works on anything with
an interval. Here the paper justifies machine learning by setting the random forest (AUC **0.814, CI
0.742–0.875**, half-width **0.0665**) against the single best lab value (BACT alone, AUC **0.730, CI
0.692–0.769**, half-width **0.0385**). The forest's interval is **1.7× wider**. Intervals widen when
samples shrink, so these were not computed on the same children. Confirm with **Hanley–McNeil (1982)**,
which gives an AUC's standard error from the AUC and the class counts: AUC 0.730 at n=245/336 (**the full
cohort**) predicts half-width **0.042**; AUC 0.814 at n=74/101 (**the holdout**) predicts **0.067**. Both
land on the reported values. So an **in-sample full-cohort baseline is being subtracted from an
out-of-sample holdout model**, and the 0.084 gap has never been measured. The fix costs nothing: score
both on the same 175 and run a paired DeLong. **Standing check: unequal CI widths = unequal samples =
find out why before accepting the comparison.** Generalises past AUC to proportions, hazard ratios,
anything bracketed. Sharpens #88 (overlapping CIs settle nothing without a paired test) by giving you a
way to detect the mismatch rather than just the uncertainty.

**106. The SHAP base value is the model's effective prevalence — read it against the real one.**
The waterfall legend prints **E[f(X)] = 0.499** and calls it "the population baseline." The training
prevalence is **171/406 = 0.421**. The 0.078 gap is `class_weight = "balanced"`, applied to every tree
model here: weighting the rarer class up until both contribute equally to the loss is the same as
training on a 50/50 world, so **the model's mean output moves to 0.5 by construction.** Ranking is
untouched (everyone shifts together, AUC unaffected); every displayed percentage is pulled toward 50%.
This is the **second paper in a week** where balanced weights broke calibration, and it is now the most
common way a clinical tool ships an uninterpretable number. **What is new here is the detection method:
you can read a model's effective prevalence straight off its SHAP base value, from a figure legend,
without any access to the model.** Extends #99 (balanced weights break calibration by construction) with
the receipt.

**107. A Brier score is not a calibration measure. Compute the no-skill Brier first.**
The figure legend reads "a Brier score of 0.188 indicates a modest calibration improvement." It does not.
Brier decomposes as **reliability − resolution + uncertainty**: calibration is one of three parts, and a
model can score well by discriminating sharply while its probabilities are systematically wrong.
Always compute the floor before reading the number: a model predicting the prevalence for everyone —
zero discrimination, perfect calibration — scores **p(1−p) = 0.4229 × 0.5771 = 0.2440**. Against that,
0.188 is a **Brier skill score of 0.230**: 23% of the available room, composition unknown. **Standing
check: Brier alone is uninterpretable; demand the calibration intercept and slope (#84), which separate
the components and give you the direction of the error.** The paper has the calibration curve and reports
a Brier score instead of reading it.

**108. When the controls are sicker than the cases, a coefficient's sign belongs to the sampling frame.**
Fever: **52.2% of culture-positives vs 73.5% of culture-negatives** (p<0.001). CRP: median **18.53 vs
24.56 mg/L** (p=0.02). The children growing bacteria were *less* systemically inflamed. The explanation is
correct and the paper gives it — the negative group is stuffed with feverish children whose infection is
elsewhere and who got a urine culture as part of a fever workup, plus genuine infections that failed to
grow. The consequence it does not follow through: **CRP enters the model with a negative weight.** The
model has learned "more inflammation → less likely a positive urine culture," which is true of *this
hospital's referral pattern* and not of urinary infection. Move to a clinic that only cultures when a
urinary source is suspected and the contrast group changes, so the coefficient weakens or flips with no
biology changing at all. **Standing check: when a predictor's direction surprises you, do not reach for a
mechanism — ask what the comparison group is made of.** Companion to #70 (baseline coupling) and the
spectrum-bias entries; this is the version where the distortion lives in the *controls*.

**109. The stated purpose names the metric that must be printed — check whether it is there.**
The introduction's own framing: 70–80% of submitted specimens culture negative, so the job is to **avoid
cultures that will come back empty.** That job is governed by **specificity** and **NPV**. Sensitivity is
given three times, to three decimals, with a bootstrap CI (**0.770, 0.662–0.865**). **Specificity is
listed in the methods, drawn as an unlabelled bar in Figure 4D, and printed as a number nowhere. NPV is
never computed.** So the paper cannot answer its own question, and neither can the reader. **Papers
report the metric the model won on far more often than the metric the use case needs; when those diverge,
the divergence is the finding.** Standing demand for any screening tool: **sensitivity and specificity at
a single stated threshold, together, plus the threshold.** Here no threshold appears anywhere, while a
public calculator returns a probability — so a clinician reading 47% cannot tell which side of the
published operating point they are on. Companion to #92 ("zero-shot" covers ranking but not thresholds).

**110. An outcome definition can make a model look WORSE than it is — the inverse of circularity.**
Every prior entry on outcome definitions in this list (#78, #98) is about a definition that flatters the
model. This is the mirror. The outcome is culture positivity, and the paper's own third limitation notes
that **up to 31% of febrile infant pyelonephritis is culture-negative** (pre-hospital antibiotics,
collection error, non-growing organisms). Those children are genuinely infected, sit in the negative
group, and the model — correctly reading their inflammation — scores them high. **They are counted as the
model's false positives, so the reported specificity is biased DOWNWARD by children it got clinically
right.** Credit: the authors found this themselves and said it. **Standing check: ask which direction an
outcome-definition problem pushes. It is not always upward, and a definition that understates a model is
still a definition problem.**

**111. A small train-to-validation gap is a fact about regularisation, not about usefulness.**
XGBoost 1.000 → 0.707; gradient boosting 0.999 → 0.732; LightGBM 0.996 → 0.713; random forest **0.845 →
0.814**. The paper reads the small forest gap as "superior generalization." The forest's gap is small
because it was **capped at depth 4** with min_samples_split 10 and max_features 0.3 — a model that cannot
memorise has nothing to lose. The gap measures how much a fitting procedure overfitted; **only the
validation number measures what the user gets.** A forest at 1.000 → 0.814 would be exactly as useful.
**Standing check: when a paper cites the gap as evidence of quality, look at the hyperparameters that
produced the training number before crediting the algorithm.**

**112. A retrospective cohort spanning calendar years contains a free temporal validation.**
Three full years of data (2023–2025), with the year already broken out in the resistance analysis — so
the variable is in the dataset. Training on 2023–2024 and testing on 2025 costs nothing, needs no new
patients, and tests the question a deployed tool actually faces. **They used a random split instead.** A
random split asks "does this work on more children exactly like these?"; a temporal split asks "does it
work next year?" **Standing check: when a study spans time and splits at random anyway, that is a
decision — ask why.** This is the single cheapest thing that would have moved this paper from 2 to 3.

**113. Check two percentages per table against their stated denominator.**
Two defects here, neither conclusion-changing, both showing the table was never re-derived. **(a)** The
clinical-diagnosis block gives fever as **128 (25.00%)** and **247 (48.24%)**; against the group sizes
those are 52.24% and 73.51%. Both reported figures correspond to a denominator of **512** — not 245, not
336, not 581, not their sum (686). The abnormal-urinalysis row uses the same phantom denominator.
**(b)** Table 1 reports **134** children with underlying disease and uses 134 for the component rows, but
the Results text gives VUR as **81.57%** of underlying disease, which needs a denominator of **114** (the
three named conditions summed). Against the table's 134, VUR is 69.4%. Also: the fever chi-square is
reported as **28.92**; from the group counts I compute **28.00** (p<0.001 either way).

**Also noted — what the paper gets genuinely right, and it is a decent list.** A **real leakage firewall
stated in the authors' own words** — all preprocessing, all three feature-selection algorithms and all
hyperparameter tuning fitted on the 406 training patients only, holdout "kept entirely unseen until final
model evaluation." **The hyperparameter search is reproducible**: RandomizedSearchCV, 40 iterations,
5-fold stratified, search space and winning configuration both printed. **EPV = 171/7 = 24.4**, comfortably
above the floor (contrast #85). **Stratification verified**: 42.12% train vs 42.29% holdout vs 42.17%
overall, and every count in the cohort, age, sex, LEU, NIT and microbiology tables reconciles exactly.
**The bootstrap holds up** — I reconstructed sensitivity as 57/74 and the exact Clopper–Pearson interval
is **0.658–0.860** against the paper's bootstrap **0.662–0.865**, so 500 replicates was adequate here
(worth recording, since I have criticised small-replicate bootstraps on #63). **Specimen collection is
described properly** (bags after disinfection, catheter or suprapubic aspiration, clean midstream,
inoculated within 1–2 h) — contamination is the dominant error source in paediatric urine and a model
trained on contaminated specimens learns contamination. **DCA performed.** **Limitations section names
single-centre design, the 74-positive holdout, boosting overfitting, its own CI width, the age skew, age
being dropped, the culture-negative-infection problem and SHAP's non-causality**, and uses the words
"strictly as preliminary."

**Against that: no external validation of any kind, an internal contradiction** (the interpretability
section says the RF "was chosen to interpret the model's output" and then that "the variables importance
based on **LGBM** model is shown in" the figure — one is wrong and the reader cannot tell which),
**no reporting guideline named** (TRIPOD+AI fits and most items are already met), **no funding statement,
no competing-interests statement**, and **no pairwise test** between any two models. The web calculator's
URL was stripped by the PMC text extraction and **was not reproduced in the summary rather than guessed
at**; Figure 4D's accuracy/precision/specificity/F1 bars are described only qualitatively in the
alt-text, so the confusion matrix could not be reconstructed and that is stated in the summary.

**Clinically useful regardless of the model.** This hospital, 2023–2025: **204/245 isolates Gram-negative
(83.27%)**, *E. coli* 72.55% and *K. pneumoniae* 10.78% of those; 41 Gram-positive, *E. faecalis* 48.78%
and *E. faecium* 39.02%. **First- to third-generation cephalosporins resisted at 21.74–65.38%**, while
amikacin, piperacillin/tazobactam, cefoperazone/sulbactam and meropenem all stayed **below 10%**.
*E. faecium* **100% resistant to penicillin G and ampicillin**; both enterococci **100% susceptible to
vancomycin and linezolid**. And as a standalone test in this cohort, **nitrite ran sensitivity 0.302 /
specificity 0.899** — it missed seven of every ten real infections.

## 2026-10-28 — Chen X et al., AKI after liver transplantation (Ren Fail; PMID 42802088) — graded 2/5

**n = 127** — the smallest cohort this list has decoded — at one Chongqing centre, 48% AKI, 62 candidate
variables screened to 4 by LASSO, five models compared, **no external validation**. Weak evidence,
**exceptionally reported**: names TRIPOD and TRIPOD+AI, publishes its own EPV, runs all 30 pairwise
DeLong tests with Benjamini–Hochberg and prints every one, adds bootstrap optimism correction and 5-fold
CV, discloses the predictor–outcome overlap unprompted, and leads its abstract with its weakest number.

**114. The AUC grid — with n₁ positives and n₂ negatives, AUC moves only in steps of 1/(n₁×n₂).**
The best small-sample tool this list has acquired. An AUC is a *counting* statistic: the fraction of
positive–negative pairs ranked the right way round. Validation cohort here: **16 × 17 = 272 pairs**, so
the smallest expressible difference is **1/272 = 0.00368**. Every reported validation AUC lands on that
grid — SVM 0.846 = **230**/272, XGBoost 0.842 = **229**/272, RF 0.816 = 222/272, LightGBM 0.811 =
220.5/272 (the half is a tie), logistic 0.761 = 207/272. So the paper's "numerically best" model, the one
chosen for the SHAP analysis, **beat the runner-up by exactly one pair** — one patient out of 33 ranked
one place differently. The paper's own DeLong agrees (ΔAUC 0.004, adjusted p = 0.926). **Standing check:
multiply positives by negatives, take the reciprocal, and ask whether the claimed difference is more than
a couple of grid steps.** Fires only on small samples — which is exactly where model-ranking claims live.
Pairs with #105 (CI width as a sample fingerprint): #105 tells you *which sample* a number came from,
#114 tells you *how finely that sample can measure*.

**115. Compute events-per-variable against the candidates screened, not the predictors that survived.**
The paper reports **EPV 11.25** (45 events / 4 predictors), states the ≥10 rule, and deserves credit for
computing it at all. But the outcome was consulted about **62 variables** — every one appears in Table 1
with a p-value in both cohorts, and LASSO then searched among them using the outcome again. The honest
denominator is the number of chances the procedure had to find a spurious pattern: **45/62 = 0.73 events
per candidate.** Supporting 62 candidates at EPV 10 needs ~620 events, roughly 1,290 recipients at this
AKI rate. Analogy for the summary: 14 heads in 20 flips is weak evidence from one coin and no evidence
at all if you flipped 62 coins and reported the best. **Standing check: find the sentence saying how many
candidates were screened; if there isn't one, count the rows of Table 1.** Corrects the application of
#85, which this list has until now computed the paper's way.

**116. When a paper gives several estimates of the same quantity, the spread between them IS the
measurement.** Plain logistic regression, no tuning, no randomness: apparent AUC **0.861** fitted and
scored on 94 patients, **0.729** fitted and scored on all 127. Same method, both in-sample, nothing
changed but which patients were in the pot — **a gap of 0.132.** That is this dataset's noise amplitude,
and it is larger than *every* between-model difference the paper reports in validation (0.004, 0.006,
0.026, 0.029, 0.031, 0.035, 0.050, 0.055, 0.081, 0.085). **Standing check: when a study runs a second
validation scheme that should not matter, read the disagreement as a free estimate of how much any one
number can be trusted.** Here 5-fold CV moved SVM by **0.142** and RF by 0.009 — which identifies the
SVM's validation figure, not the RF's, as the fluke. Extends #83.

**117. A significance test on apparent (in-sample) performance measures memorisation, not skill.**
The DeLong table's "Apparent whole dataset" rows report RF 0.941 vs XGBoost 0.881, ΔAUC 0.060, **BH-
adjusted p < 0.001** — and both AUCs are resubstitution estimates on data the models were fitted to.
RF's apparent 0.941 against its own 5-fold CV 0.807 is a gap of **0.134**, larger than the maximum
bootstrap optimism the paper itself estimated (0.025–0.059). **A "significant" difference between two
apparent AUCs tells you which model overfitted harder, and the more flexible model always wins it.**
Standing check: before reading any model-comparison p-value, find out which dataset the two AUCs were
computed on. Companion to #105.

**118. Repeated identical Benjamini–Hochberg values are the procedure's signature, not ten
measurements.** The validation column holds ten comparisons and **two distinct adjusted values**:
0.609 (×8) and 0.926 (×2). BH sorts the raw p-values, scales each by m/rank, then **enforces
monotonicity** — the adjusted values may never decrease walking down the list. Where nothing is small,
that enforcement flattens a whole run onto one ceiling, collapsing eight different raw values into one
printed number. **Correct reading: "nothing here is significant and the procedure stopped
distinguishing." Incorrect reading: "eight comparisons are equally uncertain."** Also worth recording:
BH controls the **false discovery rate** — the expected share of *declared* discoveries that are wrong —
so it does nothing at all when, as here, nothing is declared. And note where it was *not* used: Table 1's
62 variables × 2 cohorts ≈ 124 uncorrected p-values, of which ~3 would cross 0.05 by chance with nothing
going on. **These authors know about multiplicity — they applied BH where it weakened their own claim.**

**119. Check whether the predictor's measurement TIME falls inside the outcome's ascertainment
window.** Sharper than "the predictor and outcome share a variable" (#98, #110). The outcome is KDIGO
AKI: a creatinine rise ≥26.5 μmol/L **within 48 h** of transplant. The top predictor is eGFR, computed
from creatinine drawn at the first urgent post-op blood test, **generally within 1 h** — inside that
48-hour window. For any patient whose creatinine had already climbed 26.5 μmol/L by that first draw,
**the measurement used to predict the outcome is a measurement that can satisfy the outcome.** The
authors disclose "partial overlap" and frame it as a shared ingredient; the timing is the sharper
statement and they do not make it, nor report how many patients it applied to. **Standing check: write
down when the predictor was measured and when the outcome window opens. If the first is inside the
second, it is leakage, not correlation.**

**120. Credit an abstract that leads with its own weakest number.** The title is "Machine learning-based
nomogram"; the nomogram is the logistic model; its validation AUC of **0.761 is the lowest of the
five**, and the abstract reports it *before* mentioning the SVM's 0.846. Then the paper says three
separate times that the SVM was chosen only because its number was numerically highest and that the
choice should be treated cautiously. **Papers almost never volunteer the ordering that makes them look
worse.** Companion to #104 (credit the disclosure that costs the authors something).

**The grade, and why it is the point.** **2/5 for the evidence, and the best-reported 2/5 in this list.**
The grade answers "how much should a reader update?" — and 127 patients at one centre, tested only on
themselves, with a top predictor overlapping the outcome window, is weak however it is written up. But
the authors told you all of it: they published the DeLong test that killed their headline, ran a second
validation that reversed their ranking and printed it, flagged the predictor–outcome overlap before
anyone asked, computed their own EPV, named TRIPOD+AI, listed the variables they could not obtain
(donor characteristics, cold ischaemia time, BP trajectories, vasopressor dose, immunosuppression,
nephrotoxic exposure, injury biomarkers), explained *why* external validation was impossible for them,
and stated that **no organs from executed prisoners were used**. **Honesty is not strength, but it is
what lets you measure strength** — and the dangerous combination is the opposite one: a 10,000-patient
study that hides its calibration and reports only the metric it won on. Worth carrying forward as a
standing distinction when grading.

**Also noted.** Two of the four nomogram predictors are **not significant** in the multivariable model
— ln(D-dimer) p = 0.131, ALT p = 0.425, both CIs spanning 1 — yet both carry point scales a clinician
will move; ALT's mean |SHAP| is 0.014 against eGFR's 0.191, **fourteen times smaller**. Nothing
pre-operative differed between groups (not age, sex, BMI, diabetes, hypertension, Child-Pugh, MELD,
pre-op creatinine or pre-op eGFR) — the only intraoperative signal was **anhepatic phase time, 70 vs 59
min, p = 0.004**, which puts the risk factor in surgical conduct rather than patient selection.
**No calibration intercept, slope or Brier score is printed anywhere** (calibration is described in words
and drawn in Figure 2C/F/I), so the direction of miscalibration cannot be read. **No funding statement,
no competing-interests statement.** ICU stay was measured and correctly **excluded** from the predictor
set as a post-outcome consequence — worth crediting; that is exactly the variable that usually leaks in.
Exclusions removed the highest-risk group by design (15 of 19 exclusions were pre-existing renal
impairment), which the authors explain and which bounds generalisability.

## 2026-10-29 — Devisetti L et al., LLM calculating the PECARN IAI rule (JACEP Open; PMID 42893265) — graded 3/5

OpenAI o1 vs a two-clinician reference standard on **196 paediatric blunt-trauma ED notes** at two
UCSF-affiliated departments. 1,568 labels, **23 discordant (1.47%)**, binary accuracy 95.4%, weighted
Cohen's κ 0.96. **First TRIPOD-LLM paper in this list.** No held-out notes anywhere; reference-standard
reviewers unblinded; every confidence interval too narrow at the bottom.

**121. Rebuild the confusion matrix from the published percentages — the counts are recoverable and
the CI method falls out with them.** Given n = 196 and five percentages (accuracy 95.4, sensitivity
94.4, specificity 98.2, PPV 99.3, NPV 86.9), exactly one whole-number 2×2 fits: **TP 134, FN 8, FP 1,
TN 53.** Check: 134/142 = 94.37, 53/54 = 98.15, 134/135 = 99.26, 53/61 = 86.89, 187/196 = 95.41, F1 =
268/277 = 0.9675. **So the study is: clinicians called 142 children not-very-low-risk and 54 very-low-
risk; the model missed 8 of the 142 and over-called 1 of the 54** — far more informative than the
percentages, and it lets you compute whatever the paper omitted. Companion to #105 and #114: #105 reads
the sample off a CI, #114 reads the resolution off the sample, **#121 reads the raw counts off the
point estimates.**

**122. A confidence interval ending at exactly 100% (or 0%) is a broken calculation announcing
itself.** With the counts in hand, test the formula. Wald (p ± 1.96√(p(1−p)/n)) reproduces **all five**
reported intervals to two decimals — and two of them ran past the boundary and were clipped:
specificity 53/54 → Wald **94.55–101.74**, printed as 94.6–100; PPV 134/135 → Wald **97.81–100.71**,
printed as 97.8–100. The paper never names its CI method; the arithmetic names it. **Why it matters:
Wald is symmetric and misbehaves against a boundary.** Exact (Clopper–Pearson) intervals for the same
counts: specificity **90.11–99.95** (not 94.6), PPV **95.94–99.98** (not 97.8), accuracy 91.46–97.88,
sensitivity 89.20–97.54, NPV 75.78–94.16. **Every reported lower bound is too high — by 1.0, 1.4, 2.6,
3.4 and 4.4 points — and the error runs the same way every time.** Standing check: look at the upper
bounds first; a 100 is the tell, and when you see it, widen the other end too.

**123. Accuracy and kappa answer different questions, and the gap between them measures how lopsided
the data is.** Cohen's κ = (observed − chance)/(1 − chance): agreement beyond what two raters with the
same habits would reach by guessing. On the reconstructed binary table: observed 187/196 = 0.9541;
clinician marginals 0.7245/0.2755, model marginals 0.6888/0.3112, so chance = 0.7245×0.6888 +
0.2755×0.3112 = **0.5848**; κ = 0.3693/0.4152 = **0.889**. **95.4% accuracy is κ 0.89 because 72% of
these children fall in one category and ~59 of the 95 points were free.** Intuition for the summary:
two radiologists calling 95 of 100 scans normal at random agree on ~90.5 of them with no skill at all.
**Three watch-outs: (a) κ depends on the marginal distribution, so it is not comparable across studies
with a different case mix; (b) the kappa paradox — overwhelming dominance of one category can give high
agreement and low κ; (c) κ between two raters reading the same text measures consistency, not
correctness.** This paper's own best insight is that several disagreements were ambiguous *definitions*
rather than either party being wrong.

**124. "Weighted kappa" without naming the weights is not a reported statistic.** The headline is
weighted κ **0.96** on a three-category ordered scale (very low / moderate / high). Weighting is the
right choice for ordered categories — a one-step disagreement should cost less than a two-step. But
**linear weights penalise a two-step miss twice as much as a one-step; quadratic weights penalise it
four times as much**, and on a three-level scale quadratic weighting routinely prints a visibly higher
number. The scheme is not stated, so 0.96 is not reproducible. Standing demand: weighted κ must come
with its weighting scheme, and ideally the confusion matrix.

**125. In an LLM paper, model selection and prompt refinement on the evaluation set are the new
threshold-tuning.** Two instances here, both disclosed. **(a)** Three models run (GPT-4o-mini, GPT-4.1,
o1); o1 kept "because it demonstrated the highest accuracy at the predictor variable level" — on these
196 notes. **(b)** "LLM prompts were developed over 2 rounds"; two clinicians reviewed the discordant
extractions and "refine[d] prompts with clearer criteria" — on these 196 notes. **There is no held-out
set anywhere in the study**, so the reported 95.4% is post-selection and post-refinement, and the
optimism is never quantified. The paper's own limitation names only the smaller half ("may have overfit
to institution-specific documentation patterns"). Analogy: marking a practice exam, telling the student
exactly which answers were wrong, re-sitting the same paper, and reporting the second score as their
expected grade. **Standing check for every LLM paper: find the sentence describing how the prompt was
written, then ask which data that happened on.** Fix is cheap — develop on half the notes, freeze, report
on the other half.

**126. One inference run at temperature 1.0 with no seed is one draw from a distribution.** The paper
states "default sampling parameters (temperature = 1.0, no seed set)" and "one inference run was
performed per clinical note" — **credit for reporting this at all, which TRIPOD-LLM is the reason for**
— but it means the headline figures cannot be reproduced and their run-to-run variability is unknown.
Three runs and a reported spread would have cost two more hours of compute. Standing demand for LLM
evaluations: temperature, seed, model version string, and **number of inference runs with the spread
across them.**

**127. The sample size that matters is the number of units of the thing being claimed — and one study
can have several.** This paper is simultaneously **196 patients** (for anything about this cohort),
**1,568 labels** (for the extraction claim — 196 notes × 8 variables, which is what the headline
actually rests on), and **4 events** (for anything about intra-abdominal injury). The secondary result
"100% sensitivity for IAI and for IAI requiring acute intervention" is **4/4 and 1/1**: exact 95%
intervals **39.8–100%** and **2.5–100%**. **This is also why the grade is 3 rather than 2 despite no
external validation** — the claim is about label extraction and there are 1,568 labels, each of the 23
errors individually reviewed; contrast 2026-10-28, where the claim was a prediction model and there
were 61 events. **Match the denominator to the claim before judging whether a study is big enough.**

**128. When missing data is imputed in one direction, check which way the resulting clinical error
points.** Missing predictors were counted as **negative**. PECARN calls a child very low risk only when
*all* predictors are negative, so absence→negative pushes children **toward** very low risk, i.e.
**toward not scanning**. The data match the theory: the two worst errors in the study were children the
clinicians scored **high risk** and the model scored **very low risk**, and in both the model had missed
abdominal wall trauma — one of the two variables the authors name as lacking structured fields. Neither
child had an injury, which is luck in a cohort where only 2% did. **An imputation rule whose errors
concentrate in the unsafe direction is a design property, not a rounding detail.**

**Also noted — what earns the 3.** **TRIPOD-LLM and STROBE both named**, with exact model version
strings (`gpt-4o-mini-2024-07-18`, `gpt-4.1-2025-04-14`, `o1-2024-12-17`), reasoning-effort setting,
sampling parameters, inference count, prompting strategy, median input length (845 words) and prompts in
a supplement. **Funding disclosed** (NIH NICHD K23HD110716). **COI disclosed including the awkward one**
— two authors are co-founders and consultants to CaptureDx, named, with a non-involvement statement.
**IRB number given (25-43740) with the consent-waiver reasoning spelled out in full.** Two independent
reviewers with third-party adjudication and prespecified criteria. **A complete qualitative analysis of
all 23 discordances**, which is more useful than the accuracy figure. Conclusion scoped to QI and
research and repeated three times, with "larger external validation is needed before use to guide
bedside imaging decisions." **The authors call it a single-system study themselves**, correctly: the two
sites share an EHR instance, documentation templates and some physicians. And the paper's sharpest
contribution is reading its own errors the right way round — most discordances reflected **ambiguity in
the clinical definitions rather than model failure**, with the model refusing to call a mechanism
dangerous when fall height or crash speed was undocumented.

**Against that.** The Bottom Line box says "negative predictive value was 86.9% for identifying children
at very low risk of injury," which invites reading agreement-with-clinicians as accuracy-against-injury;
the Methods are unambiguous but the most-read element is not. **The F1 interval (0.97, CI 0.90–0.98) is
asymmetric 5.4 : 1 while all five others are exactly symmetric Wald** — a different, unstated method.
The per-variable table was not in the retrieved text (arithmetic is consistent with the worst variable at
185/196 = 94.4%, and 11 of 23 errors fell in one variable, named in the Discussion as dangerous mechanism
of injury). Throughput was 2 h for 196 notes (~37 s each) with **no clinician timing reported**, so the
efficiency claim is one-sided.

**Deferred-paper status recorded in the summary's appendix**, including a note to decode the JAMA
OpenEvidence study **abstract-only on 2026-11-05 if its PMC body is still empty at day 21**, and a
flag that the Annals *Choosing Wisely* overuse rate of 5.4% with a 95% CI of 0.6–12.2% is a **clustering
signature** — the interval is almost certainly computed across the 15 recommendations rather than the

## 2026-10-30 — Romano-Martinez A et al., DL echocardiography systematic review + meta-analysis (EHJ Digital Health; PMID 42899077) — graded 3/5

**The first meta-analysis in this list.** 16,248 records → 160 studies of *fully automated* DL on echo
video, five task categories, random-effects pooling with double-arcsine transformation, multilevel
models, meta-regression, Cook's distance, funnel plots. **The review half earns a 4; the meta-analysis
half earns a 2**, because the literature turned out not to be poolable and the paper's text never prints
a pooled number.

**129. I² is a RATIO, not an amount of heterogeneity — and that is why 98% is less alarming than it
sounds.** I² = between-study variation ÷ (between-study variation + sampling variation). It measures
disagreement *relative to each study's own precision*, so **making the studies bigger pushes I² up even
when the real disagreement is unchanged.** Worked intuition: ten studies reporting accuracies between
0.88 and 0.92 — a four-point spread — give I² near zero if each tested 50 patients and above 95% if each
tested 50,000. Identical disagreement, opposite verdicts. This is why I² > 90% is routine in diagnostic
meta-analysis and is not on its own grounds to dismiss a review. Values here: prediction **99.9%/98.8%**,
LVEF MAE **98.1%**, segmentation **98.2%**, view classification **75.8%**. **Standing check: don't read
I² as "how much the studies disagree" — look at the actual spread on the forest plot, and ask for the
prediction interval, which is in the original units and is the number a clinician can use.** Neither
appears in this paper's text.

**130. Residual heterogeneity RISING when you add moderators means the moderators are noise.**
Prediction tasks behaved properly: architecture alone **99.9%/98.8%** → adding target **98.4%/98.1%** →
adding training participants, resolution, frames and batch size **76.8%/82.8%** (a fall of 23.1 and 16.0
points, and even so three-quarters is still unexplained). Segmentation did the opposite: baseline
**98.2%** → by target 97.5%/94.9% → adding architecture, segmented area and training size **98.8%, i.e.
UP** — and the formal test of moderators returned **p = 0.99**. **Standing check: watch the direction.
Falling residual I² = the explanation is working; rising = you added noise, and that is informative
rather than an error.**

**131. "Outliers removed, heterogeneity resolved" reads in both directions.** Cook's distance measures
how much the pooled result moves if one study is deleted; >1 is the usual threshold. Here **5 of 21
segmentation studies exceeded it, and after removal I² fell from 98.2% to 0.00%.** Reading one: a
quarter of the studies generated all the apparent disagreement and the other sixteen are consistent.
Reading two: **deleting the 24% of observations that disagree most and announcing the rest agree is
close to assuming the conclusion.** The paper offers neither reading. Standing check: always note *what
fraction* was removed before believing a post-outlier homogeneity claim.

**132. You cannot inverse-variance pool raw proportions — the weights are rigged by the scale.**
Variance of a proportion is p(1−p)/n, which **shrinks toward the boundary**: p(1−p) is 0.2500 at p=0.50,
0.0900 at 0.90, **0.0099 at 0.99**. Since meta-analysis weights by inverse variance, **a study reporting
99% accuracy gets ~25× the weight of an equally sized study reporting 50%, purely for sitting near the
ceiling** — the optimistic studies would dominate automatically. The **double-arcsine (Freeman–Tukey)**
transform, which this paper uses correctly, maps t = arcsin√(x/(n+1)) + arcsin√((x+1)/(n+1)), whose
variance is ≈ **1/(n+1)** and contains no p at all. At n=100 the raw variances 0.002500 / 0.000900 /
0.000099 (for 50, 90, 99 correct) all become **0.009901**. **This is the same boundary problem that broke
the Wald intervals in #122, with a different fix.** Watch-out: back-transforming a Freeman–Tukey pooled
value is known to misbehave when study sizes vary widely — several methodologists prefer logit — and
this review's studies range from small single-centre series to large multicentre cohorts.

**133. In a meta-analysis of models, independence is about DATASETS, not studies.** The review names it
in limitations — "overlap in training datasets, derivative model development, and repeated use of the
same validation cohorts across studies may have reduced the independence of some performance estimates"
— and never quantifies it. In echocardiographic DL a handful of public datasets are reused across huge
numbers of papers, so **21 segmentation studies "externally validated" on substantially the same public
data are not 21 independent tests.** The review extracted dataset information from all 160 papers and
could have counted this; a single sentence saying how many of the 86 external validations used the same
repositories would change how every pooled number should be read. **Standing check on any model
meta-analysis: count the distinct test sets, not the studies.** Companion to #103.

**134. Read the screening funnel — the attrition tells you how well-posed the question was.**
16,248 identified → 7,903 duplicates → 8,345 → 7,710 excluded on title/abstract → **635 full texts** →
**160 included**. Every subtraction reconciles. Two numbers worth noting: **475 of 635 (74.8%) were
excluded after being read in full**, meaning the abstract screen was deliberately permissive; and the
overall yield was **160/16,248 = 0.98%**. A high full-text rejection rate is a sign of a tight,
operationalised inclusion rule — here, "fully automated," defined by listing the manual steps that
disqualify a study, which is the strongest single design decision in the paper.

**135. A meta-analysis whose running text contains no pooled estimate has not delivered its
deliverable.** Checked exhaustively: every occurrence of "pooled" and every CI mention in the prose is
either a methods sentence or a **figure caption describing a forest plot**. The five results sections
lead with I² values and moderator tests and give no accuracy, AUC or Dice coefficient. The abstract's
conclusion is "DL models achieve high performance across various applications" — **no number**. The
stated purpose was "to provide pooled performance estimates." **Standing check: before citing a
meta-analysis, find the pooled estimate and its interval in the text. If they exist only inside a
figure, you cannot quote the paper, only describe it.**

**136. The unit of analysis sets the weights, and here it varied across studies.** Sample size was
reported as individuals in 134 entries (83.2%), echo videos in 88 (54.7%), echo studies in 34 (21.1%) —
overlapping. Variance, hence weight, was derived from the point estimate plus the testing sample size,
so **the choice of unit directly determines how much each study counts.** The authors handled it
carefully — excluding studies without a count of individuals, marking ambiguous ones with an asterisk
where "it could be reasonably interpreted from the text that a one-to-one correspondence with
participants was intended" — but it is a disclosed judgement call sitting underneath every pooled
number. Extends #127.

**137. Divergent time trends are findings; internal falling while external rises is the signature of a
field learning not to overfit.** Four trends, reported in single clauses and never discussed:
prognostic generalisability **improving**; LVEF estimation improving **both** internally and externally;
view classification showing "enhanced generalization performance, **despite a gradual decline in
performance within derivation cohorts**" — the encouraging one, and the clearest sign of reduced
overfitting anywhere in this list; and **segmentation external performance DECREASING over seven
years**, which is remarkable and unexplained. Benign readings (later studies tested on harder or more
varied data, stricter reporting) compete with a less benign one (the earliest external results were
optimistic), and a review is uniquely placed to separate them. **Standing check: when a review reports
time trends, read internal and external separately — they can move in opposite directions and the gap
between them is the overfitting story.**

**Kappa, second instance — and the contrast is the lesson (extends #123/#124).** Screening audit: **103
abstracts, 92.2% raw agreement, Cohen's κ = 0.78.** Back out the chance agreement: p_e = (p_o − κ)/(1 −
κ) = (0.922 − 0.78)/0.22 = **0.645**, so **64.5% of the agreement was free** and the implied include rate
is ~23%. Line it up against yesterday: **PECARN 95.4% raw / p_e 0.585 / κ 0.889** versus **screening
92.2% raw / p_e 0.645 / κ 0.780** — 3.2 points less raw agreement but **10.9 points less kappa**, purely
because screening's categories are more lopsided. **κ is never comparable across tasks, and "κ > 0.8 is
excellent" is folklore with no theoretical standing.** When a paper reports raw agreement and κ, you can
always recover p_e and see how much free agreement the task handed out.

**Also noted — what earns the 3.** **PRISMA flow** (named in the Figure 1 caption) with arithmetic that
reconciles exactly. Three databases, 2014–June 2026. **Dual independent screening with agreement
measured**, which most reviews only assert. Eligibility criteria built jointly by epidemiologists,
cardiologists and DL specialists. **A data-extraction rule that prefers external results** over held-out
ones, stated. **Multilevel models for dependent effect sizes.** **Funnel plots reported with the correct
refusal to over-read them** — "difficult to interpret in diagnostic and prediction-model studies with
heterogeneous metrics, tasks, and validation designs… not interpreted as definitive evidence for or
against publication bias," which this list has not seen before. And an honest regulatory framing: three
approved commercial systems named (Caption Guidance, EchoGo Core, Us2.ai), with the observation that
their exclusion probably makes this review **understate** achievable performance — papers rarely
volunteer that their estimate may be too low.

**Against that.** No pooled estimate in abstract or text (#135). Dataset overlap named, not counted
(#133). **No PROSPERO or other prospective registration** anywhere in the retrieved text. **No funding
statement and no competing-interests statement** in the retrieved text, in a paper where one author's
affiliation line names a pharmaceutical company. Within "prediction of disease or mortality" the pooled
category mixes valvular disease (21), cardiomyopathies (21), heart failure (10) and mortality (2) —
targets that are not the same prediction problem, and entering target as a moderator moved I² only from
99.9% to 98.4%, so the mitigation did not work. "External validation" treats same-vendor, same-country,
same-public-repository testing as equivalent to a different health system.

**Descriptive findings worth carrying regardless of the pooling.** **External 86 (53.4%) vs internal
only 75 (46.6%)** of 161 task entries — nearly half this literature has never met outside data, under a
strict definition that excludes retrained or fine-tuned models. Task split: prediction 59, replication
59, segmentation 59, view classification 22, quality assessment 10. Targets: valvular disease commonest
in prediction (21/59); systolic function dominates replication (42/59); cardiac chambers dominate
segmentation (53/59); only **2 studies predicted mortality**. **Only 16 of 160 articles used transformer
or foundation-model architectures** — "a marked methodological gap between echocardiography research and
the broader DL literature." Participant characteristics reported in only **40 of 59** prediction studies.
Risk of bias improved from 2024 onward. Stenosis diagnosis was relatively hard, which the authors
correctly attribute to the models lacking Doppler.

## 2026-10-31 — Ran J et al., SARS-CoV-2 reinfection, mucosal + cellular immunity (Emerg Microbes Infect; PMID 42908321) — graded 3/5

Two **prospective** community cohorts, Wuxi and Nantong, 1,186 enrolled → 1,156 analysed, **128
reinfections (11.1%)** over a median 10 months. 18 candidates → Boruta → **2**: nasal spike-specific
sIgA (median **4.99** reinfected vs **66.16** not, p<0.001) and spike-specific CD4⁺ Tcm (0.01% vs 0.05%).
**The best internal methodology in this list, attached to the least interpretable headline number in it.**

**138. An external result that BEATS the internal result is a red flag, not a triumph — and one that
beats APPARENT performance is impossible.** The mirror of every paper decoded here. Line them up:
apparent training AUC **0.914**; nested-CV corrected **0.834 ± 0.084**; internal validation (n=170, ~20
events) **0.835**; **external validation (n=586, 62 events) 0.996.** External minus internal **+0.161**;
external minus apparent **+0.082** — the frozen model did better on 586 strangers than on the 400 people
it was fitted to, and sits **1.93 between-fold SDs** above its own cross-validated mean. Hanley–McNeil
intervals: internal ≈ **0.72–0.95**, external ≈ **0.985–1.000** — they barely touch, so these are not two
noisy estimates of one quantity. **A frozen model cannot improve; when it appears to, the task changed.**
**Standing check: treat an unexpectedly high external result with exactly the scepticism you would give
an unexpectedly low one.** Readers are trained to doubt a drop and relax at a rise; the rise above
in-sample performance is the more impossible of the two.

**139. AUC is a property of the model AND the case mix — so two cohorts' AUCs can answer different
questions.** An AUC counts (case, non-case) pairs ranked correctly. Hand the same model a population
where cases and non-cases sit further apart and more pairs become easy, so the number rises with nothing
improving. Worked contrast: cases at 20 / non-cases at 50 with heavy overlap → AUC ~0.83; cases at 3 /
non-cases at 80 with almost none → AUC ~0.99. **Same model, same biology, different question.** This
paper contains both halves of the diagnosis and never joins them: (a) Table S3 reports nasal sIgA, serum
IgG, memory B cells, cTfh and two CD4⁺ subsets **all differ significantly across the three cohorts**
(p<0.05); (b) Figure S2's description says "the Wuxi cohort showed moderate overlap between groups… the
Nantong cohort demonstrated **greater separation**." **Standing check: before comparing two cohorts'
AUCs, compare their predictor distributions — and ask for per-cohort calibration, which is far less
forgiving of case-mix shift than discrimination.** Exact mirror of #101 (entry 58: AUC travelled while
calibration collapsed); same tool resolves both.

**140. A per-unit odds ratio is uninterpretable without the variable's spread — compound it.** Headline
finding: nasal sIgA **OR 0.995 per unit (0.993–0.997), p<0.001**. Reads as nothing; the p-value says it
is among the most certain results in the study. Both true. Compound across plausible spans: 10 units →
0.951 (trivial); **61.17 units** (the gap between group medians, 66.16 − 4.99) → **0.736**, i.e. 36%
higher odds at the low end; 100 units → 0.606; **500 units → 0.082, a twelve-fold effect.** Same
coefficient, four different stories — and **the range, IQR and SD of sIgA are never reported**, so the
clinical magnitude of the paper's strongest finding cannot be read from the paper. Analogy: "0.01% risk
reduction per metre walked" is useless until you learn people walk thousands of metres. **Standing
demand: odds ratios per SD or per IQR.** The companion failure is in the same table — CD4⁺ Tcm **OR 0.427
per percentage point** on a variable whose medians are 0.01% and 0.05%, i.e. an increment nobody in the
study moves by. **As printed, this paper's two ORs (0.995 and 0.427) cannot be compared with each
other.** Watch-out: an OR near 1 with a tiny p-value is not a negligible effect; an OR far from 1 may
describe a step nobody takes.

**141. Nested cross-validation with feature selection INSIDE the loop is the gold standard — and the
corrected number is the one to cite.** Verbatim: "a nested 5-fold cross-validation framework was
implemented for model tuning and evaluation, in which Boruta feature selection and hyperparameter tuning
were **strictly restricted to the training folds of each loop**." Result: apparent **0.914** → corrected
**0.834 ± 0.084**, an optimism of 0.08 that the authors measured and published. **This is the direct
counterexample to #125** (entry 61, where model choice and prompt refinement both consumed the
evaluation set). Standing check: find out whether selection happened inside or outside the resampling
loop — outside means every reported number is post-selection.

**142. Boruta stability analysis beats a p-value for telling you whether a variable would survive a
different sample.** Boruta builds shuffled "shadow" copies of each variable and keeps only real ones
that consistently beat their own shadows. Here it was re-run on **100 random 80% subsamples**, with the
per-variable frequencies published: nasal sIgA and CD4⁺ Tcm **Confirmed in 100/100**; BMI Confirmed 10%,
Tentative 47%; vaccination rejected 87%; sex 97%; drinking 99%; **smoking, diabetes and prior infection
history 100% rejected.** "Rejected in 100 of 100 subsamples" is a far stronger statement than p > 0.05,
and "confirmed in 100 of 100" largely answers the selection-instability worry that #115 exists for.
**Standing demand for any data-driven selection: how often would this variable be picked again?**

**143. A SHAP non-linearity from a boosted tree on ~46 events is a model artefact until something else
confirms it.** The paper reports a **U-shape** for CD4⁺ Tcm — moderate protective, very low *and*
relatively high both harmful — and builds **three paragraphs** of immunology on it (naive T-cell pool
contraction, TCR repertoire diversity, repeated antigenic stimulation, memory quality vs quantity). But
the shape appears nowhere else: the univariate medians are **monotone** (0.01% vs 0.05%) and the adjusted
OR is **monotone and non-significant** (0.427, 0.167–1.093, p=0.076). Boosted trees emit step functions;
with ~46 events and 2 inputs, a step in the sparse upper tail can be a handful of people. **Standing
check: when only the ML model sees a shape, and it lives in the thinnest region of the data, it is the
model's shape, not the world's.** Extends #96.

**144. Check whether the outcome's ascertainment mechanism is correlated with the top predictor.**
Reinfection counted only when symptoms prompted a self-test. Nasal sIgA's biological job is to blunt
infection at the point of entry — so a high-sIgA person who *is* infected plausibly has a milder course
and never tests. **High sIgA therefore reduces the chance of being RECORDED as reinfected, separately
from reducing the chance of being infected**, which inflates the apparent protective effect exactly
among the people the model scores as low-risk. The paper names the ascertainment bias and says the model
applies only to symptomatic reinfection; it never notes that the under-detection is correlated with its
own strongest predictor. **Standing check: ask whether the thing that triggers outcome detection is
plausibly caused by the predictor.** Distinct from #119 (measurement-window overlap): that is timing,
this is detection probability.

**145. A calculator is only deployable if its inputs are obtainable.** The published web application
takes two inputs: a nasal spike-specific sIgA titre from an **in-house ELISA**, and a spike-specific
CD4⁺ Tcm percentage from **flow cytometry with activation-induced marker assays on freshly isolated
PBMCs**. Neither is orderable in routine care anywhere. Contrast entry 59 (paediatric UTI), whose
calculator used seven routine labs and whose deployment problem was calibration, not availability.
**Standing check on any deployed tool: can a clinic actually measure every input?** Also note the
measurement floor — CD4⁺ Tcm medians of **0.01%** mean rare-event flow cytometry: at 500,000 CD4⁺ cells
acquired that is ~50 events (CV ~14%), at 50,000 it is ~5 events (**CV ~45%**). Cells acquired are in
Supplementary Methods, unretrievable, so whether 0.01% vs 0.05% is signal or counting noise cannot be
checked — and a two-variable model resting on it should say so in the main text.

**146. Reconstructing the confusion matrix, third time in four days — and the event count is the
payoff.** From n=586, accuracy 0.974, sensitivity 1.000, specificity 0.971, PPV 0.805, NPV 1.000, F1
0.892, exactly one table fits: **TP 62 · FN 0 · FP 15 · TN 509.** All six reproduce (571/586=0.9744;
62/62=1.000; 509/524=0.9714; 62/77=0.8052; 509/509=1.000; 124/139=0.8921). Three things the paper does
not print fall out: **the external cohort held 62 events**, so "sensitivity 1.000" has exact CI **94.2%–
100%** — a genuine result, unlike entry 61's 1/1 (CI 2.5–100%); the errors are **15 false positives and
zero false negatives**, PPV 62/77 with exact CI 69.9–88.7%; and by subtraction **Wuxi held 66 events of
570**, so the training cohort had ~46 and the internal validation cohort ~**20**, which is why the
internal AUC's interval is ~0.22 wide. **EPV: 46/2 = 23 by the usual accounting, 46/18 = 2.56 against
candidates screened (#115)** — mitigated, genuinely, by #142.

**Also noted — what earns the 3.** **Prospective design with written informed consent.** **Genuine
external validation at a separate city**, 586 participants, not a held-out slice. **Loss to follow-up
2.5% (30/1186), enumerated by reason** (14 uncontactable, 7 relocated, 4 died unrelated, 5 withdrew)
**and the lost compared against the retained** with no differences found — almost nobody runs that
comparison. **A multivariable confounding check** on both selected markers adjusted for eight
covariates, with the non-significant result (p=0.076) reported rather than buried. **Nasal sampling
standardised at the step that matters** — the sponge's residual volume fixed at a 4 mL mark — which is
what makes concentrations comparable between people. **Final model chosen on generalisation, not
apparent performance**: the text notes KNN and RF had higher training AUCs and XGB was preferred for
consistency across validation. **Seven real limitations named**, including that both cohorts came from
one province in one epidemic period, that assays used JN.1 spike only, and that baseline-only markers
miss trajectories.

**Against that.** The 0.996 is reported as the model's best result and partly justifies the model choice,
with no interrogation (#138/#139). **No funding statement, no competing-interests statement, no
reporting guideline** in the retrieved text (TRIPOD+AI fits and most items are met). The calculator's URL
was stripped by the extraction and **was not guessed at**. Supplementary tables S1–S7 and figures S1–S3
unretrievable, which matters more than usual here: S3 (per-cohort baselines), S4 (full Boruta stability),
S6 (six-model metrics), S7 (nested CV per model) and S2 (joint feature-space plots) would settle most of
the above. **Note for the record: the abstract reports the internal 0.835 and describes the external
result in words only** — a reader of the abstract alone gets the more defensible figure.
